## 1. Period in One Paragraph

The Thirty Years' War began as a revolt of Bohemian Protestant nobles against their Habsburg king (the Defenestration of Prague, 1618) and grew into a series of overlapping wars about religion, the constitution of the Empire, and European power — drawing in Denmark, Sweden, France and Spain. Most of the fighting happened on German soil. Armies lived off the land through "contributions," plunder and requisitions, spreading hunger and epidemic disease. Mortality was catastrophic and uneven: some regions (Pomerania, Mecklenburg, Württemberg, the Palatinate) lost half or more of their populations, while others were barely touched. The **Peace of Westphalia (1648)** did not destroy the Empire or create modern sovereign states; it rebuilt the Empire's constitution with religious parity, confirmed the territorial rulers' rights within imperial law, and gave France and Sweden a guarantor role. The war left a lasting memory of trauma that later nationalists used to explain German "backwardness" — an interpretation now much disputed.

## 2. The Political Structure

### What was the war about? (overlapping conflicts)

1. **Religious-constitutional:** Could Protestants keep secularised church lands? Were Calvinists legal? Who interpreted the 1555 peace?
2. **Imperial power:** Would the Habsburg emperor Ferdinand II use victory (1620–1629) to strengthen imperial authority? The **Edict of Restitution (1629)** ordered the return of church lands secularised since 1552 — alarming even Catholic princes.
3. **European power:** Habsburg (Spain + Austria) vs France; Sweden's Baltic ambitions; the Dutch Revolt.

### Institutions under stress

- **Military entrepreneurs** (Albrecht von Wallenstein, Ernst von Mansfeld) raised armies as private businesses, financed by "contributions" extracted from occupied territories — an extreme form of privatised violence.
- **The Imperial Diet** did not meet between 1613 and 1640; politics moved to electoral conventions and battlefields.

### What the Peace of Westphalia (1648) actually did

- **Religious parity:** Lutherans, Catholics *and Calvinists* recognised; a **"normal year" (1624)** fixed confessional ownership of church property and public worship; minorities retained private worship if they had it in 1624; religious matters in the Diet decided by agreement between confessional blocs (*itio in partes*), not majority.
- **Landeshoheit** (territorial authority) of the estates confirmed — including the right to form alliances, **but not against the emperor or the Empire** (Osiander).
- **Territorial changes:** France gains Habsburg rights in Alsace; Sweden gains Western Pomerania, Bremen-Verden; Swiss and Dutch independence from the Empire recognised; Brandenburg gains Eastern Pomerania, Magdeburg, Halberstadt, Minden.
- **Imperial courts strengthened** (Reichskammergericht, Reichshofrat): subjects, including peasants, could sue their rulers.
- **France and Sweden as guarantors.**

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** The treaties did *not* say anything about "sovereignty"; Andreas Osiander (2001) calls the textbook idea of a "Westphalian system of sovereign states" a myth, originating in French-Swedish wartime propaganda and 19th-century nationalist historiography. In practice, larger territories (Brandenburg, Bavaria, Saxony, Austria) did act increasingly independently, but within an imperial legal framework that still constrained smaller ones.

## 3. Society

The war hit the rural majority hardest. Social hierarchies survived but were shaken: some nobles were ruined, others (military entrepreneurs, officers) rose; towns lost wealth to taxes and debts. Witch persecutions peaked in some territories during the war years (e.g., the prince-bishoprics of Würzburg and Bamberg in the 1620s–30s) — an indicator of social stress and of how local authority could run amok.

## 4. Everyday Life

The war was experienced as a sequence of local disasters: soldiers quartered in houses, livestock seized, harvests burned, flight into walled towns or forests, plague and typhus following armies. Diaries (e.g., the Swabian cobbler Hans Heberle's *Zeytregister*) record flight into Ulm some thirty times. Grimmelshausen's novel *Simplicissimus* (1668) became the literary image of the war. Violence against civilians, especially women, was pervasive.

## 5. Economy

- **Mortality:** Estimates range from **~20%** (Peter H. Wilson's "most conservative" estimate, 2009) to **30–40%** (Günther Franz 1940/1961; Christian Pfister 2020) for the Empire as a whole; regional losses ranged from minimal (NW Germany, parts of Austria) to over 50% (Pomerania, Mecklenburg, Württemberg). Imperial cities lost on average ~31% of their population, territorial towns ~25% (Gierok 2023). **Most deaths came from disease and hunger, not battle.**
- **Recovery:** Population recovered by c. 1700–1720 in many regions; urban GDP per capita did not regain pre-war levels "until well into the eighteenth century" (Gierok). Wartime taxation and debt burdens were as damaging as demographic loss.
- **Agrarian consequences:** In the east, lords used labour scarcity to tighten control over peasants (a factor in *Gutsherrschaft*); in the west, peasants often obtained better terms.

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

- **Confessional polemics** hardened; yet the war also produced weariness with religious war and an intellectual turn toward natural law, reason of state and toleration arguments (Pufendorf later described the Empire as "irregular and similar to a monster" — a *legal* critique of its classification, not a call to abolish it).
- **Memory:** Later German nationalists presented 1618–48 as the moment foreign powers (France, Sweden) dismembered Germany — blaming the war for German "delay." Recent scholarship treats this as a 19th-century construction.

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

Unresolved clauses of 1555; Calvinism's spread; Habsburg ambitions; the Bohemian estates' resistance to Catholic centralisation; European rivalry; the failure of imperial institutions (the Diet deadlocked since 1608).

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE:** Bi-confessional Empire with unresolved disputes and blocked institutions.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Bohemian revolt; Habsburg victory and overreach (1629); foreign intervention.
↓
**TRANSITION (crisis):** Three decades of war; population collapse; privatised armies.
↓
**AFTER:** Westphalian settlement — a rebuilt Empire of parity and law; strengthened territorial states; standing armies become the norm for larger princes; France and Sweden as guarantors (see `03_territorial_states.md`).

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

Huge loss of life and property; in some regions resettlement by migrants (Swiss, Austrian Protestants); in the east, more severe peasant subordination; everywhere, heavier and more permanent taxation to pay for standing armies. Confessional minorities gained some legal protection (1624 rule).

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

The Empire and its institutions; the estates order; the confessional map (largely as of 1624); Habsburg imperial title.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| Westphalia created the modern system of sovereign states | A myth (Osiander); it re-constituted the Empire under law. |
| Westphalia made the Empire meaningless | The Empire functioned for another 158 years, with courts people used. |
| "One third of Germans died" everywhere | Average estimates range 20–40%; regional variation was enormous. |
| It was a purely religious war | Religion mattered, but constitutional and power-political motives were intertwined (Catholic France allied with Protestant Sweden). |
| The war explains Germany's "late" nation-state | A teleological 19th-c. argument; many parts of Germany recovered within two generations. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **Death toll:** Franz's high estimates (produced in 1940 with Nazi-era "Volk" framing, revised 1961) vs Wilson's lower "conservative" figure; the debate concerns method (extrapolation from regional data).

  *In plain words:* How many people died in the Thirty Years' War? Günther Franz gave high estimates — first produced in 1940 with Nazi-era ideas about the 'Volk' (the ethnic people), revised in 1961. Peter Wilson gives a lower, 'conservative' figure. The quarrel is about method: how far local records can be scaled up to the whole Empire.
- **Religious vs political war:** Wilson (*Europe's Tragedy*, 2009) emphasises politics and constitution over religion; others (e.g., Johannes Burkhardt's "state-building war" thesis) stress the struggle over the form of statehood.

  *In plain words:* What was the war really about? Peter Wilson argues politics and the Empire's constitution (its rules of who holds power) mattered more than religion. Others, like Johannes Burkhardt, see it as a 'state-building war' — a struggle over what form states should take.
- **Westphalia and sovereignty:** IR textbooks vs historians (Osiander, Derek Croxton, Heinz Duchhardt).

  *In plain words:* Did the 1648 peace invent the modern world of fully independent 'sovereign' states? Many international-relations textbooks say so. Historians such as Osiander, Croxton and Duchhardt disagree: the treaties never mention sovereignty and actually rebuilt the Empire under shared law.

## 13. Confidence Assessment

| Finding | Confidence |
|---|---|
| Most deaths from disease and hunger | HIGH |
| Overall mortality 20–40%, highly regional | MEDIUM (range reflects real uncertainty) |
| Westphalia did not create "sovereignty" | HIGH (among historians) |
| Long-term economic impact (to mid-18th c.) | MEDIUM |
| War caused Germany's "delayed" nationhood | CONTESTED (largely rejected) |

### If you remember only 5 things

1. The war was several wars at once — religious, constitutional and European.
2. Most of the dying came from hunger and disease spread by armies living off the land.
3. The damage was intensely regional: some areas lost half their people, others little.
4. Westphalia rebuilt the Empire as a legal order of religious parity — it did not invent modern sovereignty.
5. The idea that the war "delayed" Germany is a later nationalist story, not a settled finding.
