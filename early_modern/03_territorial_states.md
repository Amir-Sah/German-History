## 1. Period in One Paragraph

After 1648 the Holy Roman Empire consisted of roughly 300 territories of wildly different size — from Austria and Brandenburg-Prussia (European great powers) to tiny imperial knightships of a few villages — plus about 50 imperial cities and dozens of church states. Real governing happened in the territories: princes built bureaucracies, standing armies, courts, tax systems and residences (Versailles-inspired palaces in Würzburg, Mannheim, Ludwigsburg). Historians call this "absolutism," but in most territories rulers still had to negotiate with estates, courts and the Empire's legal order. The Empire itself became a permanent congress (the **Perpetual Diet** at Regensburg, 1663–1806) with two supreme courts, ten regional circles, and a collective defence system. It protected the small against the large — until the large (Austria, Prussia) outgrew it and Napoleon destroyed it.

## 2. The Political Structure

### Two levels of government

**Imperial level (the Reich)** — per Axel Gotthard (bpb):
- **Emperor** (Habsburg almost continuously): bound by an **electoral capitulation** (*Wahlkapitulation*) negotiated before each election, specifying what required Diet consent.
- **Imperial Diet (*Reichstag*)**: three colleges — electors, princes, imperial cities; the Archbishop-Elector of Mainz chaired; from 1663 permanent in Regensburg as a congress of envoys. Resolutions ratified by the emperor became *Reichsschlüsse*.
- **Two supreme courts:** the **Reichskammergericht** (Speyer, from 1693 Wetzlar), co-staffed by the estates; the **Reichshofrat** (Vienna), closer to the emperor. Both heard cases between rulers and subjects.
- **Imperial Circles (*Reichskreise*):** ten regional units for peace-keeping, defence contingents and coinage — most effective in fragmented regions (Swabia, Franconia).
- Gotthard's summary: a "mixed constitution" whose longevity came from "loose coupling" and deliberately "elastic formula compromises" (*dehnbare Formelkompromisse*).

**Territorial level:**
- The **prince** with a **privy council** (*Geheimer Rat*), specialised boards (chamber for finances, war commissariat), local officials (*Amtmänner*), and church consistories.
- **Territorial estates (*Landstände*)** — nobles, towns, sometimes clergy or even peasants (Württemberg, Tyrol, East Frisia) — granted taxes and defended privileges. Their strength varied: weakened in Brandenburg-Prussia and Bavaria; strong in Württemberg (where estates successfully sued their duke at imperial courts in the 18th century) and Mecklenburg.

### Regime Matrix — A mid-sized 18th-century principality (e.g., Württemberg, Bavaria)

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Formally ruled | Hereditary prince (duke/elector), under imperial law. |
| 2 | Actually held power | Prince + ministers/court + bureaucracy; negotiated with estates; constrained by imperial courts and powerful neighbours. |
| 3 | Power acquired | Inheritance; dynastic marriage; imperial enfeoffment. |
| 4 | Power maintained | Bureaucracy, army, court culture, church, patronage. |
| 5 | Who could challenge | Estates (tax refusal, lawsuits), imperial courts, the emperor, neighbouring princes. |
| 6 | Money | Domains, regalia (mines, forests, salt, tolls), estates-granted taxes, excise, foreign subsidies (hiring out troops — e.g., Hesse-Kassel to Britain in the American war). |
| 7 | Enforcement | Officials, police ordinances (*Polizeiordnungen* regulating morals, trade, dress), courts, soldiers. |
| 8 | Representation | Via corporate estates only; the rural majority rarely represented. |
| 9 | Rights | Corporate privileges; access to imperial courts; religious rights under 1648. |
| 10 | Opponents | Imprisonment (e.g., Württemberg poet Schubart, 1777–87, without trial); exile. |
| 11 | Connecting institutions | Parish, school, local officials, army recruitment, guilds. |
| 12 | Ordinary person's view | More regulation, more taxes, more paperwork; also courts one could petition. |

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** ("Absolutism") Princes claimed absolute authority (*legibus solutus*), but in practice they relied on local nobles, towns, church and imperial courts — Hamish Scott, Peter Wilson and others call "absolutism" a misleading label; *negotiated* or *consensual* rule better fits many German territories.

## 3. Society

A society of **estates**: nobility (privileged in office, army, taxation), clergy, burghers (town citizens), peasants. New groups grew: officials (*Beamte*), university-educated professionals, court artists. A "court society" (*höfische Gesellschaft*) set cultural norms; French was the language of courts. Jews: small communities under princely "protection letters" (*Schutzbriefe*); "court Jews" (*Hofjuden*) financed princes (e.g., Joseph Süß Oppenheimer, executed 1738 in Württemberg after his prince's death — a case later exploited by Nazi propaganda).

## 4. Everyday Life

Rural majority under manorial lordship. Increasing state presence: tax collectors, recruiters, schools (compulsory schooling decreed in several territories — Weimar 1619, Prussia 1717/1763 — but implementation lagged), police ordinances, censuses. Religious life organised by territorial churches. Migration: emigration to Hungary, Russia (Volga Germans from 1760s), and North America; immigration of Huguenots into Brandenburg (Edict of Potsdam, 1685).

## 5. Economy

**Cameralism** — the German version of mercantilism — trained officials to maximise the prince's revenue by promoting population, manufactures and agriculture (chairs of cameral sciences at Halle and Frankfurt/Oder, 1727). Guild regulation remained strong. Two agrarian systems diverged:
- **West/South: *Grundherrschaft*** — peasants held land with hereditary rights, paying rents; lords were rentiers.
- **East of the Elbe: *Gutsherrschaft*** — noble estates farmed directly with peasant labour services; hereditary subjection (*Erbuntertänigkeit*); lords held local police and court powers. See `04_prussia_and_austria.md`.

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

- **Reason of state; natural law** (Pufendorf, Thomasius, Wolff).
- **Pietism** (Spener, Francke at Halle): practical piety, education, discipline — influential in Prussian officialdom.
- **"German liberty" (*teutsche Libertät*):** the estates' constitutional liberties against imperial or princely despotism.
- **Imperial patriotism** (*Reichspatriotismus*): some loyalty to the Empire as a legal-protective order, especially among smaller estates, imperial cities and jurists.

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

- The growth of Austria and Prussia into European great powers whose interests transcended the Empire.
- Fiscal-military competition: standing armies required permanent taxes.
- Enlightenment critique of privilege and inefficiency.
- The French Revolution and its wars (from 1792).

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE:** Empire as protective legal umbrella over ~300 territories.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Austro-Prussian dualism; fiscal-military states; Enlightenment reform ideas.
↓
**TRANSITION (crisis):** French revolutionary wars (from 1792) — left bank of the Rhine lost.
↓
**AFTER:** Napoleonic reorganisation (1803–06): church states and imperial cities abolished, middle states enlarged, Empire dissolved (see `nineteenth_century/01_napoleon.md`).

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

More state: more regular taxes, conscription/recruitment, schools, ordinances, officials. Also more access to courts and petitions. Life-chances depended enormously on *which* territory one lived in.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

Estate society; religious territorial churches; peasant dependence; fragmentation.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| The post-1648 Empire was dead | It functioned as a legal and defence order until 1806; its courts handled tens of thousands of cases. |
| German princes were absolute monarchs | Most negotiated with estates and were constrained by imperial law. |
| Small states were backward | Many were centres of culture, education and reform (Weimar, Gotha, Baden). |
| Fragmentation meant weakness in all respects | It also produced cultural pluralism, many universities and courts, and legal checks on rulers. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **Was the Empire a "state"?** Georg Schmidt (*Geschichte des Alten Reiches*, 1999) argued for a "complementary Empire-State" (*komplementärer Reichs-Staat*) and a German nation in the early modern period. Heinz Schilling and Wolfgang Reinhard rejected projecting state and nation concepts backwards ("partially modernised imperial system"). Barbara Stollberg-Rilinger (*Des Kaisers alte Kleider*, 2008) stresses ritual and symbolic communication — the Empire "existed in the immediate relationship between emperor and estates." **CONTESTED.**
- **"Absolutism":** increasingly regarded as a problematic label (Nicholas Henshall, *The Myth of Absolutism*, 1992).

### If you remember only 5 things

1. After 1648, real government happened in ~300 territories, not in the Empire as a whole.
2. The Empire still mattered as a legal umbrella: subjects could sue their princes in imperial courts.
3. "Absolutism" is misleading — most princes negotiated with estates and law.
4. East and west of the Elbe developed very different agrarian societies.
5. Austria and Prussia grew too big for the Empire, setting up its destruction.
