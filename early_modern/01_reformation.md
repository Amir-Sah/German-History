## 1. Period in One Paragraph

In 1517 Martin Luther, a monk and professor in Wittenberg (Electoral Saxony), attacked the sale of indulgences. Thanks to printing, his writings became Europe's first mass-media event, and a movement for religious renewal spread rapidly through towns, universities and parts of the nobility. Different people heard different messages in it: princes saw a chance to control church property and appointments; townspeople saw freedom from clerical privilege; peasants saw divine justice against lords — which led to the **Peasants' War of 1524–26**, crushed with tens of thousands killed. Luther sided with the princes. The political result was that **secular rulers took charge of the churches in their lands**. The **Peace of Augsburg (1555)** let each ruler decide whether his territory would be Lutheran or Catholic. Religion became a matter of state, and the Empire became permanently bi-confessional (later tri-confessional, with Calvinism). This set up the confessional tensions that exploded in 1618.

## 2. The Political Structure

### What changed in the structure of power

| Before (c. 1500) | After (c. 1555–1600) |
|---|---|
| One Latin Church, headed by the pope, with its own courts, property and taxes | Protestant territorial churches headed by the prince (*landesherrliches Kirchenregiment*) — the prince as "emergency bishop" (*Notbischof*) |
| Church property exempt from lay control | Monasteries dissolved; property taken by princes and towns (funding schools, universities, poor relief — and princely treasuries) |
| Emperor as protector of the one Church | Emperor (Charles V) unable to impose unity; religious peace negotiated among estates |
| Religion a universal matter | Religion tied to territory |

**Key steps:**
- **Diet of Worms (1521):** Luther outlawed (Edict of Worms), but his prince, Frederick the Wise, protected him.
- **Diet of Speyer (1526):** Pending a council, each estate should act on religion as it could answer "to God and the Emperor" — in practice licensing princely church reforms (bpb calls 1526 the "impulse for the landesherrliches Kirchenregiment").
- **1529:** Evangelical estates "protested" against revoking this — hence "Protestants."
- **1530:** Augsburg Confession — Lutheran creed.
- **1531:** Schmalkaldic League of Protestant princes and cities.
- **1546–47:** Schmalkaldic War; Charles V wins militarily but cannot impose a settlement.
- **1552:** Princes' revolt (allied with France).
- **1555: Peace of Augsburg:** Lutheranism legalised alongside Catholicism; rulers choose (the later formula *cuius regio, eius religio* was coined by the jurist Joachim Stephani c. 1600); dissenting subjects gained a *right to emigrate* (*ius emigrandi*) — an early individual right; "ecclesiastical reservation": Catholic prince-bishops who converted had to resign (contested by Protestants). Calvinists were excluded.

### Regime Matrix — A Lutheran territorial principality (e.g., Electoral Saxony c. 1580)

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Formally ruled | The prince (Elector), under the emperor's overlordship. |
| 2 | Actually held power | The prince + his council (*Hofrat*), consistory (church board), and the territorial estates (nobles, towns) who granted taxes. |
| 3 | Power acquired | Dynastic inheritance. |
| 4 | Power maintained | Estates' consent to taxes; control of church appointments; courts; visitations. |
| 5 | Who could challenge | Estates (by refusing taxes), neighbouring princes, the emperor, imperial courts. |
| 6 | Money | Domain income, former church lands, taxes granted by estates, mining (silver). |
| 7 | Enforcement | Officials (*Amtmänner*), church visitations, consistory, courts. |
| 8 | Representation | Nobles and towns in the *Landtag*; peasants almost never (exceptions: e.g., Tyrol, Württemberg's *Ehrbarkeit*). |
| 9 | Rights | Corporate privileges; right to emigrate for religious dissenters (1555). |
| 10 | Opponents | Imprisonment, exile; religious dissenters (Anabaptists) executed or expelled. |
| 11 | Connecting institutions | The pastor (now a state-appointed official), the parish, the school, church ordinances. |
| 12 | Ordinary person's view | The pastor preaches obedience, catechises children, records births and marriages, and reports misconduct — the church becomes the state's eyes in the village. |

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** Protestant theology (priesthood of all believers) promised congregational autonomy; in practice, church government was absorbed into princely administration.

## 3. Society

- **Clergy:** Protestant pastors married, forming a new educated middle stratum (the *Pfarrhaus*, parsonage, became a major source of German intellectuals for centuries).
- **Nobility:** Gained from secularised church lands; in Catholic areas kept access to prince-bishoprics.
- **Towns:** Early centres of reform; councils introduced reformation by decree, often under pressure from citizens.
- **Peasants:** Demanded "godly law" — the **Twelve Articles (1525)** asked for communal election of pastors, abolition of the small tithe, end of serfdom ("Christ redeemed us all"), fair rents, and access to woods, water and game. After defeat, peasants lost hope of political voice but gained, in some regions, more negotiated settlements and legal avenues (Peter Blickle).
- **Women:** Convents dissolved in Protestant lands — closing one route to independence and education; marriage and household exalted as the godly life; pastors' wives as a new role.
- **Minorities:** Anabaptists persecuted by all sides (Münster 1534–35); Jews targeted by Luther's late anti-Jewish writings (*On the Jews and Their Lies*, 1543) — later exploited by Nazis.

## 4. Everyday Life

Worship in German (hymns, Bible, catechism); fewer holy days; no saints' cults, pilgrimages or purgatory in Protestant areas; marriage courts and moral discipline; schooling expanded for boys (and some girls) to read the catechism. In Catholic areas the later **Counter-Reformation** (Jesuits, Council of Trent 1545–63) renewed parishes and piety. Religion became a marker of identity between neighbouring territories.

## 5. Economy

Transfer of church property to princes and towns; poor relief reorganised ("common chest"); printing industry boom; some economic benefits to rulers who confiscated monastic lands. The Peasants' War did not change the agrarian structure.

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

- **Luther's theology:** Justification by faith alone; scripture alone; priesthood of all believers; **two kingdoms** doctrine — spiritual freedom but worldly obedience (Romans 13), which Luther used against the peasants ("Against the Murderous, Thieving Hordes of Peasants," 1525).
- **Zwingli and Calvin:** Reformed Protestantism (Swiss origin), stronger on church discipline, spread to the Palatinate (1560s), later Brandenburg's ruling house (1613).
- **"German freedom" vs Habsburg "tyranny":** Protestant princes framed resistance to Charles V as defence of "German liberty" — a constitutional, not democratic, idea.
- **Ordinary belief:** Popular religion mixed old practices with new doctrine for generations; visitation reports show pastors complaining of ignorance, magic and non-attendance.

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

Anticlericalism; printing; humanism; princes' fiscal and political interests; the emperor's distraction by France and the Ottomans; peasant grievances over rising lordly demands.

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE:** One Church, imperial ideal of Christian unity, clerical privilege.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Luther's challenge + print + princely and urban interests + social grievances.
↓
**TRANSITION (crisis):** Peasants' War (1524–26); Schmalkaldic War (1546–47).
↓
**AFTER:** Peace of Augsburg 1555 — confessional territorial states within a bi-confessional Empire. Unresolved issues (Calvinism, church lands, ecclesiastical reservation) fuel the next crisis.

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

Your religion now depended on your ruler. Your pastor became a state official who taught you the catechism, registered your life events and reported on your morals. Your holidays, rituals and images changed (in Protestant lands). If you dissented, you could emigrate — or, if Anabaptist, face death.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

Lordship, serfdom where it existed, the estates order, and the Empire's institutions — which proved able to absorb the split for 60 years.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| Luther nailed theses to a door in a dramatic act of defiance | The posting is debated; he sent them to his archbishop; the event became symbolic later. |
| The Reformation brought freedom | It brought *confessional* choice for rulers; for subjects it often meant tighter discipline. |
| The Peasants' War was a class war in the modern sense (older Marxist view) or merely religious fanaticism (older conservative view) | It was a "revolution of the common man" (Blickle) combining religious justification with concrete economic and political demands. |
| *Cuius regio, eius religio* is a phrase from 1555 | Coined c. 1600; the treaty didn't use it. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **Confessionalisation thesis (Heinz Schilling, Wolfgang Reinhard, from the 1980s):** Lutheran, Reformed and Catholic churches all performed functionally similar work — building confessional identity, disciplining subjects, strengthening the early modern state. **Critiques:** Heinrich Richard Schmidt argued moral discipline came largely from communities "from below"; others argue the model is too state-centred ("etatist"), downplays theological differences, and overestimates effectiveness. **CONTESTED.**
- **Peasants' War:** Friedrich Engels (1850) and GDR historiography ("early bourgeois revolution") vs Western social history (Blickle's "revolution of the common man," 1975). The 2025 quincentenary produced many exhibitions stressing its democratic demands.
- **Luther and antisemitism:** Consensus that his late writings were virulently anti-Jewish; debate about continuity to modern racial antisemitism (most historians distinguish religious anti-Judaism from racial antisemitism while acknowledging Nazi exploitation).

### If you remember only 5 things

1. The Reformation's biggest political effect was to make princes the heads of their churches.
2. Religion became territorial: your ruler's faith became your faith (or you emigrated).
3. The peasants read the Gospel as a demand for justice — and were crushed, with Luther's blessing.
4. The pastor and the parish became instruments of state discipline as well as faith.
5. The Empire survived the split through compromise, but left key issues unresolved for 1618.
