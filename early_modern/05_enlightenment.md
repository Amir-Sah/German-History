## 1. Period in One Paragraph

The German Enlightenment (*Aufklärung*) was less revolutionary and more state-friendly than its French counterpart. Its home was not salons in a capital but **universities** (Halle, Göttingen, Königsberg), **Protestant parsonages**, **reading societies**, **Masonic lodges** and **journals** spread across dozens of small states. Its carriers were officials, professors, pastors and educated townspeople — a new stratum later called the *Bildungsbürgertum* (educated middle class). Its programme was reform: rational law, religious toleration, better schools and agriculture, public debate — often pursued *through* reforming princes rather than against them. Kant's 1784 essay "What is Enlightenment?" defined it as "man's emergence from his self-incurred immaturity" and celebrated the freedom to reason in public — while accepting obedience in one's official duties. Late in the century came reaction against rationalism (*Sturm und Drang*, Herder's ideas of peoples and languages, then Romanticism) — ideas that would later feed cultural nationalism.

## 2. The Political Structure

No new political structure — rather, new relations between **state** and **public**:
- **Enlightened absolutism** (Frederick II in Prussia; Joseph II in Austria; smaller princes in Baden, Weimar, Anhalt-Dessau) adopted reform ideas: legal codification, limits on torture, toleration, schooling, agricultural improvement.
- **Censorship** existed everywhere, but fragmentation meant that a book banned in one state could be printed in another.
- **Public sphere** (*Öffentlichkeit*, Jürgen Habermas's concept): reading societies (hundreds by 1800), coffee houses, journals like the *Berlinische Monatsschrift* (where Kant's essay appeared). Participation required literacy, money and leisure — i.e., a small minority.

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** Enlightenment rhetoric addressed "humanity," but political participation remained the prerogative of princes and privileged estates. Kant himself distinguished the "public use of reason" (free) from the "private use" in one's office (bound to obey) and praised Frederick's maxim: "Argue as much as you like and about whatever you like, *but obey!*"

## 3. Society

- **Bildungsbürgertum:** officials, professors, pastors, lawyers, doctors — defined by education rather than birth or wealth; closely tied to state service.
- **Nobility** increasingly educated; some aristocratic reformers.
- **Jews:** The *Haskala* (Jewish Enlightenment), led by Moses Mendelssohn in Berlin; Christian Wilhelm Dohm's *On the Civil Improvement of the Jews* (1781) argued for gradual emancipation; Joseph II's Toleration Patent for Vienna's Jews (1782). Emancipation was framed as conditional on Jews "improving" themselves — a pattern that shaped the 19th century.
- **Women:** Salons (especially Jewish women's salons in Berlin, e.g., Rahel Varnhagen, Henriette Herz, c. 1790–1806) as spaces of mixed sociability; but Enlightenment thinkers largely confined women to domestic roles; exceptions like Dorothea Erxleben (first woman to earn a medical doctorate in Germany, Halle 1754) were rare.

## 4. Everyday Life

For the majority, the Enlightenment arrived as **reform from above**: schooling decrees, new agricultural methods, vaccination (late century), reductions in holy days (Catholic states), burial reforms, and campaigns against "superstition" that could feel intrusive. Literacy rose (estimates of adult literacy around 1800 range widely — perhaps 25–50% depending on region and definition — **UNCERTAIN**). A "reading revolution" (Rolf Engelsing) — from intensive re-reading of a few religious texts to extensive reading of novels and periodicals — affected the urban middle classes.

## 5. Economy

Cameralism and physiocratic ideas; agricultural improvement (potato cultivation promoted by Frederick II); early manufactures. Critique of guild restrictions and serfdom grew but produced reform mostly after 1800.

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

| Current | Key figures | Core idea |
|---|---|---|
| Rationalist natural law | Christian Wolff, Christian Thomasius | Reason can derive laws and duties; toleration |
| Critical philosophy | Immanuel Kant | Autonomy of reason; moral law; limits of knowledge; public use of reason |
| Religious toleration | Gotthold Ephraim Lessing (*Nathan the Wise*, 1779), Mendelssohn | Truth in all religions; humane coexistence |
| Historicism/cultural nationalism | Johann Gottfried Herder | Each people (*Volk*) has a unique spirit expressed in language and culture — plural, not hierarchical in Herder, but later nationalised |
| Weimar classicism | Goethe, Schiller | Humanist education (*Bildung*) and aesthetic cultivation |
| Neohumanism | Wilhelm von Humboldt | Education as self-formation; led to the Berlin university (1810) |

**German "Kulturnation":** Because there was no German state, intellectuals imagined Germany as a *cultural* nation bound by language and literature. This idea would later be politicised.

**What ordinary people believed:** Largely traditional Christian faith; resistance to reforms that touched rituals (e.g., new hymnals, reduced feast days, bans on processions sparked protests in Catholic and Protestant areas alike).

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

Rising literacy and print; fiscal needs of states pushing efficiency reforms; the American and French Revolutions (1776, 1789) — initially welcomed by many German intellectuals, then feared after the Terror.

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE:** A culture of confessional orthodoxy and courtly display.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Print, universities, reading societies, reforming bureaucracies.
↓
**TRANSITION:** 1789 — the French Revolution politicises Enlightenment ideas; German states respond with censorship and reform.
↓
**AFTER:** Napoleonic conquest implements some Enlightenment reforms by force (Code civil, abolition of privileges) — see `nineteenth_century/01_napoleon.md`.

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

Better schooling (slowly), some reduction of judicial torture (Prussia 1740/1754), some religious toleration, and more intrusive reforming states. Politically, little.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

Estate society; princely rule; confessional churches; censorship.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| The German Enlightenment was apolitical and servile | It was reform-oriented and critical, but it worked with states more than against them. |
| Herder invented ethnic nationalism | Herder's pluralist cultural idea was later reshaped into exclusive nationalism by others. |
| Enlightenment = secular | Much of it was religious (Protestant, Jewish) reform. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **"Apolitical German" thesis:** Older view (and later Sonderweg arguments) that German intellectuals withdrew into culture, leaving politics to princes. Critics point to lively political journalism, reading societies and reform bureaucracies.

  *In plain words:* Did German thinkers retreat into culture and leave politics to the princes? An older view, later used in 'Sonderweg' (special-path) arguments, says yes. Critics reply that there was lively political journalism, reading societies and reform-minded officials.
- **Habermas's public sphere (1962):** influential, criticised for idealising a bourgeois male sphere.

  *In plain words:* How useful is Habermas's idea of a 'public sphere' — a space where private people debated public affairs in print, clubs and coffee houses? It has been very influential, but critics say it paints too rosy a picture of a world open mainly to educated middle-class men.
- **Jewish emancipation as "conditional":** broad agreement that emancipation was framed as a quid pro quo for assimilation.

  *In plain words:* On what terms were Jews offered equal rights? Here historians broadly agree: 'emancipation' (legal equality) was framed as a bargain — rights in exchange for Jews giving up their distinctiveness and assimilating.

### If you remember only 5 things

1. The German Enlightenment was carried by officials, professors and pastors — and often by reforming princes.
2. Its motto was public reasoning, not revolution: "argue, but obey."
3. A new educated middle class (*Bildungsbürgertum*) defined itself by culture and state service.
4. Without a German state, intellectuals imagined a German *cultural* nation.
5. Jewish emancipation was debated as conditional on assimilation — a pattern with long consequences.
