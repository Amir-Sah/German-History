## 1. Period in One Paragraph

Two dynasties outgrew the Empire. The **Habsburgs** ruled Austria, Bohemia and (after defeating the Ottomans in 1683–99) Hungary — a vast, multi-lingual monarchy whose centre lay partly outside the Empire, and who nearly always held the imperial crown. The **Hohenzollerns** of Brandenburg, starting from a poor, sandy electorate scattered across northern Germany, built a disproportionately large army and a disciplined administration, gained a royal title (1701, for Prussia, which lay outside the Empire), and under Frederick II seized Silesia from Austria (1740). From then on, **Austro-Prussian dualism** dominated German politics. Prussia's success rested on a bargain: the Junker nobility served as officers and kept near-total control over their peasants; the crown got an army and taxes. Whether this made Prussia uniquely "militaristic" — and whether that later shaped Germany — is one of the great debates of German history.

## 2. The Political Structure

### Brandenburg-Prussia

- **Frederick William, the "Great Elector" (r. 1640–88):** After the Thirty Years' War, built a standing army; in the **Recess of 1653** the Brandenburg estates granted taxes for the army in exchange for confirmation of the nobles' rights over their peasants. Later bypassed the estates with excise taxes in towns and a war commissariat.
- **Frederick III/I:** Crowned "King in Prussia" (1701).
- **Frederick William I, the "Soldier King" (r. 1713–40):** Doubled the army (~80,000 by 1740 in a population of ~2.2 million); created the **General Directory** (1723) for finances and war; introduced the **canton system** (1732/33 — regiments assigned recruiting districts, formalised in the 1733 canton regulation): each regiment recruited from a district; peasants served a few months a year and then returned to farm, under the supervision of their noble landlords who were also their officers.
- **Frederick II "the Great" (r. 1740–86):** Wars for Silesia; "first servant of the state"; Enlightenment reforms (limits on torture, religious toleration, judicial reform) combined with militarism and noble privilege; the **Allgemeines Landrecht (ALR)** — General Law Code — completed in 1794.

### Regime Matrix — Prussia under Frederick II

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Formally ruled | The king (absolute in claim). |
| 2 | Actually held power | King + cabinet (he ruled via written orders from Potsdam) + bureaucracy; locally, Junker landlords with police and court powers (*Gutsherrschaft*); the officer corps. |
| 3 | Power acquired | Inheritance. |
| 4 | Power maintained | Army; bureaucracy recruited partly on merit (exams for judicial officials); alliance with nobility; religious toleration to attract immigrants; strict finances. |
| 5 | Who could challenge | Foreign powers; the nobility (if its privileges were threatened) — the crown carefully did not threaten them. |
| 6 | Money | Domains (~one-third of revenue); excise in towns; land tax (*Kontribution*) on peasants (nobles largely exempt in core provinces); Silesian revenues; British subsidies in the Seven Years' War. |
| 7 | Enforcement | Officials, army, Junker local rule. |
| 8 | Representation | Provincial estates marginalised; no national representation. |
| 9 | Rights | By estate; ALR codified duties and rights of each estate; religious toleration relatively wide (Huguenots, Catholics in Silesia; Jews limited and taxed). |
| 10 | Opponents | Fortress imprisonment; censorship (though Frederick was relatively lax toward non-political writing). |
| 11 | Connecting institutions | Army (canton system), church (Lutheran and Reformed), Junker estate, schools (1763 General School Regulation). |
| 12 | Ordinary person's view | East of the Elbe: hereditary subjection to a Junker who was landlord, police chief, judge and captain; west: more free peasants and towns. |

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** The king's authority was formally unlimited; in practice, the monarchy governed the countryside *through* the nobility, whose local power it guaranteed. Otto Hintze and Hans Rosenberg described a compromise: royal centralisation at the top, noble autonomy at the bottom.

### Habsburg Monarchy

- A **composite monarchy**: Austrian hereditary lands, Bohemian crown, Hungarian crown, later Galicia (1772), Austrian Netherlands, Italian lands — each with its own estates and laws.
- **Pragmatic Sanction (1713):** allowed female succession — Maria Theresa (r. 1740–80), whose accession triggered Prussia's invasion of Silesia.
- **Theresian and Josephine reforms:** centralised administration (Directorium 1749), compulsory schooling (1774), reduction of peasant labour services (*Robotpatent*), Joseph II's abolition of personal serfdom (1781 in Bohemia), **Toleration Patents (1781–82)** for Protestants, Orthodox and Jews, dissolution of contemplative monasteries. Joseph's centralism provoked revolts (Hungary, Belgium) and much was withdrawn.

## 3. Society

**Prussia:**
- **Junkers:** Landed nobility east of the Elbe; ran estates, provided officers and officials; many were not rich.
- **Peasants under *Gutsherrschaft*:** hereditary subjects (*Erbuntertanen*), owing labour services, needing lord's consent to marry or leave; subject to the lord's patrimonial court.
- **Towns:** small, garrisoned, taxed via excise; Berlin grew into a capital (Huguenots were ~20% of its population around 1700).
- **Soldiers:** foreign recruits (up to half the army at times) plus cantonists.

**Habsburg lands:** Multi-ethnic (German, Czech, Hungarian, Polish, Italian, Croatian…), Catholic-dominated after violent re-Catholicisation of Bohemia after 1620; aristocracy extremely wealthy (Bohemian magnates).

## 4. Everyday Life

For an East Elbian peasant: work on your own holding plus several days a week on the lord's estate; the lord as judge; sons liable for military service via the canton; the pastor reading royal edicts from the pulpit. William Hagen's study of the Stavenow estate (Brandenburg) shows, however, that villagers were not passive: they litigated, negotiated, slacked, and enjoyed living standards not far below western peasants.

## 5. Economy

Agrarian, with grain exports from the east (Baltic trade). Prussian state promoted manufactures (silk, textiles, arms), immigration and land reclamation (Oderbruch). Silesia (linen, mining) was an economic prize. Habsburg economy: diverse; Bohemia proto-industrial.

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

- **Service to the state:** Frederick II's image of the ruler as "first servant of the state" — a secular justification of monarchy.
- **Pietism:** discipline and duty (Halle).
- **Enlightened absolutism:** reform from above for the common good — without participation.
- **Austrian Catholic baroque piety** and Josephinism (state-controlled Catholic reform).

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

Great-power wars (Silesian Wars 1740–63, costing Prussia perhaps 10% of its population); partitions of Poland (1772–95) — adding millions of Polish Catholic subjects to Prussia and Austria; fiscal strains; the French Revolution; and finally Prussia's catastrophic defeat by Napoleon at Jena–Auerstedt (1806).

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE:** Two dynastic great powers inside (and outside) a weak Empire.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Dualism, Polish partitions, revolutionary France.
↓
**TRANSITION (crisis):** 1805 (Austria defeated at Austerlitz), 1806 (Prussia defeated).
↓
**AFTER:** Austria proclaims an Austrian Empire (1804), lays down the Roman crown (1806); Prussia undertakes radical reforms (1807–1819) — see `nineteenth_century/01_napoleon.md`.

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

More soldiers, more taxes, more schools, more law codes; in Austria, some relief from serfdom under Joseph II; in Prussia, noble control of peasants remained until the reforms after 1807.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

The Junker-peasant relationship in the east; the multi-national Habsburg structure; the dynastic principle.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| Prussia = Germany | Prussia was one (partly non-German, partly extra-imperial) state among many; much of Germany disliked it. |
| Prussian militarism was a German national character | It was a specific institutional bargain in one state; its later "Germany-wide" influence is debated. |
| East Elbian peasants were passive, crushed serfs | Hagen shows agency, litigation and resistance. |
| Frederick the Great was a liberal | He was a reforming autocrat who strengthened noble privilege and waged aggressive war. |
| Austria wasn't really "German" | The Habsburgs were the leading German dynasty for centuries; Austria was central to the Empire and the Confederation until 1866. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **Social militarisation (Otto Büsch, 1962):** the canton system fused estate and regiment, militarising rural society and producing a lasting habit of obedience. **Critiques:** Hagen and others argue the model is too top-down; comparative work shows other European armies were similar; the canton system was more negotiated than Büsch suggested; research by Harnisch, Enders and Hagen shows wage labour mattered in the Prussian countryside well before the reforms. Reviewing the English translation, Philip Dwyer (H-German 1997) accepts Büsch's core picture of crown–Junker interdependence but criticises "sometimes excessive claims of causality" and the neglect of foreign policy: "Foreign policy determined the manner in which Prussian society developed."
- **Junkers and the Sonderweg:** Hans Rosenberg, F. L. Carsten (Junker tyranny, peasant degradation) vs William Hagen (*Ordinary Prussians*, 2002), who calls for "a nuanced depiction" that "does not strip them of their capacity to act in their own interests."
- **Christopher Clark (*Iron Kingdom*, 2006):** Prussia as a fragile, improvised state whose later "militarist" image owes much to the 19th and 20th centuries; its dissolution by the Allies (1947) was based partly on a myth.
- **Was Prussia's "rise" inevitable?** No — it nearly collapsed in 1759–61 and 1806.

### If you remember only 5 things

1. Prussia's power rested on a bargain: Junkers served the king as officers; the king left them masters of their peasants.
2. The army was huge relative to population and shaped state finances and administration.
3. Austria was a multi-national monarchy whose power base lay largely outside "Germany," yet it led the Empire.
4. "Enlightened absolutism" meant reform from above without participation.
5. Whether Prussian militarism explains later German history is debated, not settled.
