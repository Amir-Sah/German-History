# Image Index

*Every picture used in this knowledge base, with its creator, date, holding institution, source page and licence. Pictures are embedded by link (hotlinked) from the institutions' own servers, so the Markdown files need an internet connection to display them.*

## How images were chosen

- **Goal:** one or more images per chapter that convey the *atmosphere* of the era — contemporary art, objects, photographs and posters, preferred over later reconstructions.
- **Contemporary sources first.** Almost every image was made in the period it illustrates (e.g., Callot's etchings of the Thirty Years' War, 1633; Bundesarchiv photographs of 1918 and 1933). Where an image is later (e.g., Tiepolo's 1750s sketch of a 1168 event; Bürde's 1871 celebration), the caption says so.
- **Images are sources too.** Propaganda (the *Völkischer Beobachter*, Hitler Youth marches), official art (Bürde, Hünten) and posed colonial photographs show how power wanted to be seen — read them with the same source criticism as texts.
- **Graphic atrocity photographs were deliberately not used.** The Holocaust and war chapters use images of places and objects instead.

All 72 image links were checked on 27 September 2026 and returned an image. **Rights summary:** 18 images are public domain / CC0 or "no known restrictions" (Cleveland, Met, Library of Congress); 54 are © (DHM, HdG, Bundesarchiv, photographers' estates) and are for personal study only — a public version should show them as credit-and-link cards instead.

**Image types** (column *Type*) were assigned by hand from each record: painting, print (woodcut, engraving, etching, lithograph), artwork (medium not stated by the holder), manuscript, object, document, poster, cartoon, map, photograph, film still.

## Captions (full text)

- **Bow Fibula (brooch)** (`fibula`): A Frankish bow brooch (*Fibel*) used to fasten a cloak. Jewellery like this, found in graves, is much of what we actually know about early Germanic-speaking elites: status, craft and contact with the Roman world.
- **"Caster Ware" Vase with Hunt Scene** (`caster-vase`): Pottery made in Roman Cologne on the Rhine frontier. Workshops like this supplied soldiers and townspeople on both sides of the Limes (the Roman frontier line).
- **Bifolium from a Carolingian Gradual (chant book)** (`carolingian-gradual`): A page from a Carolingian chant book. Under Charlemagne and his heirs, monasteries copied liturgy and classical texts in the new, clear *Carolingian minuscule* script — the root of our lowercase letters.
- **Star Brooch** (`star-brooch`): A Frankish brooch from the age of Charlemagne — the warrior aristocracy's taste for gold and gems.
- **Christ's Mission to the Apostles (one of the 'Magdeburg Ivories')** (`ottonian-ivory`): An ivory panel made for Otto I's new archbishopric of Magdeburg. The Ottonian kings ruled *through* the Church: bishops were their administrators, and sacred art advertised a Christian, Roman-style empire.
- **Peasant Couple Dancing** (`durer-dancing`): Dürer's engraving of peasants dancing — the rural majority (*Bauern*) as seen by a Nuremberg city artist: earthy, lively, a little mocked.
- **The Peasants at Market** (`durer-market`): Peasants bringing goods to town: the market linked the village economy to the walled city.
- **Leaf from the Nuremberg Chronicle (*Schedelsche Weltchronik*)** (`nuremberg-chronicle`): The Nuremberg Chronicle, one of the great early printed books, filled with woodcut views of cities. It shows how proud imperial cities (*Reichsstädte*) imagined themselves: walled, spired, and self-governing.
- **The Investiture of Bishop Harold as Duke of Franconia (sketch for the Würzburg Residenz)** (`tiepolo-harold`): Oil sketch for the ceiling of the prince-bishop's palace in Würzburg, celebrating a bishop receiving the power of a duke from Emperor Frederick Barbarossa (1168). In the Holy Roman Empire, church and secular lordship were fused: a *Fürstbischof* (prince-bishop) ruled land like any prince.
- **Martin Luther** (`cranach-luther`): Cranach's workshop in Wittenberg produced Luther's image in series — an early media campaign that made the reformer's face known across the Empire.
- **The Hanging, from *The Large Miseries of War*** (`callot-hanging`): Callot's etchings of soldiers punished by hanging, made during the Thirty Years' War. Armies lived off the land; plunder, reprisal and hunger killed far more people than battles.
- **Pillaging, from *The Large Miseries of War*** (`callot-pillaging`): Soldiers looting a farmhouse — the everyday face of the war for villagers.
- **View of Dresden** (`bellotto-dresden`): Dresden, capital of electoral Saxony, painted by the Venetian Bellotto for the court. Baroque residence cities like this were the showpieces of the territorial princes after 1648.
- **Teapot with portrait of Frederick the Great of Prussia** (`teapot-frederick`): Frederick II's face on a teapot, made during or just after the Seven Years' War: the Prussian king had become a celebrity and a consumer brand across Europe.
- **Cagliostro** (`chodowiecki-cagliostro`): Berlin's great Enlightenment etcher mocks the celebrity occultist 'Count' Cagliostro. The Enlightenment (*Aufklärung*) fought superstition and charlatans with reason, satire and print — in journals read by a new educated middle class (*Bildungsbürgertum*).
- **Susanne (The Artist's Daughter at the Age of Twenty-One)** (`chodowiecki-susanne`): Chodowiecki's portrait of his daughter: the intimate, domestic world of an educated Berlin family in the age of Moses Mendelssohn and Lessing.
- **Bicorne hat worn by Napoleon I** (`napoleon-bicorne`): Napoleon's two-cornered hat (*Zweispitz*), captured by Prussian soldiers in his carriage after Waterloo. Napoleon destroyed the Holy Roman Empire and redrew Germany's map — and his defeat made Prussia a great power on the Rhine.
- **The morning after the Battle of Waterloo, 19 June 1815** (`waterloo-morning`): The battlefield of Waterloo the morning after — the end of 23 years of revolutionary and Napoleonic war.
- **Session of the plenipotentiaries of the eight great powers (Congress of Vienna)** (`vienna-congress`): Diplomats at the Congress of Vienna, which created the German Confederation (*Deutscher Bund*): 39 sovereign states, no nation-state, and Metternich's order of monarchy and censorship.
- **Der Geschichtsunterricht (The History Lesson)** (`geschichtsunterricht`): A father teaches his son history from pictures — a quiet *Biedermeier* interior (the domestic, private culture of the restoration years), where politics was discussed at home because public politics was censored.
- **Procession to Hambach Castle, 27 May 1832** (`hambach`): The Hambach Festival (*Hambacher Fest*): some 20,000–30,000 people marched with black-red-gold flags demanding national unity, freedom of the press and popular sovereignty.
- **Two Men Contemplating the Moon** (`friedrich-moon`): The Romantic mood of German national feeling: two figures in 'old German' dress (*altdeutsche Tracht*, a coded sign of liberal-national sympathy after 1819) look into nature and the infinite. Nation, feeling and landscape fused.
- **"Armuth" (Poverty)** (`armuth`): A contemporary image of *Pauperismus* — mass poverty before industrialisation absorbed the growing population. Hunger and unemployment fed the revolutions of 1848.
- **Barricade fighting in Berlin, March 1848** (`barrikaden`): Barricades in Berlin on 18–19 March 1848 (*Märzrevolution*). Street fighting forced King Frederick William IV to promise a constitution — for a time.
- **The Battle of Königgrätz 1866** (`koeniggraetz`): Prussia's victory over Austria at Königgrätz (Sadowa) decided that Germany would be unified *without* Austria and under Prussian leadership — by 'iron and blood'.
- **Huldigung an Kaiser Wilhelm I. (Homage to Emperor Wilhelm I)** (`kaiserproklamation`): Celebration of the new German Emperor in 1871. The Empire was founded by princes and generals — proclaimed in Versailles, not voted by a parliament.
- **Unter den Linden, Berlin (Photochrom print)** (`unter-den-linden`): Berlin's grand boulevard *Unter den Linden* in the Kaiserreich: an imperial capital of palaces, monuments and new wealth, in hand-coloured photochrom.
- **Der Streik (The Strike)** (`koehler-streik`): Workers confront a factory owner — painted in the years of Bismarck's Anti-Socialist Laws. The 'social question' (*soziale Frage*) was the great domestic conflict of the Empire.
- **Berlin tenement courtyard (*Mietskaserne*)** (`mietskaserne`): A Berlin *Mietskaserne* ('rental barracks'): deep blocks of flats around dark courtyards, built to house the millions who moved to industrial cities.
- **German colonial official and servant in German East Africa** (`kolonialbeamter`): A German colonial official with an African servant in German East Africa. The posed hierarchy is the point: colonial rule rested on racial ranking, forced labour and, when resisted, extreme violence (Maji Maji war 1905–07; genocide of Herero and Nama 1904–08 in South West Africa).
- **In the swamped battle zone before Armentières** (`armentieres`): German soldiers in the flooded, cratered ground of the Western Front, 1918 — the industrialised war of attrition that killed about two million German soldiers.
- **Instructions for using swede turnips (*Kohlrüben*)** (`kohlrueben`): Official advice on cooking turnips during the 'turnip winter' (*Kohlrübenwinter*, also called *Steckrübenwinter*) of 1916–17, when the blockade and poor harvests brought hunger to the home front.
- **Hindenburg and Ludendorff** (`hindenburg-ludendorff`): Field Marshal Hindenburg and General Ludendorff, whose Supreme Army Command (*OHL*) ran Germany as a near-military dictatorship from 1916.
- **Crowd in front of the Reichstag, 9 November 1918** (`reichstag-1918`): Crowds at the Reichstag on 9 November 1918, the day the Republic was proclaimed and the Kaiser's abdication announced.
- **The National Assembly in session, Weimar** (`nationalversammlung`): The National Assembly (*Nationalversammlung*), elected by men and — for the first time — women, met in Weimar in 1919 to write the constitution.
- **Banknote bundle of one billion marks** (`billion-marks`): A bundle of banknotes during the hyperinflation of 1923, when a loaf of bread cost billions of marks and middle-class savings vanished.
- **Streit der Bettler um den besten Platz (Beggars quarrelling over the best spot)** (`ballhause`): Ballhause photographed the unemployed of Hanover in the Great Depression. By 1932 about six million Germans were registered unemployed.
- **Hitler's cabinet on the day of his appointment** (`kabinett-hitler`): Hitler's first cabinet: only three Nazis, surrounded by conservatives (Papen, Hugenberg) who believed they could 'tame' him. He was appointed, not elected, to power.
- **Hitler Youth propaganda march** (`hj-march`): A Hitler Youth (*Hitlerjugend*) march in 1933: uniforms, flags, and the mobilisation of the young that the regime relied on.
- **Völkischer Beobachter reporting the Enabling Act** (`vb-enabling`): The Nazi party paper celebrating the Enabling Act (*Ermächtigungsgesetz*), which let the government make laws without parliament — dictatorship with a legal façade.
- **People's Receiver (*Volksempfänger*) VE 301 dyn** (`volksempfaenger`): The cheap 'people's radio' brought Goebbels' propaganda, music and Hitler's speeches into millions of living rooms.
- **British airdropped leaflet reprinting the White Rose manifesto** (`white-rose`): After the Munich students of the White Rose (*Weiße Rose*) were executed in 1943, the RAF dropped their last leaflet over Germany — rare, brave dissent in a society of conformity.
- **Ohel Jakob Synagogue, Munich, after the arson of November 1938** (`synagogue-munich`): The burned-out Ohel Jakob synagogue in Munich after the November pogrom (*Novemberpogrom*, 9–10 November 1938): public, organised violence months before the war, and a step toward genocide.
