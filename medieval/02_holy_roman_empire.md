## 1. Period in One Paragraph

The Holy Roman Empire was not a German nation-state that failed to centralise; it was a different kind of political order. From 919 a Saxon dynasty ruled the East Frankish kingdom, and in 962 Otto I was crowned emperor in Rome, fusing the German kingship with the Roman imperial title. For the next 850 years the "Reich" combined a German kingdom, claims over Italy and Burgundy, Bohemia and later Habsburg lands. Kings travelled constantly because they had no capital; they ruled with and through bishops, dukes, counts and later cities. Crucially, the kingship was **elective**: the great princes chose the king, and by the 13th century a small group of **prince-electors** monopolised that right (fixed in the Golden Bull of 1356). Conflicts with the papacy (the Investiture Controversy, 1075–1122) destroyed the king's sacred control of the Church and strengthened the princes. By 1500 the Empire had become a **federation of hundreds of principalities, church lands and cities** under an emperor who was usually a Habsburg and whose power depended on his own dynastic lands.

## 2. The Political Structure

### Regime Matrix — Medieval Empire

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Formally ruled | The king of the Romans (elected), who became emperor through papal coronation (until the 16th century). |
| 2 | Actually held power | King + great princes (dukes, archbishops, later electors) + increasingly towns; real power on the ground lay with territorial lords. |
| 3 | Power acquired | Election by the princes (combined, until the 13th c., with dynastic claims); coronation (Aachen for king, Rome for emperor). |
| 4 | Power maintained | Itinerant court; assemblies (*Hoftage*); royal estates and rights; the imperial church; marriage alliances; warfare. |
| 5 | Who could challenge | Princes (rebellions, rival kings — e.g., 1077, 1198, 1314); the papacy (excommunication); Italian cities. |
| 6 | Money | Royal lands (dwindling), tolls, mint rights, Jewish "protection" payments, imperial cities' taxes, hospitality owed by churches; no general imperial tax until the 1495 "Common Penny" (largely failed). |
| 7 | Enforcement | Royal courts, military expeditions, the ban (outlawry), and dependence on princes to enforce judgements. Feud was a legitimate legal means for nobles until 1495. |
| 8 | Representation | Princes represented themselves at assemblies; cities gained a place at Diets in the 15th c.; peasants had none. |
| 9 | Rights | "Liberties" (*Freiheiten*) were privileges of specific groups — clergy, nobles, towns — not individual rights. |
| 10 | Opponents | Outlawed, deprived of fiefs, fought in feud; defeated princes often reconciled through ritual submission. |
| 11 | Connecting institutions | Bishops and abbeys; feudal bonds (fief-holding); towns and their charters; courts. |
| 12 | Ordinary person's view | The emperor was a distant, sacred figure; the lord, the priest and the town council were the state. |

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** There was no single written constitution. The Golden Bull (1356) fixed the electoral procedure and privileges of the seven electors (archbishops of Mainz, Cologne, Trier; king of Bohemia; count palatine of the Rhine; duke of Saxony; margrave of Brandenburg), established majority election, and made electoral lands indivisible. It did *not* give the emperor administrative power over the territories. In practice, an emperor was powerful to the extent that his **own dynastic lands** made him so (the Luxembourgs in Bohemia; the Habsburgs in Austria and later Burgundy/Spain).

### Key structural developments

- **Ottonian–Salian "imperial church":** Kings appointed and invested bishops and abbots, endowed them with counties and royal rights, and used them as administrators and army suppliers. Historian Timothy Reuter (1982) warned that this was not a deliberate "system" (*Reichskirchensystem*) but a practice common across Latin Europe that German historians had over-systematised. **CONTESTED.**
- **Investiture Controversy (1075–1122):** Pope Gregory VII challenged Henry IV's right to appoint bishops; Henry's penance at **Canossa (1077)** and the rival-king elections that followed let princes act as arbiters of kingship. The **Concordat of Worms (1122)** separated spiritual investiture (by the Church) from temporal investiture (by the king). Long-term: the king lost sacral authority over the church; bishops became princes in their own right.
- **Staufer era (1138–1254):** Frederick I "Barbarossa" and Frederick II tried to build power in Italy; in Germany, Frederick II's privileges to ecclesiastical (1220) and secular (1231/32) princes recognised their territorial lordship.
- **"Interregnum" (1250s–1273) and election of Rudolf of Habsburg (1273):** Princes preferred weak kings. The electoral college crystallised in the later 13th century (Scales).
- **Imperial reform (1495):** The Diet of Worms proclaimed the **Perpetual Public Peace** (ending legal feud) and founded the **Imperial Chamber Court** (*Reichskammergericht*); the Empire was later organised into **Imperial Circles** (*Reichskreise*, 10 by 1512).

## 3. Society

See `03_medieval_society.md` and `04_cities_church_feudalism.md`. The political key: society was organised in **estates** (*Stände*) — those who pray, fight and work — and in overlapping lordships. A person's legal status depended on birth, lord, place (town or country) and religion.

## 4. Everyday Life

For the ~90% who were peasants, the Empire was nearly invisible; what mattered were the village, the lord (lay or clerical), the parish and the market town. For townspeople, the emperor was a source of privileges and protection (imperial cities answered to him directly). For Jews, the emperor was their formal protector from the 13th century (as *servi camerae*, "servants of the chamber") — a status that meant taxes and, in crises, vulnerability, since emperors pawned these rights to princes and towns.

## 5. Economy

Kingship's material basis shrank over time as royal lands were granted away. Princes built revenue from lordship, tolls, mines (silver in Saxony, Tyrol, Bohemia) and courts. See `03_medieval_society.md`.

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

- **Imperial universalism:** The emperor as protector of Christendom and heir of Rome; "Holy" (*sacrum*) added to "Empire" in the 12th century; "of the German Nation" (*Nationis Germanicae*) appears in the 15th century (e.g., 1474, 1512 usage), signalling both a German core and the loss of wider claims.
- **Papal supremacy vs imperial autonomy:** A centuries-long argument generating pamphlets and treatises (the first "public debates").
- **"German" identity:** Scales argues that "a 'German people' seems first to have become visible to contemporaries in consequence of its rulers' imperial turn during the tenth century" — others called the king's subjects *Teutonici*, and papal polemic in the Investiture Controversy deliberately reduced the emperor to *rex Teutonicorum* (king of the Germans) to deny his universal claims. Stronger articulations of German "rights" came in 13th–14th-century treatises, and in the 15th century printing and humanism (rediscovering Tacitus) spread national sentiment among the educated. Scales: "the princes became a principal repository and measure of Germanness itself."

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

- Conflicts with the papacy.
- The princes' growing territorial power and their monopoly on royal elections.
- Costly Italian commitments.
- Economic growth creating towns that sought privileges.
- In the 15th century: Hussite wars, Ottoman threat, and the demand for peace and law (*Landfrieden*) that produced the 1495 reforms.

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE (c. 1000):** Sacral, itinerant kingship ruling through bishops and dukes.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Investiture Controversy; princely territorialisation; elective monarchy; Italian entanglements.
↓
**TRANSITION (13th–15th c.):** Electoral college; Golden Bull; Habsburg dynastic dominance; 1495 imperial reform.
↓
**AFTER (c. 1500):** A legal-political federation of territories with a Diet, courts and circles — about to be split by the Reformation.

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

Little directly from imperial politics; much from the growth of **territorial lordship** (more regular dues, courts and administration) and from 1495's ban on feud (in principle reducing aristocratic violence).

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

Elective, consensual kingship; regional diversity; the combination of religious and political authority; the importance of law and privilege rather than force alone.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| "Neither holy, nor Roman, nor an empire" (Voltaire) was a fair summary | A witty jibe by an outsider; the Empire functioned as a legal and political order for centuries. |
| The Empire was a failed German nation-state | It was never intended to be a nation-state; judging it by that standard is anachronistic (Whaley & Scales). |
| Medieval kings were absolute rulers | They depended on consensus with princes and bishops. |
| Canossa was a simple humiliation | Henry IV's penance was also a shrewd move that lifted his excommunication; its symbolic meaning grew in 19th-c. *Kulturkampf* rhetoric ("We are not going to Canossa" — Bismarck, 1872). |
| Barbarossa was a national hero | A 19th-century myth (the Kyffhäuser legend); he spent much of his reign fighting in Italy. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **Decline narrative vs revaluation:** 19th- and early-20th-century German (and Austrian) historians "despised it for not being a nation state and blamed it for delaying the development of the Germans" (Whaley & Scales). A.J.P. Taylor (1945) folded it into a long road to Nazism. After 1945, Catholic scholars (Konrad Repgen, Heinrich Lutz, Karl Otmar von Aretin) and then a broad consensus reinterpreted it as a functioning, proto-federal, supranational order — sometimes compared to the EU (a comparison itself criticised as presentist).

  *In plain words:* Was the Holy Roman Empire a failure? Older German historians (1800s–early 1900s) scorned it for not being a nation-state and blamed it for holding Germans back; A.J.P. Taylor linked it to a long road to Nazism. After 1945, Catholic scholars and then most historians saw a working, federal-like order spanning many peoples. Some compare it to the EU — criticised as reading today into the past.
- **"Imperial church system":** Reuter's critique (1982) vs older view (Leo Santifaller).

  *In plain words:* Did the Ottonian and Salian kings (10th–11th centuries) deliberately build a 'system' of ruling through bishops and abbots they appointed and endowed with land and royal rights? An older view, associated with Leo Santifaller, said yes. Timothy Reuter (1982) replied that this was ordinary practice across Latin Europe, which German historians had turned into a neat system after the fact.
- **Ritual and symbolic communication:** Gerd Althoff and Barbara Stollberg-Rilinger argue that rituals (coronations, submissions, seating orders) *were* the constitution — power was performed and negotiated rather than administered.

  *In plain words:* How did power actually work in the medieval Empire without a written constitution? Gerd Althoff and Barbara Stollberg-Rilinger argue that public rituals — coronations, ceremonies of submission, who sat where — were themselves the constitution. Power, in their view, was acted out and negotiated in public, not run through administration.

### If you remember only 5 things

1. The Empire fused a German kingship with a Roman-Christian imperial title — a universal claim, not a national one.
2. Kings were elected by princes; by 1356 seven electors held that right.
3. The quarrel with the papacy (1075–1122) broke the king's sacred control of the Church and made princes kingmakers.
4. Real power moved to territorial lords; the emperor was strong only through his own family lands (usually Habsburg).
5. Historians no longer call this a "failure"; it was a different, long-lived kind of political order.
