## 1. Period in One Paragraph

Medieval German society was a society of **orders and lordships**. Most people — perhaps 85–90% — were peasants, living in villages under a lord who could be a noble, a bishop, an abbey or a town. Between roughly 1000 and 1300 the population grew strongly, forests were cleared, and new villages and hundreds of towns were founded, including far to the east of the Elbe (the *Ostsiedlung*). Peasants' legal status varied enormously: some were serfs tied to the lord's person, others were free tenants with inheritable rights. Towns became islands of different law, where citizens governed themselves through councils and guilds. The Church was everywhere — as landlord, as parish, as school, as source of meaning. Around 1348–1350 the Black Death killed perhaps a third of the population, reshaping labour, land and religious anxiety, and unleashing the destruction of many Jewish communities. By 1500, German society was more commercial, more literate and more territorially governed — but still sharply unequal and organised by birth and estate.

## 2. The Political Structure — local level

The state that mattered for most people was **lordship** (*Herrschaft*):

- **Manorial lordship (*Grundherrschaft*):** Rights over land and the people who worked it — dues in grain, money, chickens, eggs; labour services; fees on inheritance (*Besthaupt*, the best head of cattle) and marriage.
- **Jurisdictional lordship (*Gerichtsherrschaft*):** The right to hold courts, often held separately by a different lord.
- **Personal lordship (*Leibherrschaft*):** Rights over a person's body — the core of serfdom (*Leibeigenschaft*).
- **Territorial lordship (*Landesherrschaft*):** Emerging from the 13th century: princes combined these rights with tolls, mints, forests, fortresses and courts into territories.

**Checks on lords:** Custom (written down in village "wisdoms," *Weistümer*), village communities (*Gemeinden*) that negotiated with lords, the possibility of flight to towns, and occasionally appeal to higher courts.

**Who was excluded:** Peasants had no voice in imperial or princely politics; women were under guardianship in most legal matters; Jews were outside Christian society's legal order and dependent on privileges.

### Regime Matrix — a village under lordship (c. 1100–1500)

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Who formally ruled? | The lord or lords: manorial, jurisdictional and personal lordship could each belong to a different lord (a noble, a monastery, a bishop); above them the territorial prince and, distantly, the emperor. |
| 2 | Who actually held power? | The lord's officials (bailiffs, stewards) and, in everyday matters, the village community (*Gemeinde*) and its better-off farmers. |
| 3 | How was power acquired? | Inheritance, grant as a fief, purchase or pledge of lordship rights. |
| 4 | How was power maintained? | Custom, the lord's court, records of dues, the Church's support and, if needed, force. |
| 5 | Who could challenge the government? | Village communities negotiating or refusing dues; peasants fleeing to towns; rival lords; peasant conspiracies and revolts (*Bundschuh* from 1493). |
| 6 | How was money obtained? | Dues in kind and money, labour services, fees on inheritance (*Besthaupt*) and marriage, court fines; tithes went to the Church. |
| 7 | How were decisions enforced? | The lord's court (*Gerichtsherrschaft*), bailiffs, fines, imprisonment. |
| 8 | How were ordinary people represented? | Not at all above the village; the *Gemeinde* and customary law recorded in *Weistümer* gave peasants a collective voice. |
| 9 | Rights? | Customary rights written down in *Weistümer*; serfs (*Leibeigene*) owed fees and needed permission for marriage and for leaving the lordship. |
| 10 | Opponents? | Fines, expulsion, harsh punishment of rebels. |
| 11 | Institutions connecting state to society | Parish church, village community, manor court, market. |
| 12 | Ordinary person's view | The lord's demands, the priest's tithe, the weather and the harvest. |

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** In legal theory, lordship was a hierarchy of rights flowing down from king and princes to lords, with peasants as dependants who had no political voice. In practice, lords depended on custom (*Weistümer*) and on bargaining with organised village communities; dues were often fixed by custom, peasants could flee to towns, and the balance shifted with circumstances — for example after the Black Death made labour scarce (see §7–8).

## 3. Society

| Group | Situation |
|---|---|
| **High nobility / princes** | Dukes, counts, great churchmen; territorial rulers. |
| **Lower nobility / knights** | Many originated as unfree administrators and warriors (*ministeriales*) who rose into the nobility in the 12th–13th c. — a distinctly German phenomenon. |
| **Clergy** | From prince-bishops to poor village priests; monasteries as major landowners. |
| **Townspeople** | Merchants, craftsmen, labourers, servants; sharply stratified. |
| **Peasants** | Majority; from wealthy full-holding farmers (*Vollbauern*) to cottagers and landless labourers. |
| **Jews** | Urban communities (Speyer, Worms, Mainz — the "ShUM" communities — among the most important in Europe). |
| **Marginal groups** | Beggars, "dishonourable" trades (executioners, skinners), travelling people. |

**Religion:** Latin Christianity structured time, morality and community. Parish churches, confession, saints' cults, pilgrimages, confraternities. The Church was also the largest landowner and a lender.

**Women:** Worked alongside men in peasant households and in urban crafts (some guilds admitted widows or women's work, e.g., silk-making in Cologne); religious women's communities (convents; Beguines) offered alternatives to marriage. Legal guardianship by fathers/husbands was the norm.

**Mobility:** Limited but real — through the church, through service (ministeriales), through migration to towns and eastern settlements. The legal maxim that a serf who lived in a town for "a year and a day" became free existed in many town laws, though the catchy phrase *"Stadtluft macht frei"* ("town air makes free") was coined by 19th-century legal historians, and in practice lords often reclaimed runaways.

## 4. Everyday Life

- **Work:** Three-field agriculture (winter grain, summer grain, fallow) spread from the 9th–12th centuries; village communities regulated common fields and pastures.
- **Family:** Nuclear households within village networks; marriage often tied to acquiring a holding; high child mortality.
- **Food and housing:** Grain-based diet (bread, porridge, beer), some meat; timber-framed houses shared with animals in many regions.
- **Religion:** Sunday mass, feast days (some 40–60 holy days plus Sundays), confession at Easter, fear of purgatory, pilgrimage.
- **State contact:** The lord's steward, the court, the tithe collector, the priest.
- **Violence:** Noble feud was legal until 1495; banditry; wars between princes and cities.

## 5. Economy

- **Growth (c. 1000–1300):** Population rose (estimates vary widely; perhaps from ~4 million to ~12–14 million in the German lands — **UNCERTAIN**); clearing of forest; new villages and towns; money economy spread; labour services were increasingly commuted to money rents.
- **Ostsiedlung (12th–14th c.):** Lords (German and Slavic princes alike, e.g., in Silesia and Pomerania) invited settlers — from Flanders, Saxony, Franconia — with favourable terms: inheritable tenure, fixed rents, village self-government under German town and village law. Many new towns received Magdeburg or Lübeck law.
- **Crisis (14th c.):** Famine (1315–17), Black Death (1348–50) and later plagues; many villages abandoned (*Wüstungen*); labour scarcity improved some peasants' bargaining power in the west, while in the east lords would later tighten control (see `early_modern/04_prussia_and_austria.md`).
- **Mining and trade:** Silver mining (Goslar, Freiberg), salt, cloth; the Hanse in the north; south German merchant houses (Fugger, Welser in Augsburg) by 1500.

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

- **The three orders** (those who pray, fight, work) — an elite clerical model of a divinely ordered society.
- **Custom and "good old law":** Peasants and towns appealed to ancient custom against innovation by lords.
- **Religious anti-Judaism:** Accusations of host desecration, ritual murder and well-poisoning justified massacres.
- **Late-medieval reform and critique:** Calls for church reform, anti-clericalism, lay piety; the *Gravamina of the German Nation* (grievances against papal taxation) from the mid-15th century.

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

Demographic growth and collapse; commercialisation; the rise of territorial princes with fiscal demands; clerical wealth and abuses; printing (from c. 1450, Mainz); humanism.

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE (c. 1000):** Manorial estates, few towns, sacral kingship.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Growth → towns and money; plague → labour scarcity; territorial princes → taxes; church wealth → anticlericalism.
↓
**TRANSITION:** 15th c. — imperial reform, printing, humanism, peasant unrest (*Bundschuh* conspiracies from 1493).
↓
**AFTER:** An urbanising, literate, litigious society about to experience religious revolution (see `early_modern/01_reformation.md`).

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

Over five centuries: from labour services on a demesne to money rents; from scattered farms to nucleated villages with common fields; more markets and towns; for many in the east, new settlements with good terms (which would erode later). The Black Death brought devastation and, for survivors, often better wages and land.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

Birth largely determined status; the harvest determined survival; the Church structured life; lordship, not a distant state, governed daily life.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| All medieval peasants were serfs | Status varied greatly by region and period; many were free tenants. |
| "Feudalism" was a neat pyramid | Lordship was overlapping and fragmented; "feudal pyramid" is a textbook simplification. |
| The Ostsiedlung was a German "Drang nach Osten" | A 19th–20th-c. nationalist (and Nazi) framing; settlement was often invited by Slavic rulers and produced mixed societies. |
| Medieval people were uniformly pious and obedient | Anticlericalism, heresy, revolts and litigation were common. |
| "Stadtluft macht frei" is a medieval saying | It is a 19th-c. formulation of a variable medieval legal rule. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **"Feudalism" as a concept:** Susan Reynolds (*Fiefs and Vassals*, 1994) argued that the classic model distorts the evidence; German historians often prefer *Lehnswesen* (fief system) and *Grundherrschaft* as separate, more precise concepts.
- **Ostsiedlung:** Nationalist German and Polish historiographies once framed it as either civilising mission or aggression; post-1970s research (e.g., the German–Polish textbook commission; Jan Piskorski) treats it as part of a Europe-wide colonisation and legal-transfer movement.
- **Peasant condition:** Older "misery" narratives vs newer work on village agency and negotiation (Peter Blickle's "communalism" thesis).

## 13. Confidence Assessment

| Finding | Confidence | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Overwhelming majority were rural peasants under lordship | HIGH | Universal agreement |

### If you remember only 5 things

1. Most people were peasants, and "the state" for them meant their lord, their village and their priest.
2. Lordship was layered — land, courts, persons and territory could each belong to different lords.
3. Towns created islands of self-government and different law, attracting migrants.
4. Growth, eastern settlement and then plague reshaped who worked the land and on what terms.
5. Religion framed everything — including the recurring violence against Jewish communities.
