## 1. Period in One Paragraph

After Rome's western empire dissolved, the **Franks** built the most successful post-Roman kingdom. Under the Merovingian kings (c. 480–751) and then the **Carolingians** (751–911), they conquered almost all the lands that would later be "German": Alamannia, Thuringia, Bavaria, Frisia and — after thirty years of brutal war — Saxony. Charlemagne was crowned emperor in Rome in 800, reviving the idea of a western Christian empire. The Carolingians governed a huge, thinly populated realm through **personal bonds** (oaths, gifts of land), **counts** as local agents, **inspectors** sent from the court, and above all the **Church**: bishops and monasteries were the only institutions with literate staff and continuous records. When Charlemagne's heirs divided the empire (Verdun, 843), the eastern part — **East Francia** — became the core of what would later be the German kingdom. But no one yet thought of it as "Germany": it was the eastern Frankish kingdom, made of different peoples ruled by a Frankish dynasty.

## 2. The Political Structure

### Regime Matrix — Carolingian kingship

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Formally ruled | The king (from 800 also emperor), anointed by the Church. |
| 2 | Actually held power | The king *in cooperation with* great aristocratic families and bishops; in practice, local magnates. |
| 3 | Power acquired | Dynastic succession (751 the Carolingians deposed the Merovingians with papal approval); division among sons was normal. |
| 4 | Power maintained | Constant travel (itinerant court), assemblies of magnates, gift-giving and land grants, conquest and plunder, religious legitimacy. |
| 5 | Who could challenge | Royal sons and brothers; great families; conquered peoples (Saxon revolts). |
| 6 | Money | Royal estates (the *fisc*), plunder and tribute, tolls, military service owed by free men and later by vassals. |
| 7 | Enforcement | Counts (*comites*) in districts; royal envoys (*missi dominici*, usually a bishop and a count in pairs) inspecting; written decrees (*capitularies*). |
| 8 | Representation | None for ordinary people; free men attended local courts; magnates attended assemblies. |
| 9 | Rights | Legal status by group ("personal law": Frankish, Alamannic, Bavarian, Saxon codes); free vs unfree central. |
| 10 | Opponents | Blinded, tonsured and sent to monasteries, executed, or — in Saxony — massacred and deported. |
| 11 | Connecting institutions | Church (parishes, tithes), counts' courts, monasteries, oaths of loyalty sworn by all free men. |
| 12 | Ordinary person's view | A distant king; a nearer lord and a priest; tithes, labour services, and the obligation to fight or pay. |

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** Capitularies (royal decrees) such as the *Admonitio generalis* (789) prescribe an ordered Christian society under one ruler. In practice, their enforcement depended on whether local counts and bishops wanted to comply; historians now read them as *programmes and aspirations* as much as descriptions.

## 3. Society

- **Free and unfree:** The fundamental division. Many free peasants fell into dependence on lords and churches over the 8th–9th centuries, trading freedom for protection.
- **Aristocracy:** A relatively small "imperial aristocracy" of related families holding counties and abbeys across the realm.
- **Church:** Bishoprics (many founded or reorganised by Boniface in the 8th century, e.g., Würzburg, Eichstätt, Büraburg; Mainz as archbishopric) and great monasteries (Fulda, Lorsch, Reichenau, St. Gall) were landowners, schools, record-keepers and royal agents.
- **Women:** Aristocratic women could hold property and run abbeys (royal abbesses were powerful); peasant women worked in households and on manors, including in manorial textile workshops (*gynaecea*).
- **Jews:** Present in Rhineland towns as merchants; protected by royal privileges.

## 4. Everyday Life

Rural, local and precarious. On large estates organised as manors (*Villikation*), dependent peasants held a farm (*Hufe*, often ~10–15 ha) and owed labour on the lord's demesne — for unfree tenants typically up to three days a week — plus dues in kind. Christianisation reshaped the calendar (Sundays, feasts), burial (churchyards), marriage rules and tithes. For Saxons, conversion came by force: the *Capitulatio de partibus Saxoniae* (c. 782/785) threatened death for refusing baptism or practising pagan rites.

## 5. Economy

Almost entirely agrarian. Wealth = land + people to work it. Royal and ecclesiastical estates were the most organised units (estate surveys, *polyptychs*, survive for some abbeys). A silver penny coinage (Charlemagne's monetary reform) and long-distance trade (slaves, furs, wine, cloth) continued, but on a small scale.

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

- **Christian kingship:** The ruler as God's agent responsible for his people's salvation; anointing borrowed from the Old Testament.
- **Imperial revival (800):** Linked Frankish kingship to Rome and the papacy — a link that would define (and trouble) German kingship for centuries.
- **"Correction" (*correctio*):** Carolingian reform of church, script (Caroline minuscule), education and law — the "Carolingian Renaissance," carried by a tiny clerical elite.
- **"German" language:** The Latin word *theodiscus* (from the vernacular *þeudō*, "people") appears in the late 8th century for the vernacular language(s) east of the Romance-speaking zone — the root of *deutsch*. It referred to *language*, not to a nation. The **Strasbourg Oaths (842)** record oaths in early Romance and early German — evidence of a language boundary within the Frankish elite, not of two nations.

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

- Frankish partible inheritance → repeated divisions and civil wars.
- Viking, Magyar and Slavic raids in the 9th–10th centuries demanded local defence, which strengthened regional leaders (dukes).
- Great families built regional power (the "younger stem duchies": Saxony, Bavaria, Swabia, Franconia, Lotharingia).

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE:** A single Frankish empire ruled by one dynasty through counts, bishops and personal bonds.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Partible inheritance; external raids; regional aristocratic power.
↓
**TRANSITION (crisis):** Verdun 843 splits the empire; the East Frankish Carolingian line ends in 911; the dukes elect Conrad I (a Franconian) and then, in 919, **Henry I of Saxony**.
↓
**AFTER:** An East Frankish/German kingdom ruled by an **elected** Saxon dynasty (the Ottonians) that depends on consensus with dukes and on the Church.

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

Christianisation, tithes, the parish, and incorporation into manorial lordship — the slow, century-long shift from free farmer to dependent peasant in many regions. For Saxons: conquest, forced conversion, deportations.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

Local farming life; the importance of kin and lordship; the region-based identities (Saxons, Bavarians, Swabians/Alamanni, Thuringians, Franks) that survived into the medieval duchies and still echo in modern *Länder* names.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| Charlemagne was a "German" (or "French") king | He was a Frankish ruler of a multi-lingual empire; both nations later claimed him. |
| Verdun 843 "created Germany and France" | It divided an inheritance among brothers; national identities came much later. |
| Carolingian government was a bureaucracy | It was personal, itinerant and dependent on local elites; capitularies express intentions. |
| Christianisation was a peaceful spiritual process | In Saxony it was imposed by war, massacre (Verden, 782 — scale disputed) and law. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **How effective was Carolingian government?** Older views saw a proto-state; more recent work (e.g., Matthew Innes, Jennifer Davis, Rosamond McKitterick) emphasises negotiation, local elites and the role of writing as a tool of persuasion rather than command.
- **When does "German" history begin?** Historians no longer look for a founding date. Len Scales argues that a "German people" first became visible to contemporaries *as a consequence of* the Ottonians' imperial turn in the 10th century; others stress the 11th-century Investiture Contest (see next file). Carlrichard Brühl's influential thesis placed the emergence of "Germany and France" in the 11th century, as a slow process rather than an event.
- **The massacre of Verden (782):** the "4,500 Saxons" reported by the Royal Frankish Annals has been questioned (textual problems and propaganda purpose), though the violence of the conquest is not in doubt.

## 13. Confidence Assessment

### If you remember only 5 things

1. The Franks, not "Germans," united the future German lands — often by force.
2. Government meant a travelling king, loyal magnates and, above all, bishops and monasteries.
3. The link between emperor, pope and Rome (800) became a lasting feature — and burden — of German kingship.
4. "Deutsch" began as a word for a language, not a nation.
5. East Francia after 843 was the *seed* of a German kingdom, not its birth certificate.
