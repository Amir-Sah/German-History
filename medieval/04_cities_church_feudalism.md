## 1. Period in One Paragraph

Three institutions shaped medieval German life beyond the village: the **Church**, which was simultaneously a spiritual community, a landowner, a government (prince-bishops ruled territories) and a school system; **lordship and the fief system**, which bound nobles to one another and to the king through land-for-loyalty relationships; and the **towns**, which by 1500 numbered in the thousands (most very small) and included wealthy self-governing "imperial cities" (Nuremberg, Augsburg, Frankfurt, Cologne, Lübeck) answerable only to the emperor. Towns were where commerce, crafts, guilds, literacy and political experiments in self-government flourished — but they were also oligarchic, exclusionary and often violent toward Jews. This file explains how each of these institutions worked and how they interacted.

## 2. The Political Structure

### A. The Church as a political institution

- **Prince-bishops and prince-abbots:** Archbishops of Mainz, Cologne and Trier were electors; bishops of Würzburg, Bamberg, Münster, Salzburg and many others ruled territories as princes. Germany had more ecclesiastical principalities than any other part of Europe — a direct legacy of the Ottonian-Salian practice of endowing bishoprics with royal rights.
- **Cathedral chapters** (dominated by noble families) elected bishops, making church offices a career track for younger sons of the nobility.
- **Parishes and tithes** connected every village to the Church.
- **Church courts** had jurisdiction over clergy, marriage, wills and morals.

### B. Lordship and the fief system (*Lehnswesen*)

- A lord granted a fief (land or rights) to a vassal in return for loyalty, counsel and military service.
- In Germany, the *Heerschildordnung* ranked nobles by whom they could receive fiefs from — a hierarchy in theory. In practice, fiefs became hereditary, and princes treated them as property.
- Older legal history (Heinrich Mitteis) held that German kings were bound by a customary "obligation to re-grant" (*Leihezwang*) escheated princely fiefs, unlike French kings, who absorbed them. Werner Goez (1962) showed there was no such binding rule; kings *did* usually re-grant, but because of political pressure from princes, not law. **CONTESTED in its legal form; the practical outcome — territorial power staying with princes — is not in doubt.**

### C. Towns

| Type | Lord | Degree of autonomy |
|---|---|---|
| **Imperial cities** (*Reichsstädte*) | The emperor directly | Highest; seats at the Diet from the 15th c. |
| **Free cities** (*Freie Städte*) | Formerly under a bishop, gained de facto independence (e.g., Cologne, Mainz for a time) | High |
| **Territorial towns** | A prince or bishop | Variable; many had charters and councils |

**How towns governed themselves:**
- A **council** (*Rat*) of wealthy patrician families (merchants, landowners), later often shared with guild masters after 14th-century "guild struggles" (*Zunftkämpfe*).
- **Citizenship** (*Bürgerrecht*) was a privilege purchased or inherited; many inhabitants (servants, journeymen, the poor, Jews, clergy) were not citizens.
- **Town law** families: Magdeburg law and Lübeck law spread across central and eastern Europe to hundreds of towns.
- **Leagues:** Towns formed alliances for defence and trade — the Rhenish League (1254), the Swabian League, and above all the **Hanse**.

**The Hanse** (c. 12th–17th c.): Not a state, but a network of merchants and later of up to ~200 towns (Lübeck, Hamburg, Bremen, Cologne, Danzig, Riga…) with trading offices (*Kontore*) in London (the Steelyard), Bruges, Bergen and Novgorod. It negotiated privileges, organised blockades and even fought a war against Denmark (Peace of Stralsund, 1370). It had diets (*Hansetage*) but no army, no treasury and no fixed membership — an early example of power without statehood.

### Regime Matrix — An imperial city (e.g., Nuremberg c. 1450)

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Formally ruled | The emperor as overlord; the council as governing body. |
| 2 | Actually held power | A closed circle of patrician families (in Nuremberg, the guilds were excluded from council power after 1349). |
| 3 | Power acquired | Co-option within patrician families. |
| 4 | Power maintained | Control of offices, courts, militia, taxation, and guild regulation. |
| 5 | Who could challenge | Guilds (revolts), neighbouring princes, the emperor. |
| 6 | Money | Excise taxes, property taxes, tolls, loans. |
| 7 | Enforcement | Town watch, courts, militia, banishment. |
| 8 | Representation | Citizens via guilds (in guild-governed towns); non-citizens not at all. |
| 9 | Rights | Citizens' privileges: protection, trade rights, trial in town court. |
| 10 | Opponents | Banishment, execution, confiscation. |
| 11 | Connecting institutions | Guilds, parishes, confraternities, hospitals. |
| 12 | Ordinary person's view | Dense regulation of work, prices, dress and morals — and some security. |

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** In law, imperial cities answered only to the emperor, and the fief hierarchy (*Heerschildordnung*) ranked every noble below the king. In practice, the emperor was distant: cities were governed by a closed circle of patrician families (joined in some towns by guild masters after the 14th-century guild struggles), most inhabitants were not citizens, and security came from walls, money and leagues such as the Hanse. Fiefs, meanwhile, became hereditary, and princes treated them as property (see B above).

## 3. Society

**Guilds** (*Zünfte*, *Innungen*): Associations of masters in a craft that regulated entry, training (apprentice → journeyman → master), quality, prices and competition; they were also religious confraternities and militia units. They offered security to members and closed doors to outsiders, women (with exceptions), and the "dishonourable."

**Jews in towns:** Lived under imperial or episcopal protection, often in designated streets; paid special taxes; were pushed into money-lending by exclusion from guilds and Christian usury prohibitions. Massacres: **1096** (crusaders in Speyer, Worms, Mainz), the **Rintfleisch** (1298) and **Armleder** (1336–38) pogroms, and the **1348–50** Black Death persecutions, when hundreds of communities were destroyed, often with council complicity and debt cancellation as a motive. In the 15th century many cities expelled their Jews (e.g., Cologne 1424, Augsburg 1438, Nuremberg 1499). The ShUM sites of Speyer, Worms and Mainz became a UNESCO World Heritage site in 2021.

## 4. Everyday Life

Town life: dense, noisy, regulated. Most houses were timber; fire was a constant risk. Guild membership shaped identity; bells regulated time; sumptuary laws dictated dress by rank. Hospitals, alms and confraternities handled poverty. Literacy spread among merchants from the 13th century; German (rather than Latin) became the language of town records from the late 13th century.

## 5. Economy

Towns lived from markets, crafts and long-distance trade. The Hanse traded Baltic grain, timber, furs, fish and wax for Flemish cloth, English wool and French salt/wine. South German towns tied into Italian trade and, by 1500, into mining finance (the Fugger bankrolled Habsburg politics, including the 1519 imperial election).

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

- **Communal ideals:** "Common good" (*gemeiner Nutzen*) and "peace" as civic values.
- **Anticlericalism:** Resentment of clerical tax exemptions, church courts and wealth.
- **Lay piety:** Confraternities, preaching, devotional literature — fertile ground for the Reformation.

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

Guild struggles; princely attempts to subdue towns; the growing gap between rich patricians and poor townspeople; clerical privileges; printing (Mainz, c. 1450), which made towns the first centres of the Reformation.

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE:** A church-dominated, lordship-based world with self-governing urban islands.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Anticlericalism; princely territorial ambitions; urban social tension; printing.
↓
**TRANSITION:** The Reformation spreads first through towns (1520s) — councils decide on religion.
↓
**AFTER:** Urban and territorial churches under secular authority (see `early_modern/01_reformation.md`).

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

Town residents gained legal status, markets and some political voice (via guilds). But urban life also brought new forms of discipline, poverty and exclusion.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

The link between religious and political authority; corporate (group-based) rights rather than individual rights.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| Medieval towns were democracies | Oligarchies, sometimes broadened by guilds; most residents were not full citizens. |
| The Hanse was a German state or empire | A flexible network of merchants and towns without sovereignty. |
| Jewish money-lending was a cultural choice | It was a product of exclusion from other trades and Christian usury rules. |
| Prince-bishops were purely spiritual figures | They were territorial rulers, often from noble families, with armies and courts. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **Towns and "bourgeois" freedom:** 19th-century liberals saw medieval towns as seedbeds of German freedom; modern historians stress oligarchy and exclusion but also genuine traditions of self-government and legal culture.
- **The Hanse as "network" vs "organisation":** Recent scholarship (e.g., Stuart Jenks, Carsten Jahnke) emphasises informality and merchant networks over the older image of a quasi-state league.

### If you remember only 5 things

1. The Church was not just a religion — it was a government, landowner and career system for the nobility.
2. Fiefs became hereditary and princes kept them; kings could not absorb territories as French kings did.
3. Towns were self-governing but oligarchic; citizenship was a privilege, not a right.
4. The Hanse shows that economic power could be organised without a state.
5. Jewish communities lived under fragile "protection" that repeatedly failed in massacres and expulsions.
