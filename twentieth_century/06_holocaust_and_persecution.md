## 1. Period in One Paragraph

The Nazi regime murdered about six million European Jews — the Holocaust (Shoah) — alongside hundreds of thousands of Roma and Sinti, around 250,000–300,000 disabled people, some 3.3 million Soviet prisoners of war, millions of Polish and Soviet civilians, and others. Persecution of German Jews escalated in stages: legal exclusion (1933–35), expropriation and expulsion (1938–39), and — with the war against Poland and especially the invasion of the Soviet Union (1941) — systematic mass murder: shootings by mobile killing units and police battalions, then death camps using gas (Chełmno, Bełżec, Sobibór, Treblinka, Auschwitz-Birkenau, Majdanek). The genocide depended on thousands of perpetrators and the cooperation of ministries, railways, companies, the army, and local collaborators across Europe. Resistance within Germany existed across the political spectrum — communists, socialists, Christians, students, officers — but was small, isolated, and brutally suppressed; the regime was destroyed from outside. Historians agree on the facts and scale; they debate the decision-making process, the motives of perpetrators, and what "ordinary Germans" knew and did.

## 2. The Political Structure — persecution

### Stages

| Phase | Measures |
|---|---|
| **1933–1935: Exclusion** | Boycott (1 April 1933); Civil Service Law (dismissals); quotas in schools/universities; Sterilisation Law (July 1933); **Nuremberg Laws** (Sept 1935): Reich Citizenship Law and "Law for the Protection of German Blood and Honour." |
| **1936–1939: Expropriation and expulsion** | "Aryanisation" of businesses; registration of Jewish assets (April 1938); expulsion of Polish Jews (Oct 1938); **November pogrom** (9–10 Nov 1938): synagogues burned, shops wrecked, ~30,000 men to camps; "atonement" levy of 1 billion RM; Jews excluded from economic life, schools, cultural life. Emigration forced, but other countries restricted entry (Évian 1938). |
| **1939–1941: War, ghettos, first mass murder** | Poland: ghettos, mass shootings of Polish elites and Jews; "euthanasia" (T4) gassing of disabled people inside Germany (from 1939/40; ~70,000 by Aug 1941 in the gas centres, then continued decentrally); Jewish star (Sept 1941). |
| **1941–1942: Genocide** | Invasion of USSR (June 1941): Einsatzgruppen, Order Police battalions, SS brigades and Wehrmacht units shoot Jews — first men, from August 1941 women and children (Babi Yar, Sept 1941: ~33,771 in two days); deportations from Germany from Oct 1941; gas vans; Chełmno (Dec 1941); **Wannsee Conference (20 Jan 1942)** coordinates — USHMM: "The men at the table did not deliberate whether such a plan should be undertaken, but instead discussed the implementation of a policy decision that had already been made." Operation Reinhard camps (1942–43) kill ~1.7 million. |
| **1942–1945: Auschwitz and end-phase** | Auschwitz-Birkenau becomes the largest killing centre (~1.1 million victims, ~1 million of them Jews); deportations from across Europe (Hungary 1944: ~430,000 in weeks); death marches (1944–45). |

### Who did it? (Institutions)

- **SS–police complex:** RSHA (Heydrich, Kaltenbrunner), Gestapo (Eichmann's office for deportations), Einsatzgruppen, camp administrations (WVHA).
- **Order Police battalions** (e.g., Reserve Police Battalion 101 — middle-aged Hamburg men — shot or deported ~83,000 Jews).
- **Wehrmacht:** logistics, cooperation with and participation in shootings, starvation of Soviet POWs (~3.3 million died).
- **Civil administration:** Ministries (Interior, Justice, Finance — confiscation of property), Foreign Office (pressure on allies), Reich Railways, municipalities.
- **Business:** IG Farben (Monowitz), Topf & Söhne (crematoria), companies using camp labour.
- **Allies and collaborators:** Romania, Croatia, Slovakia, Hungary (1944), Vichy France, local police and auxiliaries in occupied territories.

### Regime Matrix — cross-reference

**Matrix:** see `twentieth_century/04_nazi_state.md` (the Nazi dictatorship, 1933–1945). This chapter works inside that system; its 12-question matrix is not repeated here.

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** Persecution was given legal form step by step — the Civil Service Law (1933), the Nuremberg Laws (1935), decrees on property and exclusion — so that discrimination looked like lawful administration. The mass murder itself rested on no published law: it was carried out through orders, secret coordination (Wannsee, January 1942) and the cooperation of ministries, police, army, railways and businesses. Law was used to exclude; genocide proceeded outside even Nazi law.

## 3. Society — victims

| Group | USHMM estimate |
|---|---|
| Jews | ~6 million |
| Soviet POWs | ~3.3 million |
| Non-Jewish Polish civilians | ~1.8 million |
| Serb civilians | >310,000 |
| People with disabilities | 250,000–300,000 (≥10,000 children) |
| Roma | at least 250,000, possibly up to 500,000 |
| Jehovah's Witnesses | ~1,700 |
| German political opponents, "asocials" | tens of thousands |
| Gay men | hundreds, possibly thousands |

Of ~500,000 Jews in Germany in 1933, about half emigrated; ~165,000–180,000 German Jews were murdered (estimates).

## 4. Everyday Life — perpetrators, bystanders, helpers, resisters

- **Perpetrators:** Ranged from ideologues (SS officers) to "ordinary men." Christopher Browning's study of Police Battalion 101 found that men were explicitly offered the chance not to shoot; only a minority (~10–20%) refused or evaded, and none were seriously punished.
- **Bystanders / beneficiaries:** Neighbours bought Jewish property at auctions; firms took over businesses; apartments were reallocated.
- **Helpers:** Some thousands of Germans hid Jews ("silent heroes"; ~1,700 Jews survived hidden in Berlin — estimates); Yad Vashem recognises ~650 Germans as Righteous Among the Nations (number changes over time).
- **Resistance (see §6).**

## 5. Economy

Robbery was systematic: property, bank accounts, insurance, furniture, art; forced labour in ghettos and camps; profits for companies; the state treasury gained from confiscations. Post-war restitution was partial and slow (Federal Compensation Law 1953/56; Luxembourg Agreement with Israel 1952; forced-labour foundation EVZ only in 2000).

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

- **Racial antisemitism** — Jews as a biological and "world-conspiratorial" enemy; the regime linked Jews to Bolshevism and plutocracy.
- **"Racial hygiene"/eugenics** — widely accepted in medicine and science before 1933.
- **War of annihilation** in the East — Slavs as "subhuman," Lebensraum colonisation (Generalplan Ost; Hunger Plan).

### Resistance

| Group | Examples | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **Communists** | Underground cells, leaflets (*Rote Fahne* 1933–35); ~15,000 KPD functionaries detained by summer 1933 (LeMO) | Earliest and largest in numbers; decimated early |
| **Social Democrats** | Underground networks; Sopade reports from exile | Mostly waiting and information-gathering |
| **Churches** | Confessing Church (Pfarrernotbund, ~7,000 pastors) defended church autonomy; Bishop von Galen's 1941 sermons against "euthanasia" (programme's gassing phase halted Aug 1941, murders continued); Dietrich Bonhoeffer | Institutional self-defence; few spoke for Jews (exceptions: Bernhard Lichtenberg) |
| **Individuals** | Georg Elser's bomb (Munich, 8 Nov 1939) — a carpenter acting alone | Came closest early; long neglected in memory |
| **Youth/students** | White Rose (Munich, 1942–43: Hans and Sophie Scholl); Edelweiss Pirates | Moral protest; executed |
| **Red Orchestra** (*Rote Kapelle*) | Berlin networks with Soviet contacts (~150 people; LeMO: 130 arrested, 49 executed) | Long denigrated in West Germany as "spies" |
| **Military-conservative** | 1938 plot; **20 July 1944** (Stauffenberg); Kreisau Circle (Moltke, Yorck); Goerdeler circle | Many had supported early regime; motives mixed (national, moral, religious); ~200 executed after 20 July |
| **Jewish resistance** | Baum group (Berlin, 1942) | Isolated |
| **Everyday nonconformity** | Swing Youth; refusing the Hitler salute; listening to BBC | *Resistenz* rather than resistance |

**Rosenstraße protest (1943):** Non-Jewish wives protested in Berlin for the release of their Jewish husbands; the men were released — whether because of the protest is debated.

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

Only military defeat ended the genocide. Internal resistance failed to topple the regime.

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE (1933):** Jews legally equal citizens in a democracy.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Racial ideology in power; competition for radical solutions; war and conquest.
↓
**TRANSITION (escalation):** Exclusion → expropriation → expulsion → mass murder (1941–45).
↓
**AFTER (1945–):** Liberation of camps; trials (Nuremberg 1945–49; Ulm Einsatzgruppen trial 1958; Frankfurt Auschwitz trials 1963–65); long delays in memory, compensation and prosecution in West Germany; state "antifascism" in the GDR with little focus on Jewish victims.

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

For Jews: destruction of a community of centuries. For non-Jewish Germans: participation in, benefit from, or knowledge of crimes that would shape German identity permanently after 1945.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

Many perpetrators returned to civilian careers after 1945; many were never prosecuted (see `08_occupation_and_denazification.md`).

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| The Holocaust was decided at Wannsee | Wannsee coordinated a decision already made; mass shootings were under way since mid-1941. |
| Perpetrators were forced to kill | Evidence shows refusals were possible with little punishment (Browning). |
| The Wehrmacht was "clean" | Army units participated extensively in crimes against Jews, POWs and civilians (Wehrmacht exhibition, 1995–99/2001). |
| Most Germans resisted inwardly | Resistance was rare; conformity and participation were widespread. |
| Resistance was only the 20 July officers | Communists, socialists, Christians, students and individuals resisted — often earlier and at greater cost. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **Intentionalism vs functionalism:** Did Hitler plan the Holocaust from the start (Jäckel, Dawidowicz) or did it result from cumulative radicalisation of competing agencies (Broszat, Mommsen)? **Synthesis:** Hitler's intentions and authority were essential; the decision process was incremental, with key escalation points in summer–autumn 1941 and December 1941 (Browning, *The Origins of the Final Solution*, 2004; Longerich; Christian Gerlach on December 1941). Exact timing of the "decision" is **CONTESTED**.
- **Perpetrator motives:** Daniel Goldhagen (*Hitler's Willing Executioners*, 1996): an "eliminationist antisemitism" pervading German culture made ordinary Germans willing killers. Christopher Browning (*Ordinary Men*, 1992): situational factors — conformity, careerism, deference, peer pressure, wartime brutalisation — turned ordinary men into killers; antisemitism mattered but was not sufficient. Most historians side closer to Browning while acknowledging ideology's role (USHMM 1996 symposium).
- **Knowledge:** Longerich; Bajohr/Pohl ("open secret").
- **Wehrmacht crimes:** The Hamburg Institute's exhibition (1995–99) shattered the "clean Wehrmacht" myth; photo misattributions led to a revised exhibition (2001) with the core findings confirmed.
- **Resistance memory:** West Germany long celebrated only 20 July (and treated communists as traitors); the GDR celebrated communist resistance. Both instrumentalised.
- **Holocaust singularity vs colonial comparison ("Historikerstreit 2.0," 2021–22).** See `nineteenth_century/08_colonialism.md`.

### If you remember only 5 things

1. Persecution escalated in stages — exclusion, robbery, expulsion, then from 1941 industrialised mass murder.
2. The genocide required the whole state: ministries, railways, army, police, companies — not only the SS.
3. Ordinary men could refuse to kill; most did not.
4. Resistance came from many directions but was small, isolated and crushed.
5. Historians agree on what happened and its scale; they debate exactly how decisions were made and why perpetrators acted.
