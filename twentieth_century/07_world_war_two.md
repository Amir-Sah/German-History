## 1. Period in One Paragraph

The Second World War in Europe was Germany's war of choice. Rearmament from 1933, the remilitarisation of the Rhineland (1936), the annexation of Austria and the Sudetenland (1938) and the destruction of Czechoslovakia (March 1939) were steps toward Hitler's goal of conquering "living space" in the East. After the Nazi-Soviet pact, Germany invaded Poland (1 September 1939); Britain and France declared war. Rapid victories (Poland 1939, Norway/Denmark, the Low Countries and France 1940) made Hitler overwhelmingly popular at home. The invasion of the Soviet Union (June 1941) was conceived as a war of annihilation: to destroy "Judeo-Bolshevism," starve millions, and colonise the land. It failed before Moscow (December 1941), the same month Hitler declared war on the United States. From Stalingrad (1942–43) the tide turned. The regime radicalised — "total war," forced labour on a vast scale, the Holocaust, terror against Germans themselves — and fought to the end. Germany surrendered unconditionally on 8 May 1945, with its cities destroyed, millions dead, and the state dissolved.

## 2. The Political Structure

### Regime Matrix (wartime changes)

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Formally ruled | Hitler as Führer and Supreme Commander (from Dec 1941 also Commander-in-Chief of the Army). |
| 2 | Actually held power | Hitler + an inner circle (Bormann as party chief and gatekeeper; Himmler — SS, police, from 1943 Interior Minister, 1944 Replacement Army; Goebbels — "total war" plenipotentiary 1944; Speer — armaments 1942–45); the Wehrmacht High Command (OKW) as Hitler's executive staff. |
| 3 | Acquired | Continuity from pre-war; the army's oath. |
| 4 | Maintained | Victories (to 1941), propaganda, plunder-supported rations, and increasing terror. |
| 5 | Who could challenge | Military conspirators (20 July 1944 failed). |
| 6 | Money | Taxes and borrowing (~70% of war costs from German sources — Tooze), plus occupation levies, plunder, forced labour and Holocaust-related robbery. |
| 7 | Enforcement | SS, Gestapo, military courts (tens of thousands of death sentences against Wehrmacht soldiers; ~15,000–20,000 executed), People's Court, flying courts-martial in 1945. |
| 8 | Representation | None. |
| 9 | Rights | None; "defeatism" punishable by death. |
| 10 | Opponents | Execution; after 20 July, clan liability (*Sippenhaft*). |
| 11 | Connecting institutions | Party (air-raid protection, evacuation, rationing), Wehrmacht, Volkssturm (1944), Hitler Youth (flak helpers). |
| 12 | Ordinary person's view | Early pride; then rationing, bombing, death notices, forced labourers everywhere, fear of defeat and of Soviet revenge. |

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** Formally, Hitler combined the offices of head of state, head of government, party leader and supreme commander, and the occupied territories were given legal statuses — annexation, civil administration, military administration or allied governments. In practice, in occupied Europe, Germany ran a patchwork: annexation (western Poland, Alsace), civil administration (General Government in Poland; Reich Commissariats in Ukraine and Ostland — sites of mass murder and colonial exploitation), military administration (France, Belgium), and allied/puppet regimes. The **East** was governed as a colonial space of plunder and murder; the **West** under comparatively "correct" occupation, which still deported Jews and conscripted labour.

## 3. Society

- **Soldiers:** ~17–18 million men served in the Wehrmacht; ~5.3 million died (Rüdiger Overmans's estimate).
- **Forced labourers:** ~13 million foreigners passed through the Reich (civilians, POWs, camp inmates); racial hierarchy: Western Europeans better treated; Poles and Soviet "Ostarbeiter" branded and brutalised.
- **Women:** Ran households and farms under rationing and bombing; employment rose less than in Britain; some served as Wehrmacht auxiliaries and camp guards.
- **Civilians under bombing:** Allied strategic bombing from 1942 destroyed most large cities (Hamburg 1943: ~35,000 dead; Dresden Feb 1945: ~25,000 dead, per the 2010 historians' commission).
- **Refugees:** From late 1944, millions fled the Red Army from East Prussia, Silesia, Pomerania — the beginning of flight and expulsion.

## 4. Everyday Life

1939–41: rationing but adequate supplies, victory celebrations, soldiers' leave with plundered goods from France. 1942–45: bombing, evacuation, black markets, shortages, intensified propaganda, fear of denunciation for defeatism, the deaths of husbands and sons (~5 million German military dead), and — for millions of soldiers — direct experience or knowledge of mass crimes in the East (visible in letters and photographs). 1945: chaos, flight, mass rapes by Soviet soldiers (estimates range from hundreds of thousands to ~2 million — **UNCERTAIN**), end-phase crimes by Germans against prisoners, forced labourers and "defeatists."

## 5. Economy

- **Blitzkrieg economy myth:** Older view (Alan Milward) that Germany planned short wars and limited mobilisation; Tooze and Richard Overy show heavy mobilisation from the start constrained by resource shortages (oil, food, foreign exchange).
- **Speer's "armaments miracle" (1942–44):** Output tripled — largely due to earlier investments, rationalisation and forced labour; Speer's post-war self-portrait as apolitical technocrat is now discredited.
- **Plunder:** Occupation costs, requisitions, gold, art, food (the Hunger Plan for the USSR envisaged the deaths of "tens of millions" to feed Germany and the army).

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

- War as racial struggle; Lebensraum; anti-Bolshevism; antisemitism fused into a crusade narrative.
- In the West, propaganda stressed defence against "plutocracies"; late-war propaganda stressed fear of Bolshevik revenge and "wonder weapons."
- **What soldiers believed:** Studies of letters and secret British recordings of POW conversations (Sönke Neitzel & Harald Welzer, *Soldaten*, 2011) show a "frame of reference" in which violence was normal; ideological commitment varied, but many shared racist views of Soviet people and Jews.

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

Military defeat on all fronts; Allied bombing; resource exhaustion; the Soviet advance; D-Day (June 1944).

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE (1939):** Nazi dictatorship at peak popularity, rearmed.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Overextension; two-front war; US entry; resource limits.
↓
**TRANSITION (crisis and end, 1943–45):**
- Crisis: Stalingrad (Feb 1943); total war; 20 July 1944; Allied invasion; Soviet offensive.
- End: Hitler's suicide (30 April 1945); unconditional surrender (7–8 May 1945; 9 May in Moscow).
↓
**AFTER:** Allied occupation and dissolution of German state sovereignty (see `twentieth_century/08_occupation_and_denazification.md`).

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

Nearly every family lost someone; cities in ruins; millions homeless or refugees; the end of German rule in the East; a society of women, children and old people in 1945 ("rubble women" — though clearing was often organised forced labour and later glorified).

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

The Nazi regime held until the end — no 1918-style revolution; terror and loyalty coexisted.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| Hitler's generals opposed his war and crimes | Most generals embraced the war and the war of annihilation; only a minority conspired, late. |
| Germany fought a "Blitzkrieg" economy until 1942 | Mobilisation was extensive from the start; limits were resource-based. |
| Dresden deaths numbered hundreds of thousands | Nazi and later far-right propaganda inflated figures; the Dresden historians' commission (2010) found up to 25,000. |
| The Wehrmacht fought a "normal" war in the East | It was an ideological war of annihilation with massive crimes. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **Origins of WWII:** A.J.P. Taylor (1961) argued Hitler was an opportunist like other statesmen; overwhelmingly rejected — Hitler's expansionist aims are well documented (e.g., Hossbach memorandum 1937). Debate continues on economic pressures pushing to war in 1939 (Tim Mason's "flight into war" thesis — contested).

  *In plain words:* Did Hitler plan the Second World War? A.J.P. Taylor (1961) called him an opportunist like other statesmen. This is overwhelmingly rejected: sources such as the 1937 Hossbach memorandum (notes of a meeting where Hitler set out expansion plans) document his aims. Still debated is Tim Mason's contested thesis that economic pressures pushed the regime into a 'flight into war' in 1939.
- **Wehrmacht and crimes:** Consensus since the 1990s that participation was extensive; debate on proportion of soldiers directly involved.

  *In plain words:* How many ordinary German soldiers took part in war crimes? Since the 1990s historians agree that the army's (Wehrmacht's) participation was extensive. What is still argued over is what share of soldiers were directly involved.
- **Germans as victims** (bombing, expulsion, rape): since the 2000s (W. G. Sebald; Jörg Friedrich's *Der Brand*, 2002) a debate about whether acknowledging German suffering relativises German guilt; most historians argue both must be told, in context.

  *In plain words:* Can Germans' own suffering (bombed cities, expulsion from their homes, mass rape) be acknowledged without playing down German guilt? Since the 2000s, works by W. G. Sebald and Jörg Friedrich's 'Der Brand' ('The Fire', 2002) have raised this question. Most historians argue both stories must be told, with German suffering placed in context.

### If you remember only 5 things

1. Germany launched the war deliberately to conquer living space in the East.
2. The war in the East was a war of annihilation, inseparable from the Holocaust.
3. The home front was sustained by plunder and forced labour — and then shattered by bombing and defeat.
4. The regime held to the end through loyalty, fear and terror; there was no revolution as in 1918.
5. By 1945 the German state had ceased to exist as a sovereign actor.
