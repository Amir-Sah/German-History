## 1. Period in One Paragraph

For most non-Jewish Germans who were not political opponents, daily life under Nazism before 1939 felt, in many respects, normal — and for many it felt better: jobs returned, streets were "orderly," the regime offered cheap holidays, radio sets and national pride after years of crisis. This normality was built on exclusion and fear. Everyone was classified by "race"; Jews were progressively stripped of rights, property and livelihoods in full public view; political opponents disappeared into camps; people learned to watch what they said. The regime penetrated everyday life through mass organisations for youth, women, workers and leisure, and through neighbours who could denounce. Historians debate whether support rested mainly on consent (enthusiasm, material benefits, shared prejudices) or coercion (terror, fear), and whether the "people's community" (*Volksgemeinschaft*) was a reality or a propaganda fiction. The best answer: it was a practice — of inclusion for some and brutal exclusion of others — that ordinary people participated in, with varying degrees of belief, opportunism, conformity and fear.

## 2. The Political Structure — as experienced

- **Block wardens (*Blockleiter*):** Party representatives for every ~40–60 households — collected donations, distributed information, watched and reported.
- **Mass organisations:** Hitler Youth (compulsory from 1936/1939), League of German Girls (BDM), German Labour Front (DAF, ~25 million members), Strength Through Joy (KdF — holidays, concerts, cruises), NS People's Welfare (NSV — Winter Relief collections), NS-Frauenschaft, Reich Labour Service (RAD, compulsory 1935).
- **Plebiscites and rallies:** Ritualised assent.
- **Police and denunciation:** The small Gestapo (~7,000 employees in 1937) drew on citizens' tips — about neighbours' remarks, listening to foreign radio, relationships with Jews or forced labourers; motives ranged from conviction to personal grudges (Robert Gellately). How central denunciation was is debated: Evans points out that in Düsseldorf only about a quarter of Gestapo investigations started with denunciations from the public, and that block wardens, party organisations, police and courts did much of the work — "a polymorphous, uncoordinated but pervasive system of control."

### Regime Matrix — cross-reference

**Matrix:** see `twentieth_century/04_nazi_state.md` (the Nazi dictatorship, 1933–1945). This chapter works inside that system; its 12-question matrix is not repeated here.

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** The Weimar Constitution was never formally repealed, and the regime staged plebiscites and mass rallies as proof of popular consent. In everyday practice, power reached people through block wardens, compulsory mass organisations, the police and denunciations: consent was organised, dissent was dangerous, and the "people's community" (*Volksgemeinschaft*) promised inclusion only to those the regime defined as racially and politically acceptable.

## 3. Society

| Group | Experience |
|---|---|
| **Industrial workers** | Unions destroyed, wages frozen, but jobs secure; KdF leisure; many remained sceptical but conformed. Tim Mason documented worker unrest and absenteeism in the late 1930s. |
| **Farmers** | Hereditary Farm Law (1933) protected mid-sized farms but restricted sale/inheritance; "blood and soil" praise; labour shortages. |
| **Middle classes / professionals** | Gains from Jewish dismissals (doctors, lawyers, civil servants); career opportunities in party and state. |
| **Business** | Profits; Aryanisation opportunities; later forced labour; regulation. |
| **Women** | Ideology of motherhood (Mother's Cross, marriage loans conditional on women leaving jobs), exclusion from some professions (e.g., judges); but female employment actually rose by the late 1930s; in the war, women were less mobilised for industry than in Britain, with forced labourers filling gaps. |
| **Youth** | Hitler Youth offered adventure, independence from parents and church, and indoctrination; some youth cultures resisted (Swing Youth, Edelweiss Pirates). |
| **Churches** | Protestant church split: "German Christians" (pro-Nazi) vs Confessing Church (defending church autonomy — not generally Jews); Catholic Church protected by Concordat but harassed; both churches' institutions largely survived. |
| **Jews** | ~500,000 in 1933 (~0.8%): progressive exclusion (1933 boycott and dismissals; 1935 Nuremberg Laws; 1938 pogrom and expropriation; 1939–41 forced labour and ghettoisation in "Jew houses"; from Oct 1941 deportation). About half emigrated before emigration was banned (Oct 1941). |
| **Sinti and Roma** | Racial persecution, camps (e.g., Berlin-Marzahn 1936), deportation and murder. |
| **Disabled people** | Forced sterilisation (from 1934; ~400,000); murder in the "euthanasia" programmes (from 1939). |
| **Homosexual men** | Paragraph 175 sharpened (1935); ~50,000 convicted; thousands sent to camps. |
| **"Asocials," "habitual criminals," Jehovah's Witnesses** | Camps; Witnesses refused oaths and military service. |

## 4. Everyday Life

- **Work:** Full employment by 1936–38; long hours; labour book (*Arbeitsbuch*) controlling job changes (1935).
- **Leisure:** Radio (the cheap "People's Receiver"), cinema, sport, KdF holidays (millions of short trips; cruises for a minority), the promised Volkswagen (never delivered to savers).
- **Family:** Pro-natalist policies; child allowances; marriage loans; at the same time, youth organisations pulled children away from parents' authority.
- **School:** Racial "science," physical education, political indoctrination; Jewish children expelled (1938).
- **Fear and speech:** The "German glance" (*der deutsche Blick*) — looking over one's shoulder before speaking; jokes about the regime could lead to prosecution under the "Malicious Practices Law" (1934).
- **Witnessing persecution:** Boycotts, public humiliation, the November 1938 pogrom (synagogues burned, ~30,000 Jewish men sent to camps, 91+ killed officially; more deaths in reality), auctions of deported Jews' property — happened in public. Historians call the Holocaust an "open secret" (Frank Bajohr & Dieter Pohl): information circulated via soldiers' letters, leave-takers, BBC broadcasts and rumour; many Germans knew enough to know that deported Jews were being killed, while choosing not to know more (Peter Longerich, *"Davon haben wir nichts gewusst!"*, 2006).
- **War (from 1939):** Rationing (relatively adequate until 1944 thanks to plunder from occupied Europe), blackouts, bombing (from 1942 intensively; ~350,000–600,000 civilian deaths in Germany — estimates vary), evacuation of children, millions of forced labourers in factories, farms and households — visible in every village (~13 million foreign forced labourers passed through the Reich; in August 1944 ~6 million civilian forced workers were more than a quarter of the workforce — Pagenstecher, bpb).

## 5. Economy

See `04_nazi_state.md`. For households: stable prices (controlled), wage freeze, rising incomes through overtime and full employment, limited consumer goods (rearmament priority).

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

**Four levels:**
1. **Regime:** *Volksgemeinschaft*, race, Lebensraum, leader cult.
2. **Intellectuals:** Many academics, jurists, doctors eagerly adapted ("self-coordination"); émigrés (Thomas Mann, Brecht, Arendt) and a few internal critics opposed.
3. **Movement:** Party activists, SA, SS, Hitler Youth leaders — true believers and careerists.
4. **Ordinary people:** Regime surveillance reports (SD *Meldungen aus dem Reich*) and exiled SPD reports (*Sopade*) show a complex picture: strong approval of Hitler personally (the "Hitler myth" — Kershaw), grumbling about party officials ("little Hitlers"), shortages and church policy; broad approval of anti-left measures and foreign-policy successes; mixed reactions to open violence against Jews (disapproval of the 1938 pogrom's disorder and destruction, rarely of the persecution itself).

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

War — and especially defeat from 1943 (Stalingrad), bombing, and Allied invasion — eroded the Hitler myth, increased terror against Germans themselves (defeatism trials, executions of deserters: ~15,000–20,000 Wehrmacht soldiers executed), and ended in collapse.

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE (1932):** Crisis, unemployment, political violence.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Dictatorship and terror; the promise of work and national revival; racial exclusion; preparation for war.
↓
**TRANSITION (1933–45):**
- 1933–38: "Normality" of order, jobs and national revival — for insiders; exclusion and terror for outsiders.
- 1939–45 (war): Plunder-supported home front → bombing → total war → collapse.
↓
**AFTER (1945):** Defeat experienced by many Germans as their own victimhood ("zero hour"), obscuring participation.

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

Loss of political freedoms and organisations; gains in employment and state-organised leisure for "Aryans"; racial classification of everyone; normalisation of persecution; from 1939, war, loss and — for millions of men — participation in a war of annihilation.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

Families, churches (as institutions), professions, companies, local notables; a sense of private life that many later described as "unpolitical."

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| Germans were terrorised into obedience | Terror was real but targeted; for many, consent (material, national, ideological) mattered more — though fear shaped behaviour. |
| Germans were all fanatical Nazis | Attitudes ranged from enthusiasm to conformity, opportunism, grumbling and (rarely) resistance. |
| "We knew nothing" | The persecution was public; knowledge of mass murder was widespread though often vague (open secret). |
| Women were only victims or only "mothers" | Women were supporters, beneficiaries, perpetrators (camp guards, nurses in "euthanasia") and victims. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **Consent vs coercion:** Robert Gellately (*Backing Hitler*, 2001) — a "plebiscitary dictatorship" built on consent, with citizens' denunciations powering the Gestapo; Eric Johnson similarly on denunciation. **Critique:** Richard J. Evans ("Coercion and Consent in Nazi Germany," British Academy lecture 2006, publ. 2007) — "perhaps 100,000 Germans were detained without trial" in 1933; the SPD and KPD electorates (13.1 million voters) were targets of systematic terror; plebiscite majorities were manufactured (stormtroopers marched voters to the polls); "the principal instrument of terror in Nazi Germany was not the concentration camp but the law." Reviewer Conan Fischer notes Gellately underplays material security. **CONTESTED; most now see both working together.**
- **Material benefits:** Götz Aly (*Hitlers Volksstaat*, 2005) — a "feel-good dictatorship" (*Gefälligkeitsdiktatur*) where plunder funded German living standards and taxes spared ordinary Germans. Adam Tooze — the economics are wrong: roughly 70% of war costs were financed from German taxes and borrowing, consumption was suppressed; the regime was held together by "coercion, ideological passion and the compelling force of the war itself." Tooze (2017) later noted the false separation of material and ideological motives.
- **Volksgemeinschaft as analytic concept:** Michael Wildt, Frank Bajohr — useful to study practices of inclusion/exclusion and popular participation. Peter Fritzsche's review (H-Soz-u-Kult 2009) stresses that the term was not a Nazi invention: every Weimar party except the Communists used it; it promised recognition, mobility and security, while always implying enemies to be excluded; and many contemporaries could, as he puts it, "love the Third Reich, but hate the Nazis" — a distinction post-war memory made conveniently exculpatory. critics (Ian Kershaw, Hans Mommsen, Hans-Ulrich Wehler) — risks adopting Nazi propaganda language and overstating social cohesion; rural studies show traditional attitudes persisted (e.g., Württemberg villages).
- **"Resistenz" (Martin Broszat's Bavaria project):** widespread partial non-conformity that limited the regime's reach — distinct from resistance.

### If you remember only 5 things

1. For most "Aryan" Germans before the war, life felt normal — even improved — because of jobs, order and national pride.
2. That normality rested on the visible exclusion, robbery and persecution of Jews and others.
3. The regime reached into daily life through mass organisations and neighbours willing to denounce.
4. Support combined belief, benefit, conformity and fear; historians debate the mix.
5. The murder of the Jews was an "open secret" — many knew enough, and chose not to know more.
