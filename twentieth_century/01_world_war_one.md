## 1. Period in One Paragraph

In July 1914 the German leadership gave Austria-Hungary unconditional backing (the "blank cheque") to punish Serbia, accepting the high risk of a European war. The war that followed killed about two million German soldiers, starved the home front under the British blockade, and transformed the state: parties declared a political truce (*Burgfrieden*), the SPD voted for war credits, the economy was militarised, and from 1916 the Third Supreme Army Command under Hindenburg and Ludendorff dominated politics so heavily that historians speak of a "silent" military dictatorship. Hunger, inequality and war-weariness produced strikes (1917–18) and a split in the SPD. When the army's spring 1918 offensive failed, Ludendorff himself demanded an armistice and a parliamentary government — to shift responsibility for defeat onto civilians. Sailors' mutinies at Kiel became a revolution; on 9 November 1918 the Kaiser abdicated and a republic was proclaimed. The myth that the army had been "stabbed in the back" by revolutionaries at home was born in the moment of defeat and poisoned the new republic.

## 2. The Political Structure

### Regime Matrix — Germany at war (esp. 1916–1918)

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Formally ruled | Kaiser, Chancellor, Bundesrat, Reichstag (unchanged constitution until Oct 1918). |
| 2 | Actually held power | Increasingly the **Supreme Army Command (OHL)**; from August 1916 Hindenburg and Ludendorff, who (per LeMO) "demanded for the OHL also the domestic and foreign political leadership," forced out Chancellor Bethmann Hollweg (July 1917), imposed unrestricted submarine warfare (Feb 1917) and set war aims. The Kaiser "retreated further into the background." |
| 3 | Acquired | Appointment by the Kaiser; Hindenburg's immense popularity after Tannenberg (1914). |
| 4 | Maintained | State of siege law (1851 Prussian law) gave regional deputy commanding generals power over press, assembly and food; propaganda; the Hindenburg myth. |
| 5 | Who could challenge | Reichstag majority (Peace Resolution, July 1917: SPD, Centre, Progressives); strikers; the USPD; the navy's sailors. |
| 6 | Money | War loans (nine war bond drives), borrowing and money creation — not taxes; this set up post-war inflation. |
| 7 | Enforcement | Military commanders; censorship; conscription of labour (Auxiliary Service Law, Dec 1916); courts-martial. |
| 8 | Representation | Reichstag continued; Interparty Committee (1917) of majority parties prefigured Weimar coalitions. |
| 9 | Rights | Suspended/curtailed under state of siege. |
| 10 | Opponents | Imprisonment (Karl Liebknecht 1916; Rosa Luxemburg in "protective custody"), drafting strikers to the front. |
| 11 | Connecting institutions | Army; war offices; unions (integrated through the Auxiliary Service Law's workers' committees); churches; propaganda. |
| 12 | Ordinary person's view | Husbands and sons at the front; queues, ration cards, substitute foods (*Ersatz*), black markets; casualty lists; growing anger at profiteers and inequality. |

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** Formally, the civilian constitution stood until October 1918. In practice, military commanders governed large areas of domestic life, and the OHL overruled the Chancellor and Kaiser on major questions. Paradoxically, the war also strengthened parliament and unions: the government needed the SPD's votes and the unions' cooperation, and the Interparty Committee rehearsed the Weimar coalition.

## 3. Society

- **Class tension:** Hunger and profiteering sharpened resentment against the rich, farmers (accused of hoarding) and officials. Real wages fell sharply for most workers except in armaments.
- **Women:** Entered munitions and transport work; organised food distribution; led food protests.
- **Jews:** ~100,000 served; the army's **"Jew count" (Judenzählung, Oct 1916)** — a census of Jewish soldiers prompted by antisemitic accusations of shirking — deeply wounded German Jews; its results were never published.
- **Minorities:** Poles, Alsatians and Danes subject to suspicion; occupied territories in the east ("Ober Ost") ruled as a military colony.

## 4. Everyday Life

- **Hunger:** The British blockade, loss of imports, lack of fertiliser, horses and labour, and a failed 1916 potato harvest (~50% of normal) produced the **"turnip winter" (1916/17)**; daily calorie intake fell far below need (LeMO: from ~3,000 in 1913 to under 1,000 in 1917 for many urban consumers — a figure for official rations; black-market and self-supply varied). Excess civilian deaths are often put at **~700,000–763,000**: after the war "the German government argued that approximately 763,000 German civilians died during the war because of the Allied blockade," plus some 150,000 from war-related Spanish influenza (Whalen, *1914-1918-online*, citing C. Paul Vincent). The figure originated in official German anti-blockade argument; some demographers argue for lower totals (~400,000–500,000). **CONTESTED/UNCERTAIN.**
- **Death at the front:** About 13 million men served; current best estimates (Overmans; McRandle & Quirk; Prost) put German military deaths at **2,037,000** — roughly 15% of those mobilised and ~13% of men born 1880–1899. Artillery caused most deaths. The Weimar Republic inherited responsibility for about 2.7 million disabled veterans, 533,000 war widows and 1.19 million war orphans (Whalen, *1914-1918-online*). About 12,000 German Jewish soldiers died — at the same rate as non-Jews, a fact later denied by antisemites.
- **Mood:** The famous "August experience" (*Augusterlebnis*) of war enthusiasm was real in some urban middle-class crowds but not general; Jeffrey Verhey shows that reactions varied by class and region, with anxiety widespread among workers and rural people, and that the "spirit of 1914" was later turned into a political myth (used by the Nazis, who called 1933 "a recreation of the days of 1914").

## 5. Economy

War economy: Walther Rathenau's War Raw Materials Department (1914); the Hindenburg Programme (1916) for massive munitions increases; forced labour of Belgians and Poles; state-industry-union cooperation. Financing by borrowing (Karl Helfferich: the enemy will pay) left enormous debts and a money overhang — the seed of post-war inflation.

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

- **"Ideas of 1914":** German intellectuals contrasted German "culture," community and duty with Western "civilisation," individualism and commerce — a theme later used against the republic.
- **War aims:** Chancellor Bethmann Hollweg's **September Programme (1914)** envisaged a German-dominated Central Europe (annexations, Belgian vassal state, economic union). Annexationist lobbies (Pan-Germans, Fatherland Party 1917, which claimed over a million members) vs the Reichstag's Peace Resolution (1917).
- **Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (March 1918):** Harsh peace imposed on Soviet Russia — huge eastern territories under German control; shows the regime's war aims.
- **Revolutionary socialism:** Spartacus League; Russian Revolution as model or warning.

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

Military stalemate; hunger; strikes (April 1917, January 1918 — hundreds of thousands); US entry (April 1917); the failure of the spring 1918 offensive; allies collapsing (Bulgaria, Sept 1918).

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE:** Semi-constitutional monarchy with military autonomy.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Total war, hunger, military dictatorship, failed offensives.
↓
**TRANSITION (crisis):**
- 29 Sept 1918: Ludendorff tells the Kaiser the war is lost, demands an armistice *and* a parliamentary government.
- October reforms: Prince Max of Baden's government includes SPD; constitution amended (28 Oct) — Chancellor now needs the Reichstag's confidence; military command subordinated.
- Naval command plans a last sortie; sailors at Wilhelmshaven and **Kiel mutiny** (29 Oct–3 Nov); workers' and soldiers' councils spread across Germany.
- **9 Nov 1918:** Max announces Kaiser's abdication; Philipp Scheidemann (SPD) proclaims a republic; Karl Liebknecht proclaims a "free socialist republic"; Friedrich Ebert heads the government.
- **10 Nov:** Ebert–Groener pact: the army supports the new government against the radical left; the government preserves the officer corps.
- **11 Nov:** Armistice signed by a civilian (Matthias Erzberger, Centre).
↓
**AFTER:** Council of People's Deputies (SPD + USPD) → National Assembly elections (Jan 1919) → Weimar Republic.

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

Mass death and mourning; hunger; women's wartime roles; politicisation; the end of monarchy; women's suffrage (12 Nov 1918); the 8-hour day (Nov 1918 Stinnes–Legien agreement between employers and unions); collective bargaining recognised.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

The officer corps, bureaucracy, judiciary, universities and large landowners — the Ebert–Groener pact and the priority of order meant continuity of elites into the republic.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| Germany entered the war in a wave of universal enthusiasm | Enthusiasm was concentrated and partial; anxiety was widespread (Verhey). |
| The army was "undefeated in the field" and stabbed in the back | The OHL itself declared the war lost and requested an armistice before the revolution. |
| Germany alone caused WWI / Germany bears no special responsibility | Scholarly positions range between; most historians assign Germany and Austria-Hungary a major, though not sole, share. |
| The revolution was a Bolshevik plot | It began as a sailors' mutiny against a suicidal naval sortie and spread through councils largely led by moderate socialists. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **War origins:**
  - **Fritz Fischer** (*Griff nach der Weltmacht*, 1961; *Krieg der Illusionen*, 1969) argued Germany deliberately pursued war for continental hegemony — sparking the "Fischer controversy." By the 1980s–90s a consensus accepted a "special responsibility" of the German Reich alongside other powers (Mombauer 2015).
  - **Christopher Clark** (*The Sleepwalkers*, 2012) spread responsibility across all powers and elevated Serbia's and Russia's roles; in Germany it had "a near-magical effect" (Mombauer), welcomed by many as relief from guilt.
  - **Critics** (John Röhl, Annika Mombauer, Gerd Krumeich) point to evidence of deliberate German risk-taking: the December 1912 "war council," Bethmann Hollweg's statements, the blank cheque (5 July 1914) despite high estimated risk of general war, and the September Programme.
  - **Current state:** Most specialists see German and Austro-Hungarian decisions in July 1914 as decisive, while acknowledging a system of mutual escalation. **CONTESTED (degree), not binary.**
- **"Silent dictatorship":** Martin Kitchen's term (1976) for the OHL's rule; others (e.g., Holger Afflerbach) show the OHL's power had limits and depended on popular prestige.
- **Hunger deaths:** see §4.

### If you remember only 5 things

1. Germany's leaders took a calculated risk of general war in July 1914; how much blame they bear is debated, not whether they bore a large share.
2. The war turned the state into a military-dominated system and the home front into a hunger zone.
3. The generals themselves admitted defeat — then blamed civilians (the stab-in-the-back myth).
4. The revolution of November 1918 began with sailors refusing a suicide mission and ended the monarchy.
5. The new republic kept the old army officers, judges and officials — a fateful continuity.
