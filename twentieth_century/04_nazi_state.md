## 1. Period in One Paragraph

The Nazi dictatorship was built in months, not years. Emergency decrees after the Reichstag fire (February 1933) abolished basic rights; the Enabling Act (March 1933) let the government make laws without parliament; parties and unions were dissolved, the states brought under central control ("coordination," *Gleichschaltung*), Jewish and politically "unreliable" officials purged. In 1934 Hitler had the SA leadership murdered and, on Hindenburg's death, merged the offices of President and Chancellor; the army swore a personal oath to him. But the resulting state was not a tidy pyramid. It was a jungle of competing agencies — old ministries, party offices, the SS-police empire, special plenipotentiaries — held together by Hitler's charismatic authority and radicalised by rivals "working towards the Führer." Law continued to function for "normal" matters, while a parallel "prerogative state" of police terror operated outside law. This combination — bureaucratic normality plus unrestricted terror plus competition for Hitler's favour — explains both the regime's popularity and its escalation toward war and genocide.

## 2. The Political Structure

### The seizure of power (*Machtergreifung*), 1933–34

| Date | Step |
|---|---|
| 30 Jan 1933 | Hitler chancellor (3 Nazis in cabinet of 11) |
| 4 Feb 1933 | Decree restricting press and assembly |
| 22 Feb 1933 | SA, SS, Stahlhelm as auxiliary police in Prussia (Göring) |
| 28 Feb 1933 | **Reichstag Fire Decree** — suspends basic rights (liberty, press, assembly, privacy of post, property); legal basis for "protective custody" without trial; never repealed |
| 5 Mar 1933 | Election under terror: NSDAP 43.9% (with DNVP ally a bare majority) |
| Mar 1933 | First concentration camps (Dachau, 22 March); ~tens of thousands arrested in 1933, especially Communists and Social Democrats (LeMO: ~15,000 Communist functionaries detained by summer 1933; Evans: "perhaps 100,000 Germans were detained without trial in so-called 'protective custody'" during 1933) |
| 23 Mar 1933 | **Enabling Act:** 444 votes to 94 (SPD alone against); 81 KPD mandates annulled; 26 SPD deputies absent (arrested, fled or injured); SA occupying the Kroll Opera. Otto Wels (SPD): "Freedom and life can be taken from us, but not our honour." |
| Mar–Apr 1933 | "Coordination" of the states; Reich governors (*Reichsstatthalter*) |
| 1 Apr 1933 | Boycott of Jewish shops |
| 7 Apr 1933 | Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service: Jews and political opponents dismissed |
| 2 May 1933 | Unions stormed and dissolved; German Labour Front (DAF) created |
| June–July 1933 | SPD banned; other parties dissolve; 14 July: NSDAP only legal party |
| 20 July 1933 | Concordat with the Vatican |
| Jan 1934 | States abolished as sovereign units (Law for the Reconstruction of the Reich) |
| 30 Jun–2 Jul 1934 | "Night of the Long Knives": Röhm and SA leaders, plus conservative opponents (Schleicher, Papen's aides) murdered; army leadership welcomes it |
| 2 Aug 1934 | Hindenburg dies; Hitler "Führer and Reich Chancellor"; Reichswehr oath to Hitler personally |

### Regime Matrix — Nazi Germany

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Formally ruled | Hitler as "Führer and Reich Chancellor"; a Reich government; a rubber-stamp Reichstag; the Weimar Constitution was never formally abolished. |
| 2 | Actually held power | Hitler at the apex; below him competing power centres: the **party** (Hess/Bormann, Gauleiters), the **SS–police complex** (Himmler, Heydrich: Gestapo, SD, concentration camps, from 1939 the Reich Security Main Office, RSHA), the **armed forces** (increasingly subordinated after 1938), the **ministerial bureaucracy**, **Göring's Four-Year Plan** and economic empire, **Goebbels's propaganda** apparatus, and big business (as a partner, not an independent power). |
| 3 | Power acquired | Legal-seeming appointment (1933), then terror and emergency law. |
| 4 | Power maintained | Terror + consent: police state, camps, special courts; economic recovery and full employment; foreign-policy successes (1935–38); propaganda; rewards; mobilisation through mass organisations; war and conquest. |
| 5 | Who could challenge | In theory: the army (only group with means) — conspiracies 1938 and 1944 failed; the churches (partial, on specific issues, e.g., "euthanasia" sermons by Bishop von Galen, 1941); popular unrest (feared by the regime after 1918). |
| 6 | Money | Taxes; massive borrowing (Mefo bills to hide rearmament financing); expropriation of Jews ("Aryanisation," flight tax, the 1938 "atonement" levy of one billion Reichsmarks); from 1939 plunder of occupied Europe and forced labour. |
| 7 | Enforcement | Gestapo and criminal police; "protective custody"; concentration camps; special courts (*Sondergerichte*) and the People's Court (*Volksgerichtshof*, 1934); denunciations by citizens. |
| 8 | Representation | None: plebiscites (e.g., 1933 League of Nations exit; 1934; 1936; 1938 Anschluss) with ~90–99% "yes" under pressure. |
| 9 | Rights | None guaranteed; rights depended on being a racially and politically acceptable "national comrade" (*Volksgenosse*). |
| 10 | Opponents | Camps, torture, execution; the People's Court and special courts passed ~16,000 death sentences (estimates) in total; families punished (*Sippenhaft*) after 1944. |
| 11 | Connecting institutions | NSDAP (block wardens), DAF, Strength Through Joy (KdF), Hitler Youth/BDM, NS-Frauenschaft, NS People's Welfare (NSV), Reich Food Estate, professional associations — nearly every club "coordinated." |
| 12 | Ordinary person's view | See `05_everyday_life_under_nazism.md`: for most "Aryan" Germans, normal life with jobs, leisure and national pride, alongside fear of speaking out; for Jews, Roma, disabled people, homosexuals, dissidents: exclusion, persecution, murder. |

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** The Weimar Constitution technically remained in force, "suspended" by the Reichstag Fire Decree and supplemented by the Enabling Act (renewed 1937, 1939, 1943). In practice, **Hitler's will was law** — the "Führer principle." Constitutional jurists (Carl Schmitt: "The Führer protects the law," 1934) justified murder as state necessity.

### Structural interpretations

- **The Dual State (Ernst Fraenkel, 1941):** A "normative state" (*Normenstaat*) — courts, contracts, property law — continued for ordinary affairs, while a "prerogative state" (*Maßnahmenstaat*) of police and party acted without legal limits whenever it chose. Anything could be declared "political" and moved into the prerogative sphere.
- **Polycracy (Martin Broszat, *Der Staat Hitlers*, 1969; Hans Mommsen):** Competing agencies with overlapping jurisdictions; Hitler as a "weak dictator" in administration (Mommsen), leaving rivals to fight it out.
- **"Working towards the Führer" (Ian Kershaw, 1993):** Kershaw uses a 1934 speech by the official Werner Willikens: "everyone who has the opportunity to observe it knows that the Führer can hardly dictate from above everything which he intends to realise sooner or later. On the contrary, up till now everyone with a post in the new Germany has worked best when he has, so to speak, worked towards the Führer." Collective cabinet government ceased — the cabinet last met in February 1938. Hitler's **charismatic authority** (Max Weber) combined with competition among subordinates to produce **cumulative radicalisation** (Mommsen) — officials anticipated his wishes, especially on the "Jewish question," pushing policy further than explicit orders.
- **The SS state:** Himmler's SS became a state within the state — police, camps, racial resettlement, Waffen-SS, economic enterprises — the core of the genocide machinery.

## 5. Economy

- **Recovery and rearmament:** Unemployment fell from ~6 million (1932) to near full employment by 1936–38, driven by work-creation programmes (begun before 1933), rearmament, conscription (1935), and statistical manipulation (women and Jews pushed out of labour statistics).
- **Four-Year Plan (1936, Göring):** Autarky and war preparation; synthetic fuel and rubber; the state directed investment, but private ownership remained (for "Aryans").
- **Business:** Profits recovered strongly; unions destroyed; wages frozen at 1933 levels; many firms profited from Aryanisation, rearmament, and later forced labour. Business was a *partner* with shrinking autonomy (Adam Tooze, *The Wages of Destruction*, 2006).
- **Consumption:** Held down by rearmament priorities; "guns vs butter" tensions visible by 1935–36.

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

Racial antisemitism; *Volksgemeinschaft*; *Lebensraum*; social Darwinism; leader cult; militarism; anti-Bolshevism; eugenics ("racial hygiene": Sterilisation Law, July 1933 — ~400,000 people forcibly sterilised).

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

The regime's own dynamics: rearmament required conquest; racial ideology demanded "solutions"; competition drove radicalisation. The regime did not "stabilise" into a normal authoritarian state (Kershaw); it was driven towards war and destruction.

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE:** Presidential authoritarianism within a republic (1930–33).
↓
**PRESSURES:** Nazi mass movement + conservative alliance.
↓
**TRANSITION (1933–34):** Emergency decree, Enabling Act, Gleichschaltung, purge, Führer state.
↓
**AFTER (1938–45):** Radicalisation — Anschluss, pogrom, war, genocide, total war, destruction; unconditional surrender 8 May 1945.

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

End of free elections, press, unions, parties; mass organisations structuring leisure and youth; jobs; racial classification of everyone (the "Aryan certificate"); fear of denunciation; war from 1939.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

Much of the civil service, judiciary, army leadership, universities, businesses, churches (as institutions) — continuity of personnel that would shape post-1945 West Germany.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| The Nazi state was a perfectly efficient machine | It was chaotic, polycratic and wasteful — yet highly effective at mobilisation, terror and murder. |
| Hitler micromanaged everything | He set goals and signalled wishes; subordinates competed to realise them. |
| Hitler was a "weak dictator" who didn't really decide | Mommsen's phrase refers to administrative style; no major policy (war, Holocaust) happened against Hitler's will. |
| The Gestapo was everywhere | It was small (~7,000 employees in 1937 — confirmed by Evans 2007) and depended on others: denunciations by citizens, but also party block wardens, Hitler Youth leaders, labour officials, the ordinary police and the courts. In Düsseldorf, only ~26% of Gestapo investigations began with denunciations from the general population (Evans). |
| Law disappeared | Ordinary law continued (the Dual State) — which made the regime seem "normal" to many. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **Intentionalism vs functionalism/structuralism:** Did Nazi policy (esp. the Holocaust) follow Hitler's long-held intentions (Karl Dietrich Bracher, Klaus Hildebrand, Eberhard Jäckel, Lucy Dawidowicz) or emerge from institutional chaos and cumulative radicalisation (Broszat, Mommsen)? **Current synthesis** (Kershaw, Browning, Longerich): Hitler's ideological goals and authority were indispensable; the path and timing were shaped by structures, competition and war.

  *In plain words:* Did Nazi policy, above all the Holocaust, follow Hitler's long-held plans (intentionalists: Bracher, Hildebrand, Jäckel, Dawidowicz), or grow out of chaotic rivalry between agencies that pushed each other to ever more extreme steps, 'cumulative radicalisation' (functionalists: Broszat, Mommsen)? Today's synthesis (Kershaw, Browning, Longerich): Hitler's goals and authority were indispensable, but structures, competition and war shaped how and when things happened.
- **Totalitarianism theory** (Arendt, Friedrich/Brzezinski) vs "fascism" theories; comparisons with Stalinism (Kershaw & Lewin, *Stalinism and Nazism*, 1997).

  *In plain words:* What kind of regime was Nazi Germany? Totalitarianism theory (Arendt; Friedrich and Brzezinski) sees it, like Stalin's Soviet Union, as a system aiming at total control over society. Rival 'fascism' theories classify it instead as a form of fascism, the far-right nationalist type of movement. Kershaw and Lewin's 1997 book compared Nazism and Stalinism directly.
- **Consent vs coercion:** see `05_everyday_life_under_nazism.md`.

  *In plain words:* Did Germans back the Nazi regime willingly, or were they forced? One side stresses consent, such as citizens informing on neighbours to the Gestapo (secret police); the other stresses terror, mass arrests and rigged votes. Most historians now think both worked together. The debate is set out in the chapter on everyday life under Nazism.

### If you remember only 5 things

1. Dictatorship came through emergency decree and a coerced "legal" Enabling Act — within six months.
2. The state was a polycratic jungle held together by Hitler's charismatic authority.
3. Officials "worked towards the Führer," producing cumulative radicalisation.
4. Normal law coexisted with a lawless police sphere (the "Dual State"), which made the regime look ordinary to many.
5. Old elites — army, bureaucracy, business, judiciary — served the regime and largely survived it.
