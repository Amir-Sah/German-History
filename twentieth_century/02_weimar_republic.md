## 1. Period in One Paragraph

The Weimar Republic was Germany's first parliamentary democracy — created amid defeat, revolution and civil-war-like violence, and ended fourteen years later when conservative elites handed power to Hitler. Its constitution (1919) gave all adults over 20, including women, an equal vote, a parliament to which the government was responsible, extensive rights, and a powerful directly elected President with emergency powers (Article 48). It survived early crises — the Versailles Treaty, left and right uprisings, political assassinations, the 1923 hyperinflation — and experienced relative stability and cultural brilliance in 1924–29. But its legitimacy was contested from the start: much of the old elite (army, judiciary, bureaucracy, large landowners, universities) rejected it; the left was split between Social Democrats and Communists; the stab-in-the-back myth and Versailles resentment poisoned politics. The Great Depression (from 1929) produced mass unemployment and the rise of the Nazis and Communists. From 1930 governments ruled by presidential decree rather than parliamentary majority. In January 1933 President Hindenburg, persuaded by a small circle, appointed Hitler chancellor. The republic was not overthrown by a majority vote; it was abandoned.

## 2. The Political Structure

### Regime Matrix — Weimar Republic

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Formally ruled | The people (popular sovereignty), through the **Reichstag** (proportional representation, no threshold) and the directly elected **Reich President** (7-year term); Chancellor and ministers needed the Reichstag's confidence; **Reichsrat** of the states. |
| 2 | Actually held power | 1919–1930: shifting coalitions (Weimar coalition SPD–Centre–DDP; later bourgeois coalitions; grand coalition 1928–30). 1930–33: the President and his circle (Hindenburg, his son Oskar, State Secretary Meissner, General Schleicher), the Reichswehr leadership, and chancellors appointed by the President. |
| 3 | Power acquired | Elections; coalition bargaining; from 1930, presidential appointment. |
| 4 | Power maintained | Parliamentary majorities → emergency decrees (Art. 48) + dissolution threats (Art. 25). |
| 5 | Who could challenge | Anti-system parties (KPD, NSDAP, parts of DNVP); paramilitaries; the Reichswehr ("a state within the state"); the President. |
| 6 | Money | Reich income tax (Erzberger's finance reform 1919–20 centralised taxation); borrowing; reparations imposed obligations. |
| 7 | Enforcement | Police (largely state-run; Prussia's police under SPD ministers was a pillar of the republic until July 1932), courts, Reichswehr. |
| 8 | Representation | Universal suffrage (20+), proportional representation; referenda possible. |
| 9 | Rights | Extensive basic and social rights (Part II of the constitution), but suspendable under Art. 48. |
| 10 | Opponents | Democratic procedures, but the judiciary was notoriously lenient to the right (Hitler's 1924 sentence: 5 years, released after ~9 months) and harsh to the left. |
| 11 | Connecting institutions | Parties and their milieus, trade unions, churches, paramilitary leagues, welfare offices, schools. |
| 12 | Ordinary person's view | Freedom and new opportunities (voting, unions, culture, welfare); but also inflation, unemployment, street violence, and a feeling that the state was weak. |

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:**
- *Said (1919):* Parliamentary government; President as a counterweight ("substitute emperor") with Art. 48 emergency powers to restore order; Art. 25 dissolution power; Reichstag could cancel emergency decrees.
- *Worked (1919–23):* Art. 48 used over 100 times by Ebert (SPD) — mostly to defend the republic.
- *Worked (1930–33):* Art. 48 became, per Ernst Piper (bpb), "increasingly a substitute for regular legislation." The Reichstag could cancel decrees only with a majority — which required SPD support; the SPD **tolerated** Brüning to avoid worse. Presidential cabinets (Brüning, Papen, Schleicher) governed with the President's confidence, not parliament's. Three Reichstag elections in 1930–32 destabilised further. The **Prussian coup (*Preußenschlag*, 20 July 1932)** — Papen deposed the SPD-led Prussian government by decree — removed the republic's most important bastion; Piper: "No other measure of Papen's short-lived government more prepared Hitler's seizure of power."

## 3. Society

- **Workers:** Won the 8-hour day (later eroded), recognition of unions, works councils (1920), unemployment insurance (1927). Divided politically: SPD (reformist, pro-republic) vs KPD (revolutionary, Moscow-aligned; from 1928 attacked the SPD as "social fascists").
- **Middle classes:** Savers and pensioners ruined by the 1923 inflation; civil servants' salaries cut; small business and farmers squeezed; many drifted from liberal and conservative parties to splinter parties and then the NSDAP.
- **Elites:** East Elbian landowners (agrarian crisis, "Osthilfe" subsidies scandal), heavy industry (hostile to unions and welfare costs after 1928), officer corps, judges and higher officials — mostly monarchist or authoritarian, some "republicans of reason" (*Vernunftrepublikaner*, e.g., Stresemann).
- **Women:** Voted from 1919 (turnout high; 37 women among 423 deputies in the National Assembly, ~9%); "New Woman" in cities (office work, fashion, sport); but women's employment was attacked in the Depression (*Doppelverdiener* campaigns); abortion (§218) remained criminal.
- **Jews:** ~500,000 (under 1%); full legal equality; prominent in culture, science, law and commerce; faced rising antisemitic violence and discrimination (e.g., murder of Foreign Minister Walther Rathenau, 1922).
- **Youth:** A "superfluous generation" (Detlev Peukert) of young men facing unemployment; youth movements; paramilitary appeal.
- **Religion:** Protestant churches largely nationalist and hostile to the republic; the Catholic Centre a governing pillar until 1932; Catholics relatively resistant to Nazism at the polls.

## 4. Everyday Life

1919–23: food shortages, violence, strikes, hyperinflation (a loaf of bread cost ~200 billion marks in November 1923). 1924–29: electrification, cinema, radio, mass sports, public housing (the "New Building" estates), welfare offices, Berlin nightlife — alongside rural hardship. 1929–33: mass unemployment (~6 million registered in early 1932, perhaps a third of the workforce, plus hidden unemployment), benefit cuts, evictions, street battles between SA and Communists, soup kitchens.

## 5. Economy

- **War debt + reparations + social demands** → inflation → **hyperinflation (1923)** during French-Belgian occupation of the Ruhr (passive resistance financed by printing money). Winners: debtors (including industrialists and the state); losers: savers, creditors, pensioners.
- **Stabilisation (Rentenmark, Nov 1923; Dawes Plan 1924):** US loans financed recovery and reparations.
- **Depression (1929–33):** Withdrawal of US credit; bank crisis (July 1931); Brüning's **deflationary austerity** (cuts to wages, salaries, benefits; tax rises) aimed partly at ending reparations (achieved at Lausanne 1932 — too late).

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

| Current | Carriers | Attitude to republic |
|---|---|---|
| Social democracy | SPD, Free Unions | Defender |
| Political Catholicism | Centre, BVP | Defender until drift right (1928–33) |
| Liberalism | DDP (left), DVP (right) | Weak; collapsed electorally |
| National conservatism | DNVP (under Hugenberg from 1928 radicalised) | Hostile |
| "Conservative Revolution" | Intellectuals (Moeller van den Bruck, Spengler, Jünger, Carl Schmitt) | Hostile — anti-liberal, authoritarian |
| Communism | KPD | Hostile |
| National Socialism | NSDAP | Hostile |

**What people believed:** Voting patterns show milieu loyalty (Catholics to Centre, organised workers to SPD/KPD) and the collapse of the Protestant middle-class parties into protest voting. The Versailles "war guilt" clause (Art. 231) and the stab-in-the-back myth were widely believed across the non-left spectrum.

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

Versailles and reparations; stab-in-the-back myth; elite disloyalty; left split; paramilitary violence; inflation (1923) destroying middle-class trust; Depression; the President's constitutional powers and personal preferences (Hindenburg "fundamentally rejected cooperation with the SPD" — Piper).

## 8. Transition

See `03_rise_of_nazism.md` for the detailed causal account.

**BEFORE:** Parliamentary democracy with contested legitimacy.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Depression; polarisation; presidential decree government; Nazi mass movement.
↓
**TRANSITION (crisis, 1930–33):**
- 1930: grand coalition collapses over unemployment insurance → Brüning → Papen → Preußenschlag → Schleicher → intrigue.
- **30 January 1933:** Hindenburg appoints Hitler chancellor of a cabinet with only three Nazis, believing conservatives could "contain" him (Papen: "We've hired him").
↓
**AFTER:** Within six months: dictatorship (see `twentieth_century/04_nazi_state.md`).

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People? — 1918–1933

Political rights for all adults; unions, collective bargaining, works councils; unemployment insurance; mass culture; but also inflation, unemployment and pervasive political violence.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

Army officer corps (the Reichswehr, limited to 100,000 by Versailles, remained a conservative, semi-autonomous body under Seeckt); judiciary; senior civil service; universities; East Elbian estates; federal states; the milieu-based party system.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| Hitler was elected into power | The NSDAP peaked at ~37% in free elections (July 1932) and fell to ~33% (Nov 1932); Hitler was *appointed*. |
| Weimar was doomed from the start | It survived 1919–23; the 1924–29 period showed stabilisation; failure came from specific choices in 1930–33. |
| Proportional representation caused the collapse | PR fragmented parliament, but the key factors were anti-democratic parties winning majorities and presidential decree rule. |
| Hyperinflation directly brought Hitler to power | 1923 undermined trust; the Nazi breakthrough came in the Depression (1930–32), after deflation. |
| Weimar was only crisis | It was also a period of social reform, cultural modernity and democratic practice. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **Why did Weimar fail?** Structural (constitution, Versailles, economy, elites) vs contingent (1930–33 decisions). Karl Dietrich Bracher's classic *Die Auflösung der Weimarer Republik* (1955) emphasised the power vacuum and presidential system; recent work (e.g., Piper for bpb; Ursula Büttner; Tim B. Müller) emphasises that democracy was not doomed and was destroyed by elite choices.
- **The Borchardt controversy (1979–):** Knut Borchardt argued Brüning had no room for manoeuvre ("Zwangslagen") because the Weimar economy was already "sick" (wages above productivity) and credit was unavailable. Carl-Ludwig Holtfrerich and others contested the wage data and argued alternatives existed. Albrecht Ritschl accepts Borchardt's diagnosis but locates the constraint in the external debt crisis under the Young Plan. **CONTESTED.**
- **Austerity and Nazi votes:** Galofré-Vilà, Meissner, McKee and Stuckler (NBER, 2017/2018) find that districts hit harder by austerity saw larger Nazi vote gains — "austerity is only one factor."
- **"Crisis of classical modernity" (Peukert):** Weimar as a laboratory of modernity whose tensions were exploited by the Nazis.
- **Weimar as "democracy history":** bpb's concluding chapter (2021) frames Weimar as a site of democratic history rather than merely prelude to Nazism.

### If you remember only 5 things

1. Weimar was a genuine democracy with universal suffrage and strong rights — and a President with emergency powers.
2. Much of the old elite never accepted it, and the left was split between SPD and KPD.
3. Inflation (1923) destroyed middle-class trust; the Depression (1929–33) destroyed the parliamentary majority.
4. From 1930, government by presidential decree hollowed out democracy before Hitler arrived.
5. Hitler was appointed by conservatives who thought they could use him — democracy was abandoned, not voted out.
