## 1. Period in One Paragraph

The NSDAP began in 1919–20 as one of many tiny *völkisch* groups in Munich, failed in a coup attempt (1923), and polled only 2.6% in 1928. Within four years it became the largest party (37% in July 1932). Three layers of explanation are needed. **Long-term conditions:** a political culture in which large parts of the Protestant middle classes, elites and intellectuals rejected parliamentary democracy; a nationalism radicalised by defeat and Versailles; widespread antisemitism; a society organised in hostile milieus. **Medium-term shocks:** the lost war and the stab-in-the-back myth; the 1923 inflation; agricultural crisis; the Great Depression and mass unemployment. **Short-term decisions:** the collapse of the grand coalition (1930), presidential decree government, deflationary policy, the Prussian coup, and the backroom deal that made Hitler chancellor. The Nazis were a genuine mass movement — a "people's party of protest" drawing on all classes, but disproportionately Protestant, rural and middle-class — yet they never won a free majority. They came to power because conservative elites chose to use them.

## 2. The Political Structure — how the NSDAP worked as a movement

- **Leader principle (*Führerprinzip*):** After 1925 re-founding, Hitler's authority was absolute within the party; programmes were vague and flexible (the 1920 25-point programme was declared "unalterable" and largely ignored).
- **Organisation:** Regional *Gaue* under Gauleiter; a dense network of associations for every group (farmers, students, doctors, lawyers, women, Hitler Youth); modern propaganda (Goebbels), aerial campaign tours ("Hitler over Germany," 1932), rallies, film.
- **Paramilitary:** SA (Storm Detachment), several hundred thousand strong by 1932 — street violence, intimidation, meeting protection; attracted unemployed young men.
- **Legality tactic:** After 1923, Hitler pursued power through elections while signalling that violence would follow ("heads will roll," Leipzig Reichswehr trial testimony 1930).
- **Finance:** Mainly membership dues, rally fees and small donations; big-business funding came late and was limited before 1933 (Henry Ashby Turner) — though some industrialists and landowners supported Hitler's appointment.

### Regime Matrix — presidential cabinets (*Präsidialkabinette*), March 1930 – January 1933

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Who formally ruled? | The Weimar Constitution: an elected Reichstag, an elected Reich President, and a chancellor who needed the Reichstag's confidence. |
| 2 | Who actually held power? | Reich President Hindenburg and his entourage (his son Oskar, State Secretary Meissner, General Schleicher), governing through chancellors without parliamentary majorities — Brüning, Papen, Schleicher. |
| 3 | How was power acquired? | Appointment by the president. |
| 4 | How was power maintained? | Emergency decrees under Art. 48; dissolution of the Reichstag whenever it moved to overturn them; the SPD's "toleration" of Brüning (1930–32). |
| 5 | Who could challenge the government? | A Reichstag majority (which could revoke decrees); the mass parties NSDAP and KPD; street violence; the attitude of the Reichswehr. |
| 6 | How was money obtained? | Taxes; austerity and deflation imposed by decree under Brüning. |
| 7 | How were decisions enforced? | Police (mostly run by the Länder until the *Preußenschlag* of July 1932 removed Prussia's SPD-led government) and the Reichswehr. |
| 8 | How were ordinary people represented? | Elections continued (1930, two in 1932), but parliament was sidelined. |
| 9 | Rights? | Constitutional rights, increasingly limited by decree (press and demonstration bans; SA ban April–June 1932). |
| 10 | Opponents? | Legal opposition; political violence only partly suppressed. |
| 11 | Institutions connecting state to society | Parties, paramilitary leagues, unions, agrarian lobbies, the Reichswehr, the press. |
| 12 | Ordinary person's view | Mass unemployment, wage and benefit cuts by decree, repeated elections, violence in the streets. |

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** The constitution made the chancellor dependent on the Reichstag and treated Article 48 as an emergency tool. From 1930 the emergency became the normal way of governing: laws were made by presidential decree, the Reichstag was dissolved when it objected, and real power passed to an unelected president and a small circle of advisers — which is why that circle could appoint Hitler in January 1933 (see §8).

## 3. Society — who supported the Nazis?

### Voters (Jürgen Falter's ecological-inference research)

- **Protestant vs Catholic:** The single strongest predictor. Devout Catholics were strongly resistant; Protestant rural areas showed the highest Nazi vote.
- **Class:** The NSDAP was a *Volkspartei des Protests* with a "middle-class belly" — over-represented among the self-employed, farmers, civil servants, pensioners; but **workers were not immune**: by July 1932, more workers voted Nazi than voted Communist or Social Democrat in some calculations; Falter estimates perhaps 40% of NSDAP voters came from working-class households (often non-unionised, rural, small-town workers).
- **White-collar employees:** voted Nazi *less* than average (contrary to older theories of a "panic of the middle class").
- **Unemployed:** favoured the KPD over the NSDAP (one in four unemployed voted KPD, one in six NSDAP).
- **Gender and age:** No meaningful gender gap by 1932; young voters not disproportionately Nazi.
- **Voter flows (Falter):** ~7.5 million votes came from former liberal-conservative/bourgeois parties (DNVP, DVP, DDP and splinters), ~6 million from previous non-voters, >2 million from the SPD, ~600,000 from Catholic parties, ~350,000 from the KPD.

### Members

Party membership grew from ~130,000 (1930) to ~850,000 by January 1933 and then, in the rush of 1933, to ~2.5 million. Falter's membership study (*Hitlers Parteigenossen*, 2020) confirms a broad social base with over-representation of the middle classes.

## 4. Everyday Life — how Nazism reached people

Local notables (pastors, teachers, farmers' leaders) who joined gave the movement respectability; SA taverns and soup kitchens; rallies as entertainment; propaganda tailored by group (farmers' debt, artisans' competition, workers' jobs); violence against opponents, normalised by lenient courts.

## 5. Economy

- **Agrarian crisis** from the late 1920s (falling prices, debt, foreclosures) — farmers' protests (Landvolk movement in Schleswig-Holstein) preceded Nazi rural breakthroughs.
- **Depression:** Industrial production fell ~40%; registered unemployment ~6 million (early 1932).
- **Austerity:** Brüning's deflation cut benefits, wages and public spending; NBER research finds austerity associated with higher Nazi vote shares (with caveats).

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

**Core Nazi ideology:** racial antisemitism (Jews as the enemy behind both capitalism and Bolshevism); *Volksgemeinschaft* (a racially defined "people's community" transcending class); *Lebensraum* (conquest of "living space" in the East); anti-Marxism; the leader principle; revision of Versailles; national rebirth.

**Why did it appeal?** Historians distinguish:
1. **Elites** (landowners, army, some industrialists, conservative politicians): wanted an authoritarian state, destruction of the left and unions, rearmament; saw Nazis as a mass base to borrow.
2. **Intellectuals** ("Conservative Revolution"): prepared an anti-democratic climate, though many despised Nazi vulgarity.
3. **The movement:** Genuine believers in racial nationalism, veterans, youth.
4. **Ordinary voters:** Many voted *against* the "system" and for promised national renewal, jobs, order and anti-Marxism; antisemitism was pervasive but historians debate how central it was to voters' motives (William Sheridan Allen's local study; Kershaw's assessment that antisemitism was secondary for many voters). **CONTESTED.**

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE (1928):** NSDAP a fringe party (2.6%); grand coalition governs.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Depression; austerity; farmers' crisis; decree government; Young Plan campaign (1929) gives Hitler respectability via alliance with Hugenberg's DNVP.
↓
**TRANSITION (crisis, 1930–33):**
- Sept 1930: NSDAP 18.3% (107 seats).
- 1932: Hindenburg re-elected against Hitler (with SPD support!); SA ban then lifted by Papen; July election NSDAP ~37%, KPD ~14% — negative majority of anti-system parties.
- Nov 1932: NSDAP drops to 33%; party in financial crisis; Schleicher tries to split it.
- Jan 1933: Papen–Hitler negotiations (banker Kurt von Schröder's house, 4 Jan); Hindenburg's camarilla persuaded.
- 30 Jan 1933: Hitler chancellor; conservatives (Papen vice-chancellor, Hugenberg economics) believe they will control him.
↓
**AFTER:** Dictatorship within months (see `04_nazi_state.md`).

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People? — 1930–33

Mass unemployment, poverty, daily political violence (hundreds killed in street clashes in 1932), a sense of state paralysis; a choice presented as between "Bolshevism" and "national renewal."

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

Catholic and Social Democratic milieus stayed largely loyal to their parties until 1933 — the Nazi vote hit a ceiling around 37% in free elections.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| Most Germans voted for Hitler | Maximum ~37% in a free election; 43.9% in March 1933 under terror conditions. |
| Nazis were a lower-middle-class party only | A "people's party of protest" drawing from all classes, esp. Protestant middle classes. |
| Big business brought Hitler to power | Business funding before 1933 was limited; business mostly preferred Papen; but some industrialists and landowners lobbied for Hitler in late 1932. |
| The unemployed voted Nazi | The unemployed leaned KPD; fear of decline among the still-employed mattered more. |
| Versailles alone explains Nazism | Versailles resentment was universal among non-left Germans in the 1920s, yet the Nazis were marginal until 1930. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **Class-based theories** (Seymour Martin Lipset's "extremism of the centre"; Marxist theories of fascism as capital's instrument) vs **"people's party of protest"** (Falter, Childers).
- **Economic vs political causation:** How much did the Depression vs political choices matter? (Borchardt debate; NBER austerity study; Straumann on devaluation fears.)
- **Role of antisemitism in Nazi support:** Goldhagen (central) vs Kershaw/Falter (secondary for most voters, but no obstacle).
- **Sonderweg:** Long-term German peculiarity (Wehler, Bracher) vs contingency (Evans, Piper).
- **The KPD's role:** Its "social fascism" line and attacks on the SPD contributed to the left's paralysis; GDR historiography downplayed this.

### If you remember only 5 things

1. The Nazis went from 2.6% (1928) to 37% (1932) because the Depression met a political culture already hostile to democracy.
2. Their voters came from all classes but were disproportionately Protestant, rural and middle-class; Catholics and organised workers resisted.
3. They never won a free majority.
4. Presidential decree rule and the destruction of Prussian democracy (1932) cleared the way.
5. Conservative elites installed Hitler, thinking they could control him — the decisive short-term cause.
