## 1. Period in One Paragraph

Divided Germany was the Cold War's front line. The two German states were built into rival blocs — the FRG into NATO (1955) and the European Communities, the GDR into the Warsaw Pact (1955) and Comecon — with hundreds of thousands of foreign troops and, eventually, thousands of nuclear weapons on their soil. Berlin, a Western island 160 km inside the GDR, was the flashpoint: the Soviet blockade (1948–49), the Khrushchev ultimatum (1958), and the construction of the **Berlin Wall** (13 August 1961), which stopped an exodus that had cost the GDR about 2.7–3 million people since 1949. The Wall stabilised the GDR and made division feel permanent. From the late 1960s both states moved toward coexistence: Willy Brandt's **Ostpolitik** recognised post-war borders and the GDR's existence in practice, while insisting on the unity of the nation; the 1975 Helsinki Final Act linked security with human rights. Détente brought travel, trade and information — and, as it turned out, eroded the GDR's isolation. Neither state nor superpower expected the division to end in 1989.

## 2. The Political Structure

### Two states, two alliances, one question

| Dimension | FRG | GDR |
|---|---|---|
| Alliance | NATO (1955); Bundeswehr (1955) under NATO command | Warsaw Pact (1955); National People's Army (1956) |
| Economic bloc | ECSC (1951), EEC (1957) | Comecon (1950) |
| Claim | Sole legitimate representative of Germany (Hallstein Doctrine 1955–69: no diplomatic relations with states recognising the GDR) | From 1970s: separate socialist nation |
| Constitution on unity | Preamble: goal of reunification; Art. 23/146 | 1949: "Germany is an indivisible republic"; 1974: all references to German nation removed |
| Berlin | West Berlin: special status (Allied), linked to FRG | East Berlin: capital of GDR (contrary to four-power status) |
| Foreign troops | US, UK, French (and others) ~400,000 | Soviet ~350,000–500,000 |

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** The FRG's Basic Law demanded reunification, but Adenauer prioritised Western integration and rejected Stalin's 1952 note offering a neutral united Germany (whether the offer was serious is still debated — the "Stalin Note" controversy). The GDR's 1949 constitution claimed all-German legitimacy but the SED built a separate state.

### The Berlin Wall (1961–1989)

- **Why built:** The open sector border in Berlin let ~2.7–3 million people leave the GDR/SBZ between 1945/49 and 1961 — disproportionately young and skilled (doctors, engineers, farmers after collectivisation). In 1960 alone, hundreds of thousands fled (bpb). Ulbricht obtained Soviet approval in early August 1961. Official name: "Anti-Fascist Protective Wall."
- **What it was:** By the 1980s a 155 km border system around West Berlin: inner wall, "death strip," watchtowers, patrol roads, alarm fences, and the 1,378 km inner-German border with mines (cleared by 1985) and automatic firing devices (SM-70, dismantled 1983–84).
- **Deaths:** At least **140 people** were killed or died at the Berlin Wall (101 escapees shot or killed in accidents or suicide; 30 others; 8 border soldiers) (Stiftung Berliner Mauer/ZZF research 2005–2017). At the inner-German border, estimates range from at least 260 to ~327 (FU Berlin study), and ~650 including all border-regime victims such as Baltic Sea escapes.
- **Shoot-to-kill:** There was no single written "order to shoot" (*Schießbefehl*) for all cases, but border troops were instructed to prevent "border breaches" by all means including firearms, and successful shooters were rewarded. After 1990, courts convicted border guards and political leaders (Politburo members Egon Krenz, Günter Schabowski, and others) for manslaughter.

## 3. Society

Families were split; West Germans could visit the GDR (with restrictions and mandatory currency exchange); East Germans could not visit the West until retirement age (from 1964) or on urgent family business (expanded in the 1980s). Transit traffic, parcels, West TV and phone calls maintained ties. For West Berliners, Berlin was an island subsidised by the FRG; young men moved there to avoid conscription (Berlin's special status meant no Bundeswehr service).

## 4. Everyday Life

West: Cold War anxiety (civil defence drills, the 1983 peace movement against missile deployment), anti-communism, but also normality and prosperity. East: the Wall as a daily reality, militarised education (military instruction in schools from 1978), the sense of confinement ("to be walled in").

## 5. Economy

The two economies drifted apart: FRG export boom vs GDR planned economy. Inner-German trade was treated by the FRG as domestic (so the GDR had privileged access to the EEC market). The FRG made substantial payments to the GDR (transit fees, prisoner buyouts, credits) — keeping the GDR afloat and humanitarian channels open.

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

- **Anti-communism** (FRG) vs **anti-fascism/anti-imperialism** (GDR).
- **"Change through rapprochement"** (*Wandel durch Annäherung*, Egon Bahr, 1963): recognise realities to change them.
- **"Two states, one nation"** (Brandt, 1969).
- **Helsinki (1975):** Basket III (human rights, contacts) gave GDR citizens an internationally signed text to cite in exit applications.

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

- Détente and Ostpolitik (1969–73): Moscow Treaty (1970) — borders "inviolable," not "immutable"; Warsaw Treaty (1970); Four-Power Agreement on Berlin (1971); **Basic Treaty** (1972) — mutual recognition in practice, "permanent missions" instead of embassies; both states in the UN (1973). CDU/CSU abstained in the 1972 ratification vote (Rother, APuZ 2025).
- Economic dependence of the GDR on Western credit.
- Second Cold War (1979–85) and then Gorbachev (1985).
- Rother: "Without Ostpolitik, no KSZE"; Ostpolitik reduced neighbours' fears of Germany, facilitating Soviet consent to unification in 1990.

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE (1945–49):** Occupied Germany.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Superpower rivalry; incompatible Soviet and Western plans for Germany; Berlin's exposed position; mass flight from East to West.
↓
**TRANSITION (1948–1989):**
- Division: 1948–49 currency reforms, blockade, two states.
- Cementing: 1955 alliances; 1961 Wall.
- Détente: 1969–75 Ostpolitik, Basic Treaty, Helsinki.
- Crisis: 1989 Soviet non-intervention, exodus, protests → Wall opens.
↓
**AFTER (1990):** Two-plus-Four Treaty and unification; united Germany in NATO (see `twentieth_century/12_reunification.md`).

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

Divided families; different life courses in East and West; after 1961, East Germans' life planning had to accept confinement; after 1972, more visits and contacts.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

Shared language, much shared culture (literature, football rivalries, West TV), family ties; the "German question" as a latent issue.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| The Wall was built to keep Westerners out | It was built to stop East Germans leaving. |
| Ostpolitik accepted division forever | It accepted realities to keep the German question open and ease human contacts. |
| Reunification was a constant, active West German policy | By the 1980s most West German politicians expected division to last indefinitely. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **Stalin Note (1952):** Genuine missed opportunity (Rolf Steininger) vs propaganda manoeuvre (Hermann Graml, Gerhard Wettig — supported by Soviet archival evidence). Majority now lean towards manoeuvre. **CONTESTED.**
- **Who pushed for the Wall — Ulbricht or Khrushchev?** Hope Harrison (*Driving the Soviets up the Wall*, 2003) shows Ulbricht pressed the Soviets; Khrushchev decided.
- **Did Ostpolitik stabilise or undermine the GDR?** Both: it stabilised it economically in the short term and undermined it through contacts and Helsinki norms in the long term. **CONTESTED weighting.**

### If you remember only 5 things

1. Germany was the Cold War's front line, and each German state was built into its bloc.
2. The Wall was built because the GDR was bleeding people — it worked, at a brutal cost.
3. At least 140 people died at the Berlin Wall; hundreds more at the inner-German border.
4. Ostpolitik accepted realities in order to change them — and helped make 1990 acceptable to Moscow and neighbours.
5. By the 1980s, almost no one expected unification soon.
