## 1. Period in One Paragraph

In 1989 the GDR collapsed in a **Peaceful Revolution**. Three pressures met: the Soviet Union under Gorbachev would no longer use force to save allied regimes; tens of thousands of East Germans fled via Hungary (which opened its border to Austria) and West German embassies in Prague and Warsaw; and inside the GDR, church-based opposition groups and then mass demonstrations — above all in Leipzig on Monday evenings — demanded reform ("We are the people!"). On 9 October 1989, 70,000 marched in Leipzig and the security forces did not shoot. Honecker fell (18 October). On 9 November, a botched announcement of new travel rules by Politburo member Günter Schabowski led crowds to force open the Berlin Wall. Round tables negotiated a transition; the SED lost its monopoly; the slogan shifted to "We are *one* people!" The first free election (18 March 1990) gave a large majority to parties favouring rapid unification. A currency union (1 July), the Unification Treaty and the Two-plus-Four Treaty with the four victorious powers (12 September) led to the GDR's accession to the Federal Republic on **3 October 1990**. Unification was fast, peaceful and internationally accepted — and its economic transformation in the East was traumatic.

## 2. The Political Structure

### Sequence of the revolution and transition

| Date | Event |

| 2 May / 27 June | Hungary begins dismantling border fence; symbolic cut with Austria |
| 19 Aug | Pan-European Picnic: ~600 East Germans cross into Austria |
| 10–11 Sept | Hungary opens border for East Germans; ~14,000 per week leave by September (Hoffmann) |
| Sept | Neues Forum founded (Bärbel Bohley, Jens Reich); Demokratie Jetzt; SDP (East German Social Democrats) founded 7 Oct |
| 30 Sept | Genscher announces departure permission to Prague embassy refugees |
| 7 Oct | GDR's 40th anniversary; Gorbachev present ("life punishes those who come too late" — paraphrase attributed to him); protests, arrests |
| **9 Oct** | **Leipzig: ~70,000 demonstrate peacefully; security forces stand down** — the decisive turning point |
| 18 Oct | Honecker replaced by Egon Krenz |
| 4 Nov | ~500,000 at Alexanderplatz demonstration (Berlin) |
| **9 Nov** | **Schabowski's press conference; Wall opens** |
| 13 Nov | Hans Modrow (reform SED) prime minister |
| 1 Dec | Volkskammer deletes SED's "leading role" from constitution |
| 7 Dec | Central Round Table (government, SED, bloc parties, opposition, churches as moderators) |
| Dec–Jan | Citizens occupy Stasi offices (Erfurt 4 Dec; Berlin headquarters 15 Jan 1990) to stop file destruction |
| 28 Nov 1989 | Kohl's Ten-Point Plan (confederative structures → unity) |

| 3 Oct 1990 | Accession of the five re-created eastern Länder and East Berlin; Germany united |
| 2 Dec 1990 | First all-German Bundestag election |

### Regime Matrix — the transitional GDR (Nov 1989 – Oct 1990)

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Formally ruled | Volkskammer and Council of Ministers (Modrow, then de Maizière). |
| 2 | Actually held power | Nov 1989–Mar 1990: shared/contested — reform-SED government, Round Tables, street; after March 1990: an elected government negotiating with Bonn, which held the economic and diplomatic cards. |
| 3 | Acquired | Revolution; then first free elections. |
| 4 | Maintained | Negotiation; legitimacy of elections; West German support. |
| 5 | Challengers | Continued emigration (~2,000 per day in early 1990) forced speed. |
| 6 | Money | Collapsing GDR finances; FRG funds. |
| 7 | Enforcement | Old police under new control; Stasi dissolved (Office for National Security dissolved Dec 1989–1990). |
| 8 | Representation | Free elections (March and May 1990 local). |
| 9 | Rights | Freedom of travel, press, assembly established de facto from Nov 1989. |
| 10 | Opponents | None persecuted; old leaders investigated. |
| 11 | Institutions | Round tables, churches, new parties, citizens' committees. |
| 12 | Ordinary person's view | Euphoria, uncertainty, the D-Mark, then job fears. |

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** Until 1 December 1989 the GDR constitution still guaranteed the SED's "leading role", and the Volkskammer and Council of Ministers remained the formal organs of state throughout. In practice, from October 1989 power shifted to the streets and the Round Tables and, after the March 1990 election, to negotiations in which Bonn held the economic and diplomatic cards.

**Constitutional choice: Art. 23 vs Art. 146.** The Basic Law offered two paths: **accession** of new Länder under Art. 23 (quick, adopting the Basic Law) or a **new constitution** adopted by the whole German people under Art. 146 (slower, more participatory — favoured by many civil-rights activists and the Round Table's draft constitution). The March 1990 electorate chose parties favouring Art. 23. Historian Dierk Hoffmann (bpb 2024) notes the opportunity for a jointly made constitution was missed — a point often cited in explaining later East German feelings of having been "annexed." After 1990 the Basic Law was amended (1994 constitutional reform), Art. 146 remained.

### The international settlement: Two-plus-Four Treaty (12 Sept 1990)

- United Germany = FRG + GDR + Berlin; borders **final**, incl. Oder–Neisse (confirmed in a German-Polish treaty, Nov 1990).
- Armed forces capped at **370,000**; renunciation of nuclear, biological and chemical weapons.
- Soviet troops to leave by end of 1994 (completed August 1994); no foreign NATO troops or nuclear weapons in the former GDR.
- Germany free to choose alliances — remained in NATO.
- Four-power rights terminated — full sovereignty (15 March 1991 on ratification).
- Gorbachev's consent (July 1990 Kohl–Gorbachev meeting in the Caucasus) was eased by West German credits and payments for Soviet troop withdrawal.
- **Controversy:** Whether Western leaders promised in 1990 not to expand NATO eastward — verbal assurances were made in talks (e.g., Baker's "not one inch" February 1990 discussions), but no written commitment; historians (Mary Elise Sarotte, *Not One Inch*, 2021) document the exchanges; interpretations remain politically charged. **CONTESTED.**

## 3. Society

- **The revolution's carriers:** Initially small church-based groups (peace, environment, human rights — e.g., Umweltbibliothek Berlin, Leipzig Nikolaikirche peace prayers), then mass participation of ordinary citizens, including many SED members. The exodus and the protests were two different responses ("exit" and "voice," in Albert Hirschman's terms) that reinforced each other.
- **Elites:** SED leadership divided and paralysed; security forces chose not to shoot (no order from Berlin; local leaders in Leipzig, incl. conductor Kurt Masur's appeal, helped de-escalate).
- **Opinion shift:** From "We are the people" (reform of the GDR) to "We are one people" (unification) — by December 1989 majorities favoured unification; the civil-rights activists who wanted a reformed, democratic GDR became a minority.

## 4. Everyday Life

November 1989: millions visited the West; "welcome money" (100 DM). 1990: the D-Mark, Western goods, used cars; rapid collapse of GDR firms that could not compete; job losses; new bureaucracy, laws, forms; West German officials arriving to rebuild administration.

## 5. Economy

- Currency union at 1:1 for wages boosted purchasing power but, combined with the collapse of East European markets and low productivity, made much of GDR industry uncompetitive overnight.
- The **Treuhandanstalt** (created by the Modrow government in March 1990 to hold state property; transformed into a privatisation agency) took over ~8,500 enterprises with ~4 million employees (Böick, bpb). See `contemporary/01_post_reunification.md`.

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

- **Civil-rights movement:** Democratic socialism, ecology, grassroots democracy, a "third way."
- **West German mainstream:** Unity under the Basic Law and social market economy; Kohl's promise of "blooming landscapes."
- **Doubts abroad:** Thatcher and Mitterrand initially worried about a large Germany; resolved through Two-plus-Four and Germany's deeper commitment to European integration (Maastricht, euro).
- **"Wende" vs "revolution":** The term *Wende* (turn) was used by Krenz for his own reform attempt; many historians and activists prefer "Peaceful Revolution" to stress citizens' agency.

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE (early 1989):** A stagnant GDR with a leadership rejecting Soviet reform.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Gorbachev; economic crisis; Hungary's open border; election fraud; opposition groups.
↓
**TRANSITION (Sept 1989 – Oct 1990):**
- Crisis: exodus + mass demonstrations (Sept–Oct 1989) → Leipzig 9 Oct → Honecker's fall → Wall opens 9 Nov.
- Settlement: Round Tables; free elections; currency union; treaties.
↓
**AFTER:** United Germany (3 Oct 1990) under the Basic Law.

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

East: freedom of movement, speech, assembly; free elections; Western consumer goods; the D-Mark — and mass unemployment, devaluation of qualifications and biographies, new rules everywhere. West: solidarity tax, new Länder, change of capital (Berlin), a larger country.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

The FRG's institutions (Basic Law, federalism, social market economy, alliances) — extended eastward almost unchanged. This continuity was both the strength and the grievance of unification.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| The fall of the Wall was planned | It resulted from a botched announcement and crowds pressing at checkpoints. |
| Reunification was the goal of the protesters from the start | Early protests sought reform of the GDR; unification became the majority demand from late 1989. |
| The West "annexed" the GDR | Accession was decided by a freely elected Volkskammer; but the choice of Art. 23 and the speed limited East German co-design. |
| Kohl or Gorbachev "made" 1989 | Both mattered, but East German citizens' exit and protests drove events. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **Revolution, collapse or implosion?** Emphasis on citizens' agency (Ilko-Sascha Kowalczuk, *Endspiel*, 2009) vs structural collapse (economic bankruptcy, Soviet withdrawal — e.g., Stephen Kotkin, *Uncivil Society*, 2009, stressing the elites' implosion).
- **Speed and form of unification:** Necessary (emigration pressure, Gorbachev's fragile position) vs avoidable costs (currency conversion rate; Treuhand; Art. 23). Hoffmann (bpb 2024) emphasises openness and missed chances.
- **NATO expansion assurances:** Sarotte and others document verbal exchanges; no binding agreement; politically instrumentalised by Russia.

### If you remember only 5 things

1. The GDR fell because Moscow stopped protecting it, its people fled, and its people marched — all at once.
2. Leipzig on 9 October 1989, when the state did not shoot, was the turning point.
3. The Wall opened by accident; the revolution's direction shifted from reform to unity within weeks.
4. East Germans voted for fast unification by joining the Federal Republic under its existing constitution.
5. The international deal (Two-plus-Four) gave Germany full sovereignty in exchange for final borders, arms limits and NATO restraint in the East.
