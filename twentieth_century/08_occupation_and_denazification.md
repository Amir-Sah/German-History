## 1. Period in One Paragraph

In May 1945 there was no German government. The four Allies (US, UK, USSR, France) assumed supreme authority, divided Germany into four zones and Berlin into four sectors, and at Potsdam (July–August 1945) agreed on the "four Ds" — denazification, demilitarisation, democratisation, decentralisation (plus decartelisation) — and on the transfer of German territories east of the Oder–Neisse line to Polish and Soviet administration. Some 12 million Germans fled or were expelled from Eastern Europe. Society was shattered: cities in ruins, millions of refugees and displaced persons, hunger (the "hunger winter" 1946–47), black markets, and families waiting for prisoners of war. The Allies tried and punished Nazi leaders (Nuremberg, 1945–46) and screened millions of Germans. But their policies diverged: the Soviet zone used denazification to carry out a social revolution (land reform, expropriation, SED dictatorship), while the Western zones moved from strict purges to mass rehabilitation as the Cold War made West Germany an ally. By 1949 two German states existed.

## 2. The Political Structure

### Regime Matrix — Allied occupation

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Formally ruled | Allied Control Council (Berlin) for Germany as a whole; military governors in each zone. |
| 2 | Actually held power | Each zonal commander in his zone (Control Council required unanimity and soon deadlocked). |
| 3 | Acquired | Conquest and unconditional surrender; Berlin Declaration (5 June 1945). |
| 4 | Maintained | Military occupation; licensing of parties, newspapers, unions; control of food supplies. |
| 5 | Who could challenge | Other Allies; German politicians only within limits. |
| 6 | Money | Occupation costs paid by Germans; reparations (especially Soviet dismantling of factories). |
| 7 | Enforcement | Military government, re-established German police and courts under supervision. |
| 8 | Representation | Local and state (*Land*) elections from 1946; parties licensed (SPD, KPD, CDU/CSU, liberals). |
| 9 | Rights | Limited; freedom from Nazi terror but subject to occupation law, censorship of Nazi content. |
| 10 | Opponents | Werewolf resistance negligible; Soviet special camps (NKVD) held ~150,000+ Germans (many Nazis, also innocents and later political opponents), with ~40,000+ deaths. |
| 11 | Connecting institutions | Re-founded municipalities, churches (regarded by Allies as relatively untainted), licensed press, *Länder*. |
| 12 | Ordinary person's view | Hunger, cold, housing shortage, black market, the questionnaire (*Fragebogen*), the tribunal (*Spruchkammer*), waiting for POWs. |

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** Potsdam envisaged treating Germany as a single economic unit; in practice, zones became separate economies within two years, and the US–UK Bizone (1947) and currency reform (1948) laid foundations for a Western state.

### Denazification — what happened

**Nuremberg Trials:** International Military Tribunal (1945–46): 22 major defendants, 12 death sentences; followed by 12 US successor trials (doctors, judges, industrialists, Einsatzgruppen, generals). Established precedents for crimes against humanity.

**Mass screening (Western zones):**
- **US zone:** Law for Liberation from National Socialism and Militarism (5 March 1946) required every adult to fill in a questionnaire; German tribunals (*Spruchkammern*) sorted people into five categories: I Major offenders, II Offenders, III Lesser offenders, IV Followers (*Mitläufer*), V Exonerated. GHDI data: of ~3.6 million people affected in the US zone, ~950,000 cases went before tribunals and ~2.5 million were amnestied; only ~2.5% ended in categories I–II. The tribunals were mocked as "follower factories" (*Mitläuferfabrik*); exonerating affidavits were nicknamed "Persil certificates" (after the detergent).
- **British zone:** Focused on public employees and applicants; no population-wide registration; German committees advisory.
- **French zone:** Most lenient, focused on senior officials and teachers; amnesty of ordinary party members in July 1948.
- **Statistics caveat:** GHDI notes that comparable statistics across zones encounter "insurmountable difficulties."

**Soviet zone:** Mass dismissals (e.g., ~80% of judges and prosecutors; many teachers replaced by rapidly trained "new teachers"); used to break old elites; land reform (1945: estates over 100 ha expropriated without compensation — the Junker class destroyed); industrial expropriation; by February 1948 about half of Saxony's public employees were SED members (Bundestag research service). Denazification declared complete in 1948; former nominal Nazis integrated via a special party (NDPD).

**End of Western denazification:** By 1948 rehabilitation became the West German political consensus; Article 131 of the Basic Law and the 1951 "131 Law" reinstated most dismissed civil servants; amnesty laws (1949, 1954). The judiciary, foreign office, police and ministries rehired many former Nazis (e.g., studies of the Foreign Office, 2010, and Justice Ministry, 2016 — the "Rosenburg" files: in the 1950s–60s a majority of senior Justice Ministry officials were former NSDAP members).

## 3. Society

- **Refugees and expellees:** ~12 million Germans from Eastern Europe (East Prussia, Silesia, Pomerania, Sudetenland, and German communities elsewhere); estimates of deaths during flight and expulsion range from ~400,000 to 2 million (LeMO: "unclear to this day"). By 1950 ~8 million lived in West Germany and ~4 million in East Germany (where they were called "resettlers," *Umsiedler*, and could not publicly mourn lost homelands). Schleswig-Holstein's population rose by a third, Mecklenburg's by ~44%.
- **Displaced persons (DPs):** ~8–10 million former forced labourers and camp survivors in 1945; most repatriated; Jewish DPs in camps until the late 1940s/1950s.
- **Women:** A demographic "surplus of women"; household survival work, black market, rubble clearing; mass rapes in the Soviet zone.
- **Prisoners of war:** ~11 million German POWs in Allied hands; the last from the USSR returned in 1955–56.

## 4. Everyday Life

Rations in the Western zones fell to ~1,000–1,500 calories per day at times; "hamster trips" to the countryside to barter; cigarettes as currency; coal theft (Cardinal Frings's 1946 sermon gave it the name *fringsen*); housing shared with refugees by requisition.

## 5. Economy

Reparations and dismantling (heaviest in the Soviet zone); Western currency reform (20 June 1948) and price decontrol (Ludwig Erhard) — shops filled overnight; Soviet counter-reform and the **Berlin Blockade** (June 1948 – May 1949) answered by the Airlift. Marshall Plan (ERP) aid to the Western zones from 1948.

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

- **Allied re-education:** Democracy courses, US Amerika-Häuser, licensed press; Soviet "antifascist-democratic" order.
- **German attitudes:** Surveys by the US military government (OMGUS) found persistent sympathy for National Socialism "as a good idea badly carried out" among a substantial share of respondents in the late 1940s — **MEDIUM** reliability, but corroborated by later polling. Widespread self-perception as victims; collective guilt rejected (Karl Jaspers's *The Question of German Guilt*, 1946, distinguished criminal, political, moral and metaphysical guilt).
- **Churches:** Stuttgart Declaration of Guilt (Protestant, Oct 1945) — vague but significant.

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

The Cold War split between the Soviet Union and the Western Allies; economic collapse, hunger and a worthless currency; some 12 million refugees and expellees to house and feed; conflict over reparations and over Soviet vs Western models for Germany; the Western Allies' growing need for a stable West German partner.

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE (May 1945):** No German state; Allied supreme authority.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Cold War; economic collapse; refugees; Soviet vs Western models.
↓
**TRANSITION (crisis):** 1947 Bizone; Marshall Plan; 1948 currency reforms; Berlin Blockade.
↓
**AFTER (1949):** Federal Republic (May, West) and GDR (October, East).

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

Survival struggles; the experience of being classified and judged; for expellees, loss of home; for East Germans, land reform and a new dictatorship; for West Germans, return to relative normality by 1948–49.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

In the West: many elites and professionals (judges, doctors, officials, business leaders); private property; churches. In the East: a new elite replaced much of the old — but dictatorship and a secret police returned in new form.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| 1945 was a "zero hour" (*Stunde Null*) | Much continuity in people, institutions, mentalities (esp. in the West). |
| Denazification was a complete failure | It removed top Nazis, delegitimised Nazism publicly, and screened millions; but most perpetrators escaped punishment. |
| The Soviet zone denazified more thoroughly and therefore better | It was more thorough in removing elites but used denazification to build a dictatorship. |
| The expulsions were spontaneous revenge only | They were agreed in principle at Potsdam ("orderly and humane" — rarely the reality). |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **Success or failure of denazification:** The traditional verdict "administratively successful, ideologically failed" (Bundestag WD summary) vs revisionist work (Mikkel Dack, *Everyday Denazification in Postwar Germany*, CUP 2023) arguing that screening via the *Fragebogen* had real effects on political life and elite recruitment.
- **Continuities of personnel:** Norbert Frei's *Vergangenheitspolitik* (1996) on Adenauer-era amnesty and integration; debate whether integrating former Nazis stabilised democracy (Hermann Lübbe's "communicative silence" thesis, 1983) or damaged it.
- **Victimhood narratives** of expellees vs the context of German crimes.

### If you remember only 5 things

1. In 1945 the German state disappeared; four Allies ruled zones that drifted into two blocs.
2. Twelve million expellees reshaped German society, especially in the north and east.
3. Denazification tried everyone, punished few, and ended in the West with mass rehabilitation.
4. In the East, denazification became the lever for a social revolution and a new dictatorship.
5. "Zero hour" is a myth: people, institutions and attitudes carried over — especially in the West.
