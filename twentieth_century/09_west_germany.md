## 1. Period in One Paragraph

The Federal Republic of Germany (FRG) was designed as a "provisional" state and became the most stable democracy in German history. Its constitution, the **Basic Law** (1949), was written by a Parliamentary Council of state politicians under Allied supervision, explicitly to avoid Weimar's failures: human dignity as the first article, directly enforceable rights, a strong Chancellor who can only be removed by electing a successor, a ceremonial President, a powerful Constitutional Court, federalism, and "militant democracy" able to ban anti-constitutional parties. Under Konrad Adenauer (CDU, 1949–63) it integrated into the West (NATO 1955, European Economic Community 1957), rearmed, and experienced the "economic miracle." It also absorbed 8 million expellees, reintegrated most former Nazis, and at first avoided confronting the Nazi past. From the 1960s, the Auschwitz trials, the student movement of 1968, the SPD-led governments of Willy Brandt (Ostpolitik, reforms) and Helmut Schmidt, terrorism (RAF), new social movements (environment, women, peace), and the arrival of millions of "guest workers" transformed society. By 1989 the FRG was a prosperous, liberal, Western-oriented democracy with a strong welfare state — and the magnet that pulled East Germans west.

## 2. The Political Structure

### Regime Matrix — Federal Republic (Bonn Republic)

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Formally ruled | The people through the **Bundestag** (mixed-member proportional representation; 5% threshold from 1953); the **Federal Chancellor** elected by the Bundestag; the **Bundesrat** (state governments) co-legislating on matters affecting the Länder; the **Federal President** (elected by the Federal Convention, largely ceremonial). |
| 2 | Actually held power | Chancellor ("chancellor democracy" under Adenauer) and coalition parties; party leaderships; Länder governments (through the Bundesrat); the Federal Constitutional Court as a powerful veto player; organised interests (unions, employers, churches) in corporatist bargaining; the Bundesbank on monetary policy; Allied reserved rights (until 1990 over Berlin and Germany as a whole). |
| 3 | Power acquired | Free elections and coalition negotiations. |
| 4 | Power maintained | Economic success; welfare state; broad catch-all parties (CDU/CSU, SPD, FDP); Western integration. |
| 5 | Who could challenge | Opposition parties; courts; the press ("Spiegel affair" 1962 — defence minister Strauss forced out after police action against the magazine); extra-parliamentary movements; the Länder. |
| 6 | Money | Income, corporate and consumption taxes shared between federal, state and local levels; social insurance contributions. |
| 7 | Enforcement | Rule-of-law bureaucracy and police (Länder), independent courts. |
| 8 | Representation | Universal suffrage (21+, from 1970 18+); parties with public financing. |
| 9 | Rights | Directly binding basic rights; constitutional complaint to the Constitutional Court (from 1951). |
| 10 | Opponents | Lawful opposition free; but "militant democracy": bans of the neo-Nazi SRP (1952) and the Communist KPD (1956); the 1972 "Radicals Decree" (*Radikalenerlass*) screening public employees for loyalty (~1.4 million checks; ~1,000–2,000 rejections — estimates); anti-terror laws in the 1970s. |
| 11 | Connecting institutions | Parties, unions (DGB), churches (church tax collected by the state), associations, co-determination in companies, local self-government. |
| 12 | Ordinary person's view | Rising prosperity, the car, the holiday in Italy, stable jobs; a paternalistic, conservative 1950s giving way to liberalisation in the 1960s–70s. |

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:**
- *Said:* The Basic Law was "provisional" (Preamble; Art. 146) until Germans could decide freely on unity; Art. 23 allowed other parts of Germany to accede. Parliamentary democracy with strong checks.
- *Worked:* It became permanent in all but name. The **constructive vote of no confidence** (Art. 67) was used successfully only once (1982, Kohl replacing Schmidt). The **Constitutional Court** became one of the world's most influential courts — shaping abortion law (1975), party finance, codetermination, census/data protection ("informational self-determination," 1983). The **Bundesrat** gave opposition-led Länder a veto on many laws, producing a consensus-oriented "grand coalition state" (Manfred G. Schmidt). **Corporatism** — unions and employers negotiating wages autonomously (*Tarifautonomie*) and co-determination (1951 coal/steel; 1976 large firms) — institutionalised class compromise.

### Design features of the Basic Law as lessons from Weimar (Feldkamp, bpb)

| Weimar problem | Basic Law response |
|---|---|
| Rights could be suspended; programmatic | Art. 1 human dignity; rights "directly applicable law" binding all state power (Art. 1(3)) |
| Strong President with emergency powers (Art. 48) | Weak, indirectly elected President; no presidential emergency decree |
| Governments toppled by negative majorities | Constructive vote of no confidence (Art. 67) |
| Anti-democratic parties used democracy to destroy it | Party bans by the Constitutional Court (Art. 21); forfeiture of rights (Art. 18) — "militant democracy" |
| Constitution could be legally hollowed out (Enabling Act) | Eternity clause (Art. 79(3)): human dignity, democracy, federalism, rule of law unamendable |
| Plebiscites used demagogically | No federal referenda (except territorial reorganisation) |
| Splinter parties | 5% threshold (electoral law) |

## 3. Society

- **Expellees:** 8 million integrated through housing programmes and the Equalisation of Burdens Act (*Lastenausgleich*, 1952); their associations became a conservative political force.
- **Class:** "Levelled middle-class society" (Helmut Schelsky's 1953 phrase — contested; inequality of wealth persisted) — rising wages, mass consumption, social mobility via education expansion in the 1960s–70s.
- **Religion:** Roughly equal Protestants and Catholics (the Catholic share rose after losing the Protestant east); churches influential in the 1950s; secularisation from the 1960s.
- **Women:** Art. 3(2) "Men and women have equal rights" (thanks to Elisabeth Selbert); but the Civil Code kept the "housewife marriage": the 1957 Equality Act still allowed wives to work only "insofar as this is compatible with duties in marriage and family"; only the **1977 marriage-law reform** removed that model. Women's movement from the late 1960s (abortion campaign 1971: "We've had abortions!" — *Stern*). Female employment below that of the GDR.
- **Guest workers (*Gastarbeiter*):** Recruitment agreements with Italy (1955), Spain and Greece (1960), **Turkey (1961)**, Morocco, Portugal, Tunisia, Yugoslavia (1968). Recruitment stop in November 1973 (oil crisis) — paradoxically led to settlement and family reunification. Official doctrine until the late 1990s: "Germany is not a country of immigration."
- **Former Nazis:** Integrated at all levels (e.g., Hans Globke, co-commentator on the Nuremberg Laws, as Adenauer's chief of staff; most judges); prosecutions limited until the Ludwigsburg Central Office (1958) and the Frankfurt Auschwitz trial (1963–65, Fritz Bauer).
- **Homosexuality:** The Nazi-era version of Paragraph 175 remained until 1969 (~50,000 convictions in the FRG 1949–69); fully repealed 1994.

## 4. Everyday Life

1950s: reconstruction, full employment by the late 1950s, the "Wirtschaftswunder" consumption wave (food → furniture → car → travel), 48-hour weeks becoming 40-hour weeks (1960s–70s), conservative family values, *Heimat* films. 1960s–80s: mass higher education, youth culture, rock music, sexual liberalisation, TV, more leisure, home-ownership, environmental awareness (Greens founded 1980), peace movement (1981–83 against Pershing missiles).

## 5. Economy

**Social market economy** (*Soziale Marktwirtschaft* — Alfred Müller-Armack's term; Ludwig Erhard as economics minister): markets and competition (cartel law 1957) + social insurance + co-determination + an independent central bank (Bundesbank, 1957) guarding price stability. Export-oriented manufacturing (cars, machinery, chemicals) — "Modell Deutschland."

**Why the "economic miracle"?** Debated:
- **Reconstruction thesis (Werner Abelshauser):** High growth was largely catch-up to a pre-war trend: skilled labour, a surviving capital stock (industrial capacity was less destroyed than cities), and pent-up demand.
- **Policy** (currency reform, Erhard's decontrol), **Marshall Plan** (important for foreign exchange and confidence, but modest in size relative to GDP), **Korean War boom** (1950–51), **European integration**, **expellee labour**, undervalued currency (Bretton Woods).
- Most economic historians give the Marshall Plan a limited direct role and emphasise catch-up and institutions.

After 1973: oil shocks, unemployment rising (over 2 million in the 1980s), deindustrialisation in the Ruhr and shipbuilding.

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

- **Christian Democracy:** Cross-confessional, anti-communist, social-market, pro-Western (Adenauer).
- **Social Democracy:** Godesberg Programme (1959) — abandoned Marxism, accepted market economy and Western integration; became a people's party.
- **Liberalism (FDP):** kingmaker party.
- **Anti-communism** as state doctrine.
- **"Coming to terms with the past"** (*Vergangenheitsbewältigung*): from silence and self-victimisation (1950s) to confrontation (1960s–80s): Auschwitz trial, 1968 generation's accusations against parents, TV series *Holocaust* (1979), President Weizsäcker's speech (8 May 1985) calling 8 May a "day of liberation," the **Historikerstreit (1986)** (Ernst Nolte suggested the Holocaust was a reaction to Bolshevik crimes; Jürgen Habermas and most historians rejected this relativisation).
- **Constitutional patriotism** (*Verfassungspatriotismus*, Dolf Sternberger/Habermas): loyalty to the Basic Law's values rather than to an ethnic nation.
- **1968 and after:** anti-authoritarianism, New Left, feminism, environmentalism; and a violent fringe (Red Army Faction, 1970–98: 34 murders).
- **Ordinary opinions:** Polls show declining approval of Nazi-era ideas, rising support for democracy by the 1960s (Almond & Verba found low "civic culture" in 1959; by the 1980s West Germans resembled other Western democracies).

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

Generational revolt; economic slowdown (1970s–80s); unemployment; environmental crisis; the East German crisis and exodus of 1989.

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE (1945–49):** Occupation zones.
↓
**PRESSURES:** The Cold War split; economic collapse and the 1948 currency reform; the Western Allies' demand for a West German state (Frankfurt Documents, 1948).
↓
**TRANSITION (1949 and after):**
- Founding: Basic Law (23 May 1949); first Bundestag election (Aug 1949).
- Development: Western integration (1950s) → liberalisation (1960s–70s) → consolidation (1980s).
↓
**AFTER (1990):** Accession of the GDR under Art. 23; the Basic Law becomes the constitution of united Germany (see `twentieth_century/12_reunification.md`).

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

Democracy, rule of law, prosperity, social security, freedom of movement and expression; a new relationship to authority (1960s); from ethnic homogeneity toward a de facto immigration society.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

Federalism; civil service traditions (*Berufsbeamtentum*, Art. 33(5)); church–state cooperation (church tax); social insurance model (Bismarckian); many personnel from the Nazi period in the early decades; export-oriented industry.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| West Germany confronted its Nazi past immediately | The 1950s were marked by amnesty and integration; confrontation came gradually from the late 1950s. |
| The Marshall Plan created the economic miracle | It helped, but catch-up growth, institutions and labour mattered more. |
| 1968 created democracy in Germany | Democratic institutions were already stable; 1968 accelerated cultural liberalisation (and also produced dogmatism and violence). |
| Guest workers were meant to stay | Policy assumed rotation; settlement was unplanned and long unacknowledged. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **"Successful democracy" narrative** (Edgar Wolfrum, *Die geglückte Demokratie*, 2006) vs critical accounts emphasising continuities of Nazi personnel, authoritarian mentalities and restrictive policies (e.g., Radicals Decree, treatment of migrants).

  *In plain words:* Was West Germany above all a success story? Edgar Wolfrum's account, Die geglückte Demokratie ('The Successful Democracy', 2006), says yes. Critical accounts point to what carried on from the Nazi era — former Nazi officials in office and authoritarian habits of mind — and to restrictive policies such as the Radicals Decree (loyalty checks on public employees) and the treatment of migrants.
- **Westernisation vs Americanisation** (Anselm Doering-Manteuffel): how deeply Western political culture was adopted.

  *In plain words:* How deeply did West Germans really take on Western political culture after 1945? The debate, shaped by historian Anselm Doering-Manteuffel, weighs two labels: 'Westernisation' (adopting the political values shared across Western Europe and North America) versus 'Americanisation' (influence coming specifically from the United States) — and asks how far the change went beneath the surface.
- **Integration of former Nazis:** Lübbe's "communicative silence" as stabilising vs Frei's critique.

  *In plain words:* Was it right that the early Federal Republic quietly took former Nazis back into jobs and public life? Lübbe argued that this 'communicative silence' — not openly discussing individual Nazi pasts — helped stabilise the new democracy. Historian Norbert Frei criticises this view of how the young republic handled its former Nazis.
- **1968:** Liberalisation catalyst (Wolfgang Kraushaar) vs overrated myth (Götz Aly's *Unser Kampf*, 2008 — controversial comparison with 1933).

  *In plain words:* How important was the 1968 student movement? Wolfgang Kraushaar sees it as a catalyst that sped up the liberalisation (loosening and opening up) of West German society. Götz Aly, in Unser Kampf (2008), calls its importance an overrated myth; his book is controversial because it draws a comparison with 1933, the year the Nazis came to power.

### If you remember only 5 things

1. The Basic Law was built as an anti-Weimar machine: strong rights, strong chancellor, weak president, powerful constitutional court, militant democracy.
2. Western integration and economic growth gave the new democracy legitimacy.
3. The early Federal Republic reintegrated former Nazis and confronted the past only gradually.
4. The 1960s–70s liberalised society; guest workers turned it into an immigration society without admitting it.
5. Power was dispersed — Länder, courts, unions, employers, central bank — producing a consensus democracy.
