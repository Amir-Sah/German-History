Three things the map shows at a glance:

1. **"Germany" as a single state is young.** For most of history the German-speaking world was a patchwork of polities under a loose Empire. A German nation-state existed from 1871 to 1945 — and then again only from 1990.
2. **The story is not a straight line.** It branches (Austria out in 1866; East and West in 1949) and loops (federalism returning after dictatorships).
3. **The biggest ruptures came from war** — 1618, 1806, 1918, 1945 — and were followed by rebuilt institutions.

## Part 1 — A world without "Germany" (to 919)

<!-- IMAGES:START -->
!["Caster Ware" Vase with Hunt Scene](https://openaccess-cdn.clevelandart.org/1992.126/1992.126_web.jpg)
Pottery made in Roman Cologne on the Rhine frontier. Workshops like this supplied soldiers and townspeople on both sides of the Limes (the Roman frontier line).  
<sub>"Caster Ware" Vase with Hunt Scene — Workshop in Cologne (Colonia Claudia Ara Agrippinensium), mid-100s CE. Source: [Cleveland Museum of Art, Open Access](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1992.126). Public domain (CC0). [Image index](images/IMAGE_INDEX.md#caster-vase)</sub>
<!-- IMAGES:END -->

**Why start here?** Because the first trap in German history is to imagine that "the Germans" were always there, waiting to become a nation. They weren't.

Roughly two thousand years ago, the lands between the Rhine and the Elbe were home to many small farming peoples who spoke related languages. The Romans lumped them together as ***Germani*** — a label Julius Caesar found useful to explain why he stopped conquering at the Rhine. The most famous Roman description, Tacitus's *Germania*, painted them as noble savages to shame Roman luxury. Nobody on the ground called themselves "Germanic."

Rome ruled the south and west for about five centuries. The frontier — the **Limes** — was not a wall but a controlled border where goods, soldiers and ideas crossed. Rome brought cities (Cologne, Trier, Mainz, Augsburg, Regensburg), roads, wine and, later, Christianity. Beyond the frontier, contact with Rome — trade, pay for mercenaries, raids — made local war leaders richer and helped larger confederations form: Franks, Alamanni, Saxons.

When Roman power in the West dissolved in the 5th century, it was the **Franks** who picked up the pieces. Their kings converted to Latin Christianity and, over three centuries, conquered almost all of the later German lands — Saxony only after thirty years of brutal war under **Charlemagne**, who was crowned emperor in Rome in 800. Carolingian government worked through personal loyalty, travelling kings, local counts and, above all, **bishops and monasteries**, the only institutions that could read, write and keep records.

When Charlemagne's grandsons split the empire (843), the eastern part — **East Francia** — became the seed of a German kingdom. Even the word *deutsch* began as a description of a **language** (the "people's tongue" as opposed to Latin), not of a nation.

> **If you remember only 5 things (to 900)**
> 1. "Germanic peoples" were many shifting groups; the label was Roman.
> 2. Half of today's Germany was Roman for five centuries — and that line still shows in its oldest cities.
> 3. The Franks, not "Germans," united the lands that would become Germany.
> 4. Early medieval rule meant personal bonds plus the Church's administration.
> 5. "German" first meant a language, not a people.

## Part 2 — The Holy Roman Empire: a different kind of order (919–1806)

<!-- IMAGES:START -->
![Leaf from the Nuremberg Chronicle (Schedelsche Weltchronik)](https://openaccess-cdn.clevelandart.org/1998.397/1998.397_web.jpg)
The Nuremberg Chronicle, one of the great early printed books, filled with woodcut views of cities. It shows how proud imperial cities (*Reichsstädte*) imagined themselves: walled, spired, and self-governing.  
<sub>Leaf from the Nuremberg Chronicle (*Schedelsche Weltchronik*) — Michael Wolgemut and workshop (woodcuts); Hartmann Schedel (text), 1493. Source: [Cleveland Museum of Art, Open Access](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1998.397). Public domain (CC0). [Image index](images/IMAGE_INDEX.md#nuremberg-chronicle)</sub>
<!-- IMAGES:END -->

**Why did the Empire survive so long without becoming a nation-state?** Because it was never trying to be one.

In 919 the dukes of East Francia elected a Saxon, Henry I, as king. His son **Otto I** was crowned emperor in Rome in 962. From then on, the German king was also a candidate for the **Roman imperial** crown — a universal, Christian title, not a national one. The resulting "Holy Roman Empire" at various times included Bohemia, Austria, the Low Countries, Burgundy and northern Italy.

How did it work? Think of it as a **federation of lordships** held together by law, ritual and shared institutions:

- **The king was elected.** By the 13th century a small group of **prince-electors** chose him; the **Golden Bull of 1356** fixed their number at seven (three archbishops, four secular princes). An elected king had to bargain.
- **Kings had no capital.** They travelled constantly, ruling through assemblies of great men.
- **Bishops were princes.** German kings gave bishoprics lands and powers to use them as reliable administrators. When the papacy challenged this in the **Investiture Controversy** (1075–1122 — the episode of Canossa), kings lost sacred control over the Church, and princes gained leverage.
- **Real power was local.** Most people lived under a **lord** — a noble, a bishop, an abbey or a town — who held rights over land, courts and sometimes their persons. For a peasant, the "state" was the lord, the village and the priest.
- **Towns were islands of different law.** Hundreds of towns, some answering only to the emperor ("imperial cities"), governed themselves through councils and guilds; merchant networks like the **Hanse** organised trade without being a state.

By 1500, the emperor was almost always a **Habsburg**, strong because of his family lands (Austria, later Spain and the Netherlands), not because of the Empire itself. The **Imperial Reform of 1495** replaced legal feuding with a public peace and a supreme court.

Then came the **Reformation**. When Martin Luther challenged the Church in 1517, printing turned his protest into a mass movement. Different people heard different things in it: princes saw control of church property; townspeople saw freedom from clerical privilege; peasants saw divine justice — and rose in the **Peasants' War** (1524–26), which the princes crushed (Luther urged them on). The lasting political result: **princes became heads of their territories' churches**, and the **Peace of Augsburg (1555)** let each ruler choose his territory's faith. Religion became territorial — and your pastor became, among other things, a state official.

Unresolved religious and constitutional disputes exploded in the **Thirty Years' War (1618–48)**, which drew in Sweden, France and Spain. Armies lived off the land; disease and hunger followed them. Somewhere between a fifth and two-fifths of the population died — with enormous regional variation. The **Peace of Westphalia (1648)** did *not*, as textbooks often say, invent modern "sovereign states"; it rebuilt the Empire with religious parity and confirmed princes' territorial rights *within* imperial law.

For the next 150 years the Empire was a **permanent congress** (the Diet at Regensburg), two supreme courts where even peasants could sue their rulers, and regional defence circles. Real government happened in some 300 territories, from tiny imperial knightships to great powers. Two of those powers outgrew the Empire:

- **Austria** (Habsburgs) — a vast, multi-ethnic monarchy that nearly always held the imperial crown.
- **Brandenburg-Prussia** (Hohenzollerns) — a poor, scattered state that built a huge army through a bargain: the **Junker** nobility served as officers and kept near-total control over their peasants; the king got soldiers and taxes. Frederick II seized Silesia from Austria in 1740, and **Austro-Prussian rivalry** became the axis of German politics.

Meanwhile, the **Enlightenment** in Germany was carried by professors, pastors and officials — often working *with* reforming princes rather than against them. Kant's famous formula captured the mood: "Argue as much as you like … but obey!" Without a German state, intellectuals imagined a German **cultural nation** of language and literature.

> **If you remember only 5 things (919–1806)**
> 1. The Empire was an elected, federal, law-bound order — not a failed nation-state.
> 2. Power lay with princes, bishops and towns; the emperor was strong only through his own dynasty.
> 3. The Reformation made religion a matter of territory and princes.
> 4. The Thirty Years' War devastated central Europe; Westphalia rebuilt the Empire rather than ending it.
> 5. Prussia's rise rested on a crown–Junker bargain; Austria and Prussia outgrew the Empire.

## Part 3 — Destruction and reinvention (1792–1866)

**Why did the old order collapse?** Not because Germans rose up, but because French armies knocked it over.

Revolutionary and Napoleonic France annexed the left bank of the Rhine. To compensate the princes who lost land there, the Empire's own deputation in 1803 **abolished almost all church states and most imperial cities**, handing them to bigger secular princes — about five million people got new rulers overnight. In 1806 sixteen princes left the Empire for Napoleon's **Confederation of the Rhine**, and Emperor Francis II laid down the crown. After roughly 850 years, the Holy Roman Empire was gone. Some Germans mourned; many barely noticed.

The winners — **Bavaria, Württemberg, Baden** — became kingdoms and grand duchies and modernised by decree: centralised ministries, legal equality, religious toleration, abolition of serfdom on paper. **Prussia**, crushed by Napoleon in 1806, reformed "from above" to survive: it abolished hereditary serfdom (1807), gave towns self-government, opened officer careers, introduced universal conscription, and founded a new university in Berlin. But the price of peasant freedom — ceding a third to a half of their land to their lords — made the **Junkers richer**, not weaker.

The "Wars of Liberation" (1813–15) were later remembered as a national uprising against Napoleon. Research shows that in much of Germany, ordinary people experienced them mainly as **conscription, requisitions and suffering**; the national legend was built afterwards.

The Congress of Vienna created a **German Confederation** (1815) — a league of about 39 sovereign states, including Austria and Prussia, with a single assembly of ambassadors in Frankfurt. Its main job was **keeping order**: after 1819 it censored the press, watched universities and hunted liberal "demagogues." The south German states got constitutions; Prussia and Austria did not.

Two forces grew anyway:

- **Nationalism + liberalism.** Students, gymnasts, singers, professors and businessmen wanted a united Germany *with* a constitution, rights and a free press — a movement of educated minorities, organised in clubs because parties were illegal. It had festivals (Hambach, 1832), a flag (black-red-gold) and a question it couldn't answer: "Where is the German's fatherland?" — with Austria? With Schleswig? With Polish Posen?
- **Economic integration.** A Prussian-led **Customs Union** (1834) and **railways** (from 1835) created a common market — without Austria.

By the 1840s, population growth had outrun jobs; **mass poverty** (*Pauperismus*) and a hunger crisis (1846–47) drove emigration and riots. In March 1848, following Paris, revolutions broke out everywhere. Frightened princes conceded. An elected **National Assembly** in Frankfurt wrote sweeping **basic rights** and a constitution for a federal empire with a parliament elected by all men. But the princes still controlled the **armies**; revolutionaries were divided (constitutional monarchy vs republic; with or without Austria); peasants, once freed from feudal dues, went home; and the King of Prussia refused a crown "from the gutter." By summer 1849 the revolution was crushed.

It was not a pointless failure: feudal burdens ended, Prussia kept a constitution (though with a three-class voting system weighted by taxes), and the Frankfurt constitution became the template for later German democracy.

> **If you remember only 5 things (1792–1866)**
> 1. French power, not German nationalism, destroyed the Holy Roman Empire.
> 2. The middle states and Prussia modernised "from above" — equality before the law without political participation.
> 3. The Confederation's job was order, not unity.
> 4. Nationalism was born liberal and oppositional: unity *and* freedom.
> 5. 1848 failed to unify Germany but defined its political agenda for a century.

## Part 4 — The German Empire: unity without full democracy (1866–1918)

<!-- IMAGES:START -->
![Huldigung an Kaiser Wilhelm I. (Homage to Emperor Wilhelm I)](https://www.dhm.de/lemo/fileadmin/medien/lemo/Titelbilder/96006594_2.jpg)
Celebration of the new German Emperor in 1871. The Empire was founded by princes and generals — proclaimed in Versailles, not voted by a parliament.  
<sub>Huldigung an Kaiser Wilhelm I. (Homage to Emperor Wilhelm I) — Paul Bürde, 1871. Source: [Deutsches Historisches Museum, Berlin (LeMO)](https://www.dhm.de/lemo/). © DHM — used with credit for personal study. [Image index](images/IMAGE_INDEX.md#kaiserproklamation)</sub>
<!-- IMAGES:END -->

**Why did unification happen when it did, and in that form?** Because Prussia had the army, the economy, a daring leader and a favourable European moment — and liberals were willing to trade freedom for unity.

In the 1860s Prussia's liberal parliament refused to fund King Wilhelm I's army reform. The king appointed **Otto von Bismarck**, who governed without a legal budget, declaring that the great questions would be settled "by iron and blood." He then fought three short wars: against **Denmark** (1864), against **Austria** and its German allies (1866), and against **France** (1870–71). The war of 1866 pushed Austria out of German affairs for good; Prussia annexed Hanover, Hesse-Kassel, Nassau and Frankfurt. Victory also bought forgiveness: the Prussian parliament retroactively approved Bismarck's illegal budgets, and many liberals switched to supporting him. The war against France brought the southern states in. On **18 January 1871**, in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles — before princes and officers, not parliamentarians — Wilhelm was proclaimed German Emperor.

**How did the new Empire work?**

| On paper | In practice |
|---|---|
| Sovereignty lay with the federal princes (the Bundesrat) | Prussia — two-thirds of the population — dominated; the Kaiser was King of Prussia |
| The Reichstag, elected by **all men over 25** in secret ballot, passed laws and budgets | The **Chancellor answered only to the Kaiser**; parliament could block but not govern |
| The army was funded through the budget | Army budgets were approved for 7 (later 5) years at a time; the Kaiser's command power was outside parliamentary control |

Historians call it "a system of avoided decisions": democratic and monarchical principles side by side, neither winning. It was also a **Rechtsstaat** (rule-of-law state) with courts, a mostly free press, and very high election turnout. Society was organised into stable **milieus** — Protestant-conservative, liberal, Catholic, socialist — each with its own party, newspapers and clubs.

The state tried to define the nation *against* "enemies of the Reich." It fought the **Catholic Church** in the *Kulturkampf* (1870s) — at one point a quarter of Catholic parishes in Prussia had no priest — and lost: Catholics rallied to their Centre Party. It banned **socialist** organisations (1878–90) — and the Social Democratic Party (SPD) kept growing, becoming Germany's largest party by 1912. Bismarck's answer included the carrot as well as the stick: the world's first **national social insurance** (health 1883, accidents 1884, pensions 1889). Poles in Prussia faced Germanisation; organised **antisemitism** appeared after the 1873 crash.

Meanwhile Germany became **Europe's leading industrial power** — coal and steel, then chemicals and electrical engineering. Cities exploded (8 cities over 100,000 in 1871; 48 by 1910). Millions moved west to the Ruhr or emigrated to America. It also acquired **colonies** in Africa and the Pacific, where the war against the Herero and Nama (1904–08) became a **genocide** — which the German government formally acknowledged in 2021.

**Was the Kaiserreich on a "special path" to Nazism?** This is one of the great debates. One school (the *Sonderweg* thesis) argues that powerful pre-industrial elites, an unaccountable army and weak parliamentarism set Germany apart from the West. Critics answer that there was no "normal" Western path, that German society was modern and democratising through its elections, and that reading the Empire backwards from 1933 distorts it. The evidence clearly supports one point: **the army's independence from parliament** was a real and dangerous feature — and it mattered enormously in 1914 and 1918.

**War and collapse.** In July 1914 Germany's leaders gave Austria-Hungary unconditional backing against Serbia, knowingly risking a European war. How much responsibility this places on Germany is debated — most specialists see German and Austro-Hungarian decisions as decisive within a system of escalation. The "war enthusiasm" of August 1914 was real in some city crowds but far from universal. The war killed about two million German soldiers (current best estimate 2,037,000); the British blockade and poor harvests brought hunger ("turnip winter" 1916/17). From 1916 the generals **Hindenburg and Ludendorff** effectively dominated politics.

In September 1918, **Ludendorff himself** told the Kaiser the war was lost and demanded an armistice and a parliamentary government — to shift blame onto civilians. When the navy ordered a suicidal last sortie, sailors mutinied at Kiel; councils spread; on **9 November 1918** the Kaiser abdicated and a republic was proclaimed. The generals then spread the **"stab-in-the-back" myth**: that the undefeated army had been betrayed at home by socialists and Jews. It was false — and it poisoned the new republic.

> **If you remember only 5 things (1866–1918)**
> 1. Germany was unified by Prussian wars and a princes' treaty, excluding Austria.
> 2. Men could vote, but the government and army answered to the Kaiser, not parliament.
> 3. The state persecuted Catholics and socialists, failed, and ended up bargaining with them.
> 4. Rapid industrialisation created a huge working class, the SPD, and the first welfare state.
> 5. The generals lost the war, admitted it, and then blamed civilians — the stab-in-the-back myth.

## Part 5 — Weimar: why the first democracy failed (1918/19–1933)

<!-- IMAGES:START -->
![Banknote bundle of one billion marks](https://www.dhm.de/lemo/fileadmin/medien/lemo/Titelbilder/xy002723.jpg)
A bundle of banknotes during the hyperinflation of 1923, when a loaf of bread cost billions of marks and middle-class savings vanished.  
<sub>Banknote bundle of one billion marks — 1923. Source: [Deutsches Historisches Museum, Berlin (LeMO)](https://www.dhm.de/lemo/). © DHM — used with credit for personal study. [Image index](images/IMAGE_INDEX.md#billion-marks)</sub>
<!-- IMAGES:END -->

The new republic was a real democracy. Its 1919 constitution gave **all adults over 20 — including women — an equal vote**, made the government responsible to parliament, listed extensive rights, and created a directly elected **President** with emergency powers (**Article 48**) as a safeguard.

But it was born in defeat and violence. The Social Democratic leaders who took over in November 1918 made a pact with the army to keep order; they used right-wing *Freikorps* to crush a communist uprising (the murders of Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht, January 1919) — deepening a bitter, lasting split between Social Democrats and Communists. The **Versailles Treaty** (1919) — territorial losses, "war guilt," reparations — was hated across the non-left spectrum. Much of the old elite — **army officers, judges, senior officials, big landowners, professors** — never accepted the republic. Right-wing killers of republican politicians got light sentences.

The republic survived its early crises — a coup attempt (1920), the assassination of ministers, the French occupation of the Ruhr and the **hyperinflation of 1923**, which wiped out middle-class savings. From 1924 to 1929 came relative stability, American loans, social reforms (unemployment insurance in 1927) and a famous cultural flowering.

Then the **Great Depression** hit. By early 1932 about six million people were registered unemployed. The last parliamentary coalition collapsed in 1930 over the cost of unemployment insurance. From then on, chancellors governed through **presidential emergency decrees** instead of parliamentary majorities: President **Hindenburg**, a monarchist field marshal, and a small circle around him (his son, his state secretary, General Schleicher) decided who governed. Chancellor **Brüning** cut spending, wages and benefits to balance the budget and end reparations — whether he had any alternative is still debated. Democracy was hollowed out *before* Hitler arrived.

The **Nazi party**, a fringe group with 2.6% in 1928, exploded to 18% in 1930 and **37% in July 1932**. Who voted for it? Voters from all classes — the Nazis were a "people's party of protest" — but disproportionately **Protestant, rural and middle-class**. Practising **Catholics** and **organised workers** largely resisted; the **unemployed** leaned more to the Communists. In November 1932 the Nazi vote fell to 33%.

In **July 1932** Chancellor Papen deposed the Social Democratic government of Prussia by decree — removing the republic's strongest bastion. Then, in January 1933, a backroom deal: Papen persuaded Hindenburg to appoint **Hitler** chancellor in a cabinet dominated by conservatives, confident they could control him ("We've hired him"). On **30 January 1933**, Hitler became chancellor. He was never elected by a majority of Germans; he was **appointed** by elites who preferred him to democracy or the left.

**So why did Weimar fail?** Not because Germans were incapable of democracy, and not because the outcome was inevitable. It failed because a devastating economic crisis met a political culture in which large parts of the elite and the Protestant middle classes had never accepted the republic, the left was split, the constitution gave the President a way to bypass parliament — and a small number of powerful men chose to hand power to Hitler.

> **If you remember only 5 things (1918–1933)**
> 1. Weimar was a genuine democracy with women's suffrage and strong rights.
> 2. Old elites never accepted it; the left was split; Versailles and the stab-in-the-back myth undermined it.
> 3. Inflation (1923) destroyed middle-class trust; the Depression (1929–33) destroyed the parliamentary majority.
> 4. From 1930 the President's circle governed by decree — democracy was hollowed out before Hitler.
> 5. Hitler was appointed by conservatives, not elected by a majority.

## Part 6 — How the Nazi state worked, and why people went along (1933–1945)

<!-- IMAGES:START -->
![Hitler Youth propaganda march](https://www.dhm.de/lemo/fileadmin/medien/lemo/Titelbilder/ba132831.jpg)
A Hitler Youth (*Hitlerjugend*) march in 1933: uniforms, flags, and the mobilisation of the young that the regime relied on.  
<sub>Hitler Youth propaganda march — 1933. Source: [Deutsches Historisches Museum, Berlin (LeMO)](https://www.dhm.de/lemo/). Bundesarchiv photo via DHM LeMO — used with credit for personal study. [Image index](images/IMAGE_INDEX.md#hj-march)</sub>
<!-- IMAGES:END -->

**How exactly did the Nazi state work?** It was built fast, "legally," and then ran as organised chaos.

Within weeks, the **Reichstag Fire Decree** (28 February 1933) suspended basic rights and allowed arrest without trial; tens of thousands of Communists and Social Democrats were thrown into the first camps. The **Enabling Act** (23 March 1933) let the government make laws without parliament; it passed 444 to 94, with the Communist deputies already excluded and SA men filling the hall — only the Social Democrats voted no. Within months, unions were smashed, parties banned, the federal states subordinated, and Jewish and "unreliable" officials dismissed. In 1934 Hitler had the leaders of his own storm troopers murdered and, on Hindenburg's death, made himself "Führer"; the army swore a personal oath to him.

The resulting state was not a neat pyramid:

- **Hitler** set goals and signalled wishes; he disliked paperwork; the cabinet stopped meeting in 1938.
- Below him, rival empires — the **party**, the **SS and police** (Himmler, Heydrich), the old **ministries**, the **army**, **Göring's economic apparatus**, **Goebbels's propaganda** — competed for his favour.
- Officials, as one put it in 1934, "worked towards the Führer" — anticipating what he wanted. This competition produced **cumulative radicalisation**, especially against Jews.
- Normal law (contracts, property, ordinary courts) kept running for "normal" matters, while a parallel **police sphere** acted without any legal limits — a "Dual State."

**Why did ordinary Germans support, tolerate, resist or participate?** Historians have argued this intensely, and the honest answer is: *all of these, in different mixtures*.

- **Consent:** Jobs returned (rearmament and public works); order replaced street violence; foreign-policy triumphs (Rhineland 1936, Austria 1938) restored national pride; there were cheap holidays and radios. Many admired Hitler personally even while grumbling about local party bosses.
- **Coercion:** The terror of 1933 against the labour movement was massive and formative. Everyone knew about the camps. Speaking carelessly could be dangerous. The Gestapo was small (about 7,000 employees in 1937) but drew on **denunciations** by neighbours — for conviction, grudges or gain — and on block wardens, party organisations, police and the courts; historians disagree about which mattered most.
- **Participation and benefit:** Many profited from the exclusion of Jews — jobs, businesses, apartments, furniture bought at auctions.
- **Resistance:** Real but rare and fragmented — Communists and Social Democrats underground (earliest and hardest hit), church figures (Bishop von Galen against the murder of disabled people), individuals (Georg Elser's bomb in 1939), students (the White Rose), and a group of officers and conservatives who tried to kill Hitler on **20 July 1944**. None could overthrow the regime.

**The Holocaust.** Persecution escalated in stages: exclusion (1933–35, the Nuremberg Laws), robbery and forced emigration (1938 pogrom), ghettos and the first mass shootings in Poland (1939–41), the murder of disabled people inside Germany (from 1939), and from the **invasion of the Soviet Union in 1941**, the systematic murder of Jews — first by shooting, then in death camps (Auschwitz-Birkenau, Treblinka, Sobibór, Bełżec, Chełmno, Majdanek). About **six million Jews** were murdered, along with hundreds of thousands of Roma, around 3.3 million Soviet prisoners of war, and millions of Polish and Soviet civilians. The genocide needed the whole state: ministries, railways, the army, police battalions of middle-aged "ordinary men" (who were offered the chance not to shoot — and mostly shot anyway), companies and local collaborators. The Wannsee Conference (1942) coordinated a decision already being implemented. Inside Germany, the mass murder was an **"open secret"**: many knew enough to know, and chose not to know more.

**The war** was Germany's war of choice, aimed at conquering "living space" in the East. It began with stunning victories that made Hitler hugely popular, turned at Moscow (1941) and Stalingrad (1943), and ended in total defeat on **8 May 1945** — with German cities in ruins, around five million German soldiers dead, and millions of refugees fleeing west.

> **If you remember only 5 things (1933–1945)**
> 1. Dictatorship came through emergency decree and a coerced "legal" law within six months.
> 2. The regime was a competitive jungle radicalised by officials "working towards the Führer."
> 3. Support mixed belief, benefit, conformity and fear; resistance was real but rare.
> 4. The Holocaust required the whole state and many ordinary participants; it was an open secret.
> 5. The regime ended only through total military defeat — not revolution from within.

## Part 7 — Two Germanys (1945–1990)

<!-- IMAGES:START -->
![Aerial view of the Wall at the Brandenburg Gate](https://www.hdg.de/lemo/img/galeriebilder/geteiltesdeutschland/luftansicht-mauerbau_foto_LEMO-F-7-046_barch.jpg)
The Wall cutting through the centre of Berlin at the Brandenburg Gate — the Cold War made concrete.  
<sub>Aerial view of the Wall at the Brandenburg Gate — Bundesarchiv, 1960s. Source: [Stiftung Haus der Geschichte (LeMO)](https://www.hdg.de/lemo/). © Haus der Geschichte / rights holders — used with credit for personal study. [Image index](images/IMAGE_INDEX.md#wall-aerial)</sub>
<!-- IMAGES:END -->

**What happened in 1945?** The German state ceased to exist. The four Allies divided Germany into zones, moved the eastern border to the Oder–Neisse line, and around **twelve million Germans** fled or were expelled from Eastern Europe — the biggest population movement in German history. The Allies tried Nazi leaders at **Nuremberg** and screened millions of Germans with questionnaires and tribunals (**denazification**). In the West, this became a "follower factory" that ended in mass rehabilitation; in the Soviet zone it was used to sweep away old elites — landowners were expropriated, industry nationalised — and install a new ruling party. As the Cold War set in, the zones hardened into two states in **1949**.

### The Federal Republic (West)

Its constitution, the **Basic Law**, reads like a list of lessons from Weimar:

| Weimar weakness | Basic Law answer |
|---|---|
| Rights could be suspended | Human dignity first (Art. 1); rights directly enforceable in court |
| A powerful President with emergency decrees | A ceremonial President |
| Governments toppled by negative majorities | A chancellor can only be removed by electing a successor |
| Anti-democrats used democracy to destroy it | A Constitutional Court that can ban anti-constitutional parties ("militant democracy") |
| Constitution hollowed out by Enabling Act | Core principles made unamendable |

West Germany anchored itself in the West (NATO, the European Community), enjoyed an **economic miracle** (mostly catch-up growth plus good institutions; the Marshall Plan helped less than legend says), and absorbed eight million expellees. It also quietly reintegrated most former Nazis — judges, officials, doctors — and faced the past only gradually: the Auschwitz trials of the 1960s, the student revolt of 1968, and a long public reckoning since. It invited "guest workers" from Italy, Turkey and elsewhere, expected them to leave, and found itself — unwillingly — an immigration country. Power was dispersed among the Chancellor, coalition partners, the states, a powerful Constitutional Court, the central bank, unions and employers. It became the most stable democracy in German history.

### The German Democratic Republic (East)

The GDR was a one-party dictatorship of the **SED**, created by forcing the Communists and Social Democrats together, and protected by Soviet troops. Real power sat in the **Politburo**; parliament voted unanimously. It carried out a genuine **social revolution** — old elites gone, workers' children in universities in the early decades, women in work (around 90%), jobs, childcare, cheap rent and bread guaranteed. It also built one of the densest surveillance systems in history: in 1989 the **Stasi** had about 91,000 staff and some 189,000 informants. When workers rose in **June 1953**, Soviet tanks crushed them. When nearly three million people had left for the West, the regime built the **Berlin Wall (1961)**; at least 140 people died there trying to cross or in connection with the border. Most East Germans neither resisted nor fully believed: they adapted, participated, and withdrew into private "niches."

### Cold War and détente

Each Germany became a front-line state in its bloc. In the 1970s West Germany's **Ostpolitik** (Willy Brandt) accepted the post-war borders and the GDR's existence in practice, while keeping the idea of one nation alive; the 1975 Helsinki accords gave East German dissidents an international text on human rights to cite.

> **If you remember only 5 things (1945–1990)**
> 1. In 1945 the German state vanished; 12 million expellees reshaped society.
> 2. West Germany's constitution was built as an anti-Weimar machine — and it worked.
> 3. The West prospered, integrated into Europe and slowly confronted the Nazi past.
> 4. The East was a dictatorship with real social provision, a vast secret police and a wall.
> 5. Ostpolitik accepted division to soften it — and helped make 1990 acceptable to neighbours.

## Part 8 — 1989–90: why reunification happened then

<!-- IMAGES:START -->
![People on the Wall at the Brandenburg Gate](https://www.hdg.de/lemo/img/galeriebilder/deutscheeinheit/menschen-auf-der-mauer-am-brandenburger-tor_foto_LEMO-F-6-176_uls.jpg)
Crowds on the Wall at the Brandenburg Gate the day after it opened.  
<sub>People on the Wall at the Brandenburg Gate — 10 November 1989. Source: [Stiftung Haus der Geschichte (LeMO)](https://www.hdg.de/lemo/kapitel/deutsche-einheit.html). © Haus der Geschichte / rights holders — used with credit for personal study. [Image index](images/IMAGE_INDEX.md#brandenburger-tor-1989)</sub>
<!-- IMAGES:END -->

**Why 1990?** Three pressures met at once.

1. **Moscow let go.** Gorbachev would no longer use force to save allied regimes, and the East German leadership had rejected his reforms.
2. **People left.** In summer 1989 Hungary opened its border to Austria; tens of thousands of East Germans fled via Hungary and West German embassies.
3. **People marched.** Church-sheltered opposition groups formed (Neues Forum), and Monday demonstrations in **Leipzig** grew; on **9 October 1989**, 70,000 marched and the security forces did not shoot.

The regime crumbled. On **9 November**, a bungled announcement of new travel rules sent crowds to the border crossings, and the Wall opened. Round tables negotiated a transition; the chant "We are *the* people" became "We are *one* people." In the first free election (March 1990) East Germans chose parties that wanted rapid unification. A currency union (July) brought the D-Mark; the **Two-plus-Four Treaty** with the four wartime Allies (September) settled borders and military limits; on **3 October 1990** the East German states joined the Federal Republic under its existing constitution.

The speed had costs. Most East German industry could not survive overnight exposure to the West; the privatisation agency (**Treuhand**) sold about 80% of firms to West German buyers and ran up a deficit of about DM 260 billion; unemployment soared. The choice to join under Article 23 — rather than write a new constitution together — left many East Germans feeling their lives had been judged rather than shared.

> **If you remember only 5 things (1989–90)**
> 1. Soviet non-intervention was the precondition.
> 2. East Germans' exit and protest drove the collapse — Leipzig was the turning point.
> 3. The Wall opened by accident; reform turned into a demand for unity within weeks.
> 4. Unification meant the East joining the West's institutions.
> 5. The economic transformation was brutal and still shapes East German politics.

## Part 9 — United Germany (1990–2026)

<!-- IMAGES:START -->
![Building site in the government quarter, Berlin (new Chancellery)](https://www.hdg.de/lemo/img/galeriebilder/deutscheeinheit/baustelle-berlin-reichstag_foto_LEMO-F-6-183_uls.jpg)
Construction of the new Chancellery in the Berlin government district during the Bonn–Berlin move. The rebuilt Reichstag, with its glass dome over the plenary hall, has housed the Bundestag since 1999.  
<sub>Building site in the government quarter, Berlin (new Chancellery) — late 1990s. Source: [Stiftung Haus der Geschichte (LeMO)](https://www.hdg.de/lemo/kapitel/deutsche-einheit/baustelle-deutsche-einheit.html). © Haus der Geschichte / rights holders — used with credit for personal study. [Image index](images/IMAGE_INDEX.md#baustelle-reichstag)</sub>
<!-- IMAGES:END -->

United Germany kept the Bonn Republic's institutions and moved its capital to Berlin. It gave up the D-Mark for the **euro** — a key part of the deal that reassured its neighbours — and became the EU's pivotal economic power. It became an acknowledged **immigration society**: by 2025, over a quarter of the population had an immigration history, and citizenship law moved from descent-based (1913) toward residence-based (2000, 2024). It endured racist violence in the early 1990s, the NSU terror series, and, after the arrival of about a million refugees in 2015–16, the rise of the radical-right **AfD**.

Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 ended Germany's reliance on cheap Russian gas and its post-Cold War assumptions — a *Zeitenwende* (turning point) that led to rearmament, a 2025 constitutional change exempting defence spending from the debt brake, and a new military service law. In the 2025 federal election the CDU/CSU won and formed a coalition with the SPD under Chancellor Friedrich Merz, while the AfD became the second-largest party (about 21%). In **September 2026**, the AfD came first in Saxony-Anhalt (about 44%) and Mecklenburg-Vorpommern (about 38%), while the Left Party came first in Berlin. A court in February 2026 provisionally barred the domestic intelligence service from labelling the AfD "confirmed right-wing extremist," finding a "strong suspicion" but not certainty. The question of how Germany's anti-Weimar safeguards — and the other parties' refusal to cooperate with the AfD — will hold up is now practical, not theoretical.

> **If you remember only 5 things (1990–2026)**
> 1. Unification extended West German institutions east — at a heavy social cost.
> 2. Germany became Europe's central power by binding itself to the EU and the euro.
> 3. It became a diverse immigration society, whatever its older self-image.
> 4. 2022 forced a strategic turn: rearmament, energy change, new debt.
> 5. East–West gaps and immigration debates fuel a fragmented party system and a strong radical right.

## Part 10 — The big patterns

<!-- IMAGES:START -->
![Two Men Contemplating the Moon](https://images.metmuseum.org/CRDImages/ep/web-large/DP-31997-001-NEW.jpg)
The Romantic mood of German national feeling: two figures in 'old German' dress (*altdeutsche Tracht*, a coded sign of liberal-national sympathy after 1819) look into nature and the infinite. Nation, feeling and landscape fused.  
<sub>Two Men Contemplating the Moon — Caspar David Friedrich, c. 1825–30. Source: [The Metropolitan Museum of Art, Open Access](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/438417). Public domain (CC0). [Image index](images/IMAGE_INDEX.md#friedrich-moon)</sub>
<!-- IMAGES:END -->

### What stayed continuous

- **Federalism.** From the Empire's princes to the Confederation's states to the Kaiserreich's Bundesrat to today's Länder, power in Germany has usually been shared among regions. Only the Nazis and the GDR abolished it.
- **Law and courts as checks.** Imperial courts where peasants sued princes → the 19th-century *Rechtsstaat* → the Federal Constitutional Court.
- **A strong professional bureaucracy** that served every regime — which made regime changes smoother and, in 1933 and 1945, morally compromised.
- **The welfare state.** Bismarck's insurance survived every regime change.
- **Regional and confessional identities**, which shaped politics (even who voted Nazi) for centuries.

### What fundamentally changed

- From **estates** to **classes** to a **meritocratic-market society**.
- From **subjects** to **citizens** — for men gradually from 1848/1871, for women from 1918, for immigrants since 2000.
- From a **Roman-Christian imperial identity** to a **cultural nation**, a **Prussian nation-state**, a **racial community** (catastrophically), and finally a **constitutional, European** identity.
- From **military autonomy** to a **parliamentary army**.
- From a **country of emigration** to a **country of immigration**.

### Why Prussia was so powerful

A poor, scattered state became a great power through a **bargain** (Junkers served as officers; the crown left them masters of their peasants), a disproportionately large **army** financed by an efficient **bureaucracy**, luck and aggression (Silesia, 1740), reforms after defeat (1807–19), and then **economic leadership** (Customs Union, Rhineland and Ruhr industry) and **Bismarck's wars**. Its power was not inevitable; it nearly collapsed twice (1759–61, 1806).

### How modern Germany inherited earlier institutions

The Basic Law's federalism descends from the Empire; its Bundesrat from the Kaiserreich's; its basic rights from 1848 and 1919; its social insurance from the 1880s; its co-determination from 1918–20; its civil service from Prussian and cameralist traditions; its "militant democracy," weak presidency and constructive vote of no confidence from the lessons of 1933.

### The most important caution

**Continuity is not destiny.** The same federalism, bureaucracy and nationalism that existed before 1933 also underpinned the successful Federal Republic. What made the difference each time was who controlled the army, the emergency powers and the courts — and what choices people made in moments of crisis.
