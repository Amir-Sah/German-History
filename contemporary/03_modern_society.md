## 1. Period in One Paragraph

Germany in the 2020s is a society of about 83 million people: wealthy but unequal in wealth, ageing, increasingly secular, and — with more than a quarter of the population having an immigration history — far more diverse than any previous German polity. Its social model rests on institutions built over 140 years: Bismarckian social insurance, the post-1949 social market economy, co-determination, vocational training (the "dual system"), federal education systems, strong local government and a dense associational life. The old milieus that organised politics from the Kaiserreich to the 1960s (Catholic, social democratic, Protestant-bourgeois) have dissolved; new divides run between East and West, city and countryside, university-educated and not, and around migration, climate policy and trust in institutions. Memory of the Nazi past remains a pillar of official identity, while its meaning is debated anew in a diverse society.

## 2. The Political Structure — key social institutions

| Institution | Historical root | Today |
|---|---|---|
| Social insurance (health, pension, accident, unemployment, long-term care 1995) | 1880s Bismarck; 1927 unemployment insurance | Contribution-based; demographic pressure on pensions |
| Collective bargaining & co-determination | 1918 Stinnes–Legien; 1951/1976 laws | Coverage of collective agreements declining (~half of employees) |
| Dual vocational training | Guild apprenticeships; 1969 Vocational Training Act | Respected, but fewer applicants; skilled-labour shortage |
| Federal education (Länder) | Kaiserreich tiered school | Early tracking; strong link between parental background and educational success (PISA debates since 2001) |
| Church tax & welfare associations | 1919/1949 church–state settlement | Churches as major welfare employers (Caritas, Diakonie) despite shrinking membership |
| Associations (*Vereine*) | 19th-c. associational culture | ~600,000 registered associations; volunteer fire brigades, sports clubs |
| Public broadcasting | 1950s Allied-inspired model | ARD/ZDF; contested by populists |

### Regime Matrix — cross-reference

**Matrix:** see `contemporary/02_modern_political_system.md` (Federal Republic today). This chapter describes the society that lives under that system; its 12-question matrix is not repeated here.

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** The Basic Law promises equality before the law and forbids disadvantage because of sex, parentage, origin, faith or other grounds (Art. 3), and defines Germany as a "social federal state" (Art. 20). In practice, educational success still depends strongly on parental background (the PISA debates since 2001), wealth is unequally distributed, and who counts as "German" in a society where over a quarter of people have an immigration history remains politically contested (§3, §12).

## 3. Society

- **Population and migration:** ~26% with immigration history (2025: 21.8 million; top origins Poland, Turkey, Ukraine, Russia, Syria — Destatis). Large Turkish-German community (third/fourth generation), post-Soviet German resettlers, EU migrants, refugees.
- **Religion:** Catholics and Protestants together below half the population by the mid-2020s; ~5–6 million Muslims; small but revived Jewish communities (largely post-Soviet immigration after 1990), facing rising antisemitic incidents.
- **Gender and family:** Women's employment high but often part-time in the West; expanded childcare since 2013 (legal right to a place from age 1); parental allowance (2007); same-sex marriage (2017); self-determination law for gender entry (2024).
- **Class and inequality:** Income inequality moderate by international standards; wealth inequality high (low home ownership, inheritance concentration); regional gaps.
- **East–West:** See `01_post_reunification.md`.

## 4. Everyday Life

Rented housing (the majority of households rent), strong public transport in cities, cars central in rural areas, Sunday shop closing, recycling culture, strong local clubs, high savings rates, cash still important though declining.

## 5. Economy

Export-oriented manufacturing employment in the South and West; service jobs; low unemployment by historical standards before 2023, rising since; skilled-labour shortages; minimum wage since 2015.

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

- **Memory culture:** Survey research (e.g., the MEMO studies) shows broad acceptance of remembrance but also a tendency to believe one's own ancestors were helpers or victims rather than perpetrators.
- **Trust:** High trust in the Constitutional Court; lower and falling trust in parties and government.
- **Identity:** Constitutional patriotism, regional identities (Bavarian, Saxon…), European identity, and contested ideas of who is "German."

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

Ageing, housing, migration and integration, climate transition, deindustrialisation risks, polarisation.

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE (1990):** The West German social model — social insurance, co-determination, dual training, associations — extended to the east, while the old confessional and class milieus were already dissolving.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Ageing; immigration and asylum; secularisation; East–West gaps; housing; climate and energy transition.
↓
**TRANSITION (2000s–2020s):** Citizenship reform (partial *ius soli*, 2000); expansion of childcare (legal right to a place from age 1, 2013) and parental allowance (2007); minimum wage (2015); same-sex marriage (2017); church membership falling below half the population; new divides between city and countryside and by education.
↓
**AFTER (2026, the present):** A diverse, ageing, more secular and more polarised society whose core institutions still date from the 1880s, 1918–19 and 1949.

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

More diverse neighbourhoods, schools and workplaces; childcare places and parental allowance; a statutory minimum wage (2015); the legal recognition of same-sex couples (marriage 2017); weaker ties to churches and to the old party milieus.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

Federalism, associations (*Vereine*), social insurance, consensus orientation, the dual training system — and the strong link between family background and schooling.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| Germany is ethnically homogeneous | Over a quarter have immigration history; the share is much higher among children. |
| Germans are uniformly wealthy | Median household wealth is lower than in several EU neighbours, reflecting low home ownership and East–West gaps. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

Integration and citizenship; the place of Islam; antisemitism (including in migrant communities and on the far right and far left); the meaning of memory culture in a diverse society; East German identity.

### If you remember only 5 things

1. Today's social model is built on institutions from the 1880s, 1918–19 and 1949 layered together.
2. Germany is a diverse immigration society, whatever its older self-image.
3. The old confessional and class milieus have dissolved; new divides are regional, educational and cultural.
4. Memory of the Nazi past remains central to official identity.
5. Ageing, housing and integration are the everyday pressures shaping politics.
