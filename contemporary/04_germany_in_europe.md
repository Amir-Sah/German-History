## 1. Period in One Paragraph

For most of its history, the German question was a European question: too big and central for a balance of power, too fragmented or too aggressive for stability. After 1945 the answer was **integration**: West Germany regained sovereignty and trust by binding itself into Western institutions — the European Coal and Steel Community (1951), the European Economic Community (1957), NATO (1955) — and by reconciliation with France (Élysée Treaty, 1963) and later Poland and Israel. Unification (1990) was accepted by neighbours partly because Germany committed to deeper integration: the Maastricht Treaty (1992) and the euro (1999/2002), replacing the powerful D-Mark. Since the 2008–12 crises Germany has been the EU's indispensable economic power — often criticised for austerity policies in the eurozone crisis, trade surpluses, and dependence on Russian gas. Since 2022 it has become one of Ukraine's largest military supporters (the largest in Europe by most tallies) and is rearming, reopening questions about German leadership that its post-war culture of restraint had avoided.

## 2. The Political Structure — how Germany works in Europe

- **Constitutional basis:** Art. 23 GG ("Europe article," 1992): Germany participates in the EU; the Bundestag and Bundesrat have participation rights; the Constitutional Court reviews EU acts for "identity" and *ultra vires* (Maastricht 1993, Lisbon 2009, PSPP 2020).
- **Channels of influence:** Council votes (largest population), European Parliament (largest delegation — 96 seats), Commission presidency (Ursula von der Leyen, since 2019), Bundesbank's weight in the ECB, Franco-German tandem.
- **Security:** NATO membership; Bundeswehr under parliamentary control ("parliamentary army," Constitutional Court 1994 requires Bundestag approval for armed deployments).

### Historical phases

| Phase | Features |
|---|---|
| 1949–1969 | Adenauer's Westbindung; reconciliation with France; rearmament in NATO |
| 1969–1989 | Ostpolitik within Western framework; EC deepening; Helmut Schmidt–Giscard (EMS 1979) |
| 1989–1999 | Unification embedded in Europe; Maastricht; euro; Kosovo (first combat mission) |
| 1999–2014 | Enlargement to the East (2004); Afghanistan; eurozone crisis — Germany as creditor and rule-setter |
| 2014–2022 | Crimea; Nord Stream 2 controversy; refugee crisis and EU asylum conflicts; Brexit; COVID recovery fund (2020 — Germany accepted joint EU borrowing) |
| 2022– | *Zeitenwende*: €100 bn Bundeswehr fund; weapons to Ukraine; end of Russian gas; 2025 debt-brake exemption for defence; debates over leadership in European defence |

### Regime Matrix — Germany in the EU and NATO multilevel system (since the 1990s)

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Who formally ruled? | In areas transferred under Art. 23 GG, the EU institutions under the treaties (Maastricht 1992, Lisbon 2009): Council, European Parliament, Commission, Court of Justice, European Central Bank; in all other areas the German constitutional organs; NATO for collective defence. |
| 2 | Who actually held power? | Member-state governments in the European Council and Council (with Germany's weight as the most populous member and the Franco-German tandem); the ECB in monetary policy; the German federal government, which must involve Bundestag and Bundesrat; the Federal Constitutional Court as watchdog. |
| 3 | How was power acquired? | Treaties ratified by national parliaments; national and European elections; appointments by member-state governments. |
| 4 | How was power maintained? | EU law (applied by national courts and the Court of Justice); economic interdependence (single market, euro); coalitions of member states. |
| 5 | Who could challenge the government? | The Federal Constitutional Court ("identity" and *ultra vires* review — Maastricht 1993, Lisbon 2009, PSPP 2020); national parliaments; other member states; Eurosceptic parties (AfD, BSW); external shocks. |
| 6 | How was money obtained? | EU budget from member-state contributions and own resources (Germany is the largest net contributor); joint EU borrowing for the COVID recovery fund (2020), which Germany accepted. |
| 7 | How were decisions enforced? | National administrations implement EU law; Commission infringement procedures; Court of Justice rulings. |
| 8 | How were ordinary people represented? | European Parliament (96 German seats); national government in the Council; Bundestag and Bundesrat participation rights (Art. 23 GG). |
| 9 | Rights? | EU citizenship and free movement; the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights alongside the Basic Law and the European Convention on Human Rights. |
| 10 | Opponents? | Ordinary democratic opposition; Euroscepticism is legal and represented in parliaments. |
| 11 | Institutions connecting state to society | Parties (in European party groups), interest groups in Brussels, the Franco-German relationship (Élysée Treaty 1963), NATO. |
| 12 | Ordinary person's view | Open borders, the euro, EU neighbours and colleagues — and decisions that feel distant. |

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** Formally, the EU is a union of member states bound by treaty law, and Germany is one member among 27, with its own participation rules fixed in Art. 23 GG. In practice, Germany's size, economic weight and creditor position — above all in the eurozone crisis — gave it outsized influence, yet it rarely acts alone: its preferences depend on coalitions (especially with France), are constrained by EU law and by its own Constitutional Court, and often lose (see §11–12).

## 3. Society

- **Attitudes:** European integration was part of the post-war answer to nationalism (see §6). Euroscepticism grew with the eurozone crisis, refugee policy and the energy shock after 2022, and is represented above all by the AfD and BSW (§7).
- **Europe in daily society:** free movement brought EU citizens — notably from Poland — to live and work in Germany (see `03_modern_society.md`).
- **Memory:** Remembrance of Nazi crimes shapes attitudes toward military power, Israel, Poland and Russia (§6).

## 4. Everyday Life

Open borders (Schengen), the euro in every wallet, EU citizens as neighbours and colleagues, and EU-funded projects in eastern Länder.

## 5. Economy

Trade primarily within the EU; the export model depends on the single market; the euro replaced the D-Mark (1999/2002); EU funds for eastern Länder; Germany as the eurozone's main creditor in the 2008–12 crises; dependence on Russian gas until 2022.

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

- **"A European Germany, not a German Europe"** (Thomas Mann, 1953, often quoted by Kohl).
- **Culture of military restraint** (*Kultur der Zurückhaltung*) vs "responsibility."
- **Ordoliberal economic ideas** (rules, stability) shaped German EU policy.
- **Special relationship with Israel** ("Staatsräson," Merkel 2008) and sensitivity toward Poland and Russia rooted in WWII memory.

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

Russian aggression; US reliability; China; migration; eurozone architecture; energy transition; domestic Euroscepticism (AfD, BSW).

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE (1949):** A defeated, divided and distrusted state without full sovereignty.
↓
**PRESSURES:** The Cold War; the need for Western security and markets; neighbours' fear of German power; after 1989, the need to reassure them about unification.
↓
**TRANSITION (1950s–1990s):** Integration step by step — ECSC (1951), NATO (1955), EEC (1957), Élysée Treaty (1963), Ostpolitik inside the Western framework, Maastricht (1992), the euro (1999/2002).
↓
**AFTER (2026, the present):** The EU's pivotal economic power, rearming after 2022 and facing demands to lead in European defence (see §2 "Historical phases").

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

The freedom to live, work and study anywhere in the EU; travel without passport controls inside the Schengen area; the euro instead of the D-Mark (2002); a larger, more mobile labour market.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

The Franco-German relationship as the core of Germany's European policy; NATO membership; a culture of military restraint until 2022; memory of Nazi crimes as a guide for foreign policy; an export-oriented economy.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| Germany dominates the EU | It is the most powerful member but depends on coalitions (esp. France) and EU rules; its preferences often lose. |
| Germany's Russia policy was purely commercial | It combined Ostpolitik tradition, energy interests and a belief in "change through trade" (*Wandel durch Handel*), now widely judged a failure. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

Reluctant hegemon (William Paterson; Hans Kundnani's *The Paradox of German Power*, 2014); German responsibility for eurozone austerity; the lessons of Ostpolitik after 2022; European strategic autonomy.

*In plain words:* Four disputes. Is Germany a 'reluctant hegemon' — Europe's leading power, yet hesitant to lead — as discussed by William Paterson and Hans Kundnani? Does Germany bear responsibility for the spending cuts (austerity) in the eurozone crisis? What lessons does Ostpolitik, the old policy of dialogue with the East, hold after 2022? And should Europe gain 'strategic autonomy' — the ability to act without depending on others?

### If you remember only 5 things

1. Post-war Germany solved the "German question" by binding itself into Europe and the West.
2. Unification was accepted partly because Germany gave up the D-Mark for the euro.
3. Germany became the EU's pivotal economic power — a "reluctant hegemon."
4. Its Russia policy of engagement collapsed in 2022, forcing rearmament.
5. Memory of Nazi crimes still shapes German foreign policy toward Israel, Poland and military power.
