## 1. Period in One Paragraph

United Germany kept the West German institutions and extended them east. The first decade was dominated by the costs of unification: the Treuhand's rapid privatisation and closure of East German firms, mass unemployment in the East, huge transfer payments from West to East, and a wave of racist violence (1991–93). Germany moved its capital to Berlin, helped build the European Union and the euro, and cautiously took on military roles abroad (Kosovo 1999, Afghanistan 2001–21). The 2000s brought labour-market reforms (Agenda 2010/Hartz), an export boom and — after the 2008–12 financial and euro crises — Germany's position as the EU's pivotal economic power. Angela Merkel (2005–21), an East German, embodied continuity and crisis management. The 2015–16 arrival of about a million refugees transformed the debate on migration and fuelled the rise of the radical-right AfD. Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine (2022) ended Germany's reliance on cheap Russian gas and its post-Cold War security assumptions ("Zeitenwende"). By 2025–26, Germany faced economic stagnation, rearmament, a fragmented party system, and an eastern electorate where the AfD had become the strongest party in several states.

## 2. The Political Structure

The institutions of the Bonn Republic continued (see `02_modern_political_system.md`). Key changes:
- **Five new Länder** + unified Berlin (16 Länder total).
- **Capital move** to Berlin (Bundestag decision 1991; government moved 1999).
- **Constitutional amendments:** asylum compromise (Art. 16a, 1993), EU article (Art. 23 new text, 1992), environmental protection (Art. 20a, 1994), gender equality mandate (Art. 3(2), 1994), debt brake (2009), defence and infrastructure exemption (2025).
- **Party system:** from a stable three/four-party system to six to seven parties: PDS/Left (East-anchored), Greens, AfD (2013), BSW (2024 split from the Left).

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** In 1990 the Basic Law and the institutions of the Bonn Republic were extended to the east, and federal policy aims at "equivalent living conditions" (*gleichwertige Lebensverhältnisse*, a phrase from Art. 72 GG) across the country. In practice, gaps between East and West in wages, wealth, representation in national elites and voting behaviour have persisted for more than three decades (see §3 and §12).

## 3. Society

- **East–West:** A generation after unification, significant gaps persist — wages (full-time employees in the East earned €837 less per month than in the West in 2024), inherited wealth (several times higher in the West), population decline and ageing in the East, and under-representation of East Germans in elite positions; but the gender pay gap is much smaller in the East (5% vs 17%) and childcare more available — GDR legacies (ZDF, 2025).
- **Migration:** Late-resettler (*Spätaussiedler*) immigration from the former USSR (~2.5 million after 1990); EU free movement (Poland, Romania); refugees from Yugoslav wars (1990s), Syria/Afghanistan/Iraq (2015–16: ~890,000 arrivals in 2015 by corrected count; EASY registrations were higher), Ukraine (2022–: over a million). By 2025, 21.8 million people — **26.3%** of the population — had an "immigration history" (immigrated themselves since 1950, or both parents did) (Destatis, April 2026).
- **Citizenship:** 2000 reform introduced partial birthright citizenship (*ius soli*) for children of long-term residents; 2024 reform reduced residence requirement to 5 years and allowed dual citizenship generally; October 2025: the 3-year fast track was abolished (Bundestag vote 450–134).
- **Racist and right-wing violence:** Hoyerswerda (1991), Rostock-Lichtenhagen (1992), Mölln (1992), Solingen (1993); the NSU terror series (2000–07, 10 murders, uncovered 2011); Halle synagogue attack (2019); Hanau (2020); murder of politician Walter Lübcke (2019).
- **Religion:** Rapid secularisation — by the 2020s members of the two large churches fell below half the population; ~5–6 million Muslims.

## 4. Everyday Life

East (1990s): unemployment (East German rate peaked near 20% in the late 1990s/early 2000s), retraining, early retirement, out-migration of young women and skilled workers, renovated city centres but emptied towns; "Ostalgie." West: solidarity surcharge, slow growth in the 1990s ("sick man of Europe" around 2000), then job growth after 2005. 2020s: pandemic (2020–22), inflation after 2022, housing shortage in cities, infrastructure decay (railways, bridges), digital lag.

## 5. Economy

- **Treuhand (1990–94):** ~8,500 enterprises and ~4 million jobs at the start; ~80% of privatised firms went to West German buyers, 14% to foreign, 6% to East German buyers; revenue ~DM 60 billion vs a final deficit ~DM 260 billion (Böick, bpb 2020). IfZ research (2022 volumes): "the Treuhandanstalt ultimately stood as a symbol for the decline in productivity and employment and for the social upheaval in eastern Germany." Debate: overwhelmed institution under impossible conditions vs avoidable harm and West German interests.
- **Transfers:** Net West-to-East transfers of well over €1.5–2 trillion (estimates vary) since 1990.
- **Agenda 2010 / Hartz reforms (2003–05):** Merged unemployment assistance and welfare (Hartz IV), expanded low-wage/mini-jobs; credited with job growth and blamed for insecurity and SPD's decline; replaced by *Bürgergeld* (2023), itself reformed again from 2025.
- **Export model:** Cars, machinery, chemicals; reliance on China (markets) and Russia (energy).
- **Fiscal:** Debt brake (2009); March 2025 exemption for defence spending above 1% of GDP and a €500 billion infrastructure/climate fund (bpb).

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

- **Memory culture:** Holocaust Memorial (Berlin, 2005); the Holocaust as a core of national identity ("Staatsräson" on Israel's security — Merkel 2008); debates over colonialism, GDR memory, and the place of migrants in memory.
- **Europeanism:** Germany as the EU's anchor; tension between solidarity and fiscal orthodoxy.
- **Civic vs ethnic nationhood:** Shift toward civic citizenship; contested by the radical right.
- **Radical right:** AfD founded 2013 as a eurosceptic party; radicalised after 2015; classified by the Federal Office for the Protection of the Constitution as a suspected extremist case (*Verdachtsfall*); a 2025 upgrade to "confirmed right-wing extremist" was provisionally blocked by the Administrative Court of Cologne on 26 February 2026, which found a "strong suspicion" but not the required certainty for the party as a whole (VG Köln, 13 L 1109/25).

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

Globalisation; demographic ageing; migration; energy dependence; Russia's war; climate transition; digitalisation; China's rise; US unreliability (Trump administrations); regional inequality; declining trust in established parties.

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE (1990):** Enlarged Bonn Republic optimistic about "blooming landscapes" and European peace.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Unification costs; globalisation; euro crisis; migration; Russian aggression; stagnation.
↓
**TRANSITION (turning points):** 2015 refugee summer; 24 February 2022 ("Zeitenwende"); 2025 fiscal turn.
↓
**AFTER (2026, the present):** A more fragmented, more contested, rearming democracy; see `02_modern_political_system.md`.

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

East: everything — political system, currency, jobs, laws, property, social relations; West: less, but taxes, migration and later energy and inflation shocks. Everyone: a more diverse society, the euro, open European borders (Schengen), more insecure labour markets, the pandemic.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

The Basic Law, federalism, social partnership, the export model, consensus coalition politics, and the centrality of memory of the Nazi past.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| East Germany has "caught up" | Large progress (productivity, infrastructure, life expectancy), but persistent gaps in wages, wealth and elite representation. |
| The AfD is only an eastern phenomenon | It won ~20.8% nationally in 2025 and is strong in parts of the West too, though strongest in the East. |
| Germany has always been an immigration country / never was one | Germany has had large immigration since the 1950s but only officially acknowledged it around 2000–2005 (Immigration Act 2005). |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **The Treuhand:** Institute for Contemporary History (IfZ) research (2017–2022) vs public perception; Böick's "Achilles heel" of unity; debate on responsibility of politics vs agency.
- **East German grievance and the radical right:** Explanations emphasise economic transformation shocks, demographic decline, GDR-era socialisation, and a sense of cultural devaluation (Steffen Mau, *Lütten Klein*, 2019; *Ungleich vereint*, 2024); others stress ideological factors (ethnic nationalism, anti-migrant attitudes) and party agency. **CONTESTED.**
- **"Berlin Republic" vs "Bonn Republic":** Continuity or new era?

### If you remember only 5 things

1. Unification meant extending West German institutions east — with a brutal economic shock for East Germans.
2. Germany became Europe's central power through the EU and the euro, while avoiding military leadership until 2022.
3. Germany became an acknowledged immigration society; over a quarter of people now have an immigration history.
4. The 2022 Russian invasion forced a strategic turn: rearmament, energy change, new debt.
5. East–West gaps and immigration debates fuel the rise of the AfD, now the largest party in parts of the East.
