## 1. Period in One Paragraph

The Federal Republic of Germany is a federal, parliamentary democracy governed by the **Basic Law** (1949, extended to the whole country in 1990). Power is deliberately dispersed: between the federal level and 16 Länder; between the Chancellor and a coalition of parties in the Bundestag; between parliament and a powerful Federal Constitutional Court; between the German state and the European Union; and between elected politics and autonomous institutions (the Bundesbank within the European Central Bank system, social partners, public broadcasters, churches). Governments are almost always coalitions, and many laws need consent from the Bundesrat, where state governments sit — so German politics is built for compromise and slow change. This design was a response to Weimar and Nazism; in the 2020s it is being tested by fragmentation of the party system, the rise of the radical-right AfD, and demands for faster action on security, energy and the economy.

## 2. The Political Structure

### Regime Matrix — Federal Republic today

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Formally ruled | The people via the **Bundestag** (elected every 4 years; mixed-member proportional system; 5% threshold or 3 constituency seats; reformed 2023 to cap size at 630 seats); the **Federal Chancellor** (elected by the Bundestag); the **Bundesrat** (Länder governments); the **Federal President** (head of state, largely ceremonial). |
| 2 | Actually held power | The Chancellor and coalition committee; party leaderships; ministers (department principle); Länder governments (via Bundesrat and implementation); the **Federal Constitutional Court (BVerfG)**; EU institutions (majority of economic and trade regulation); ECB (monetary policy). |
| 3 | Power acquired | Elections and coalition agreements. |
| 4 | Power maintained | Parliamentary majority; constructive vote of no confidence protects chancellors; coalition discipline. |
| 5 | Who could challenge | Opposition, Bundesrat, Constitutional Court (e.g., the November 2023 ruling voiding a €60 bn budget reallocation, which set off the budget conflicts that ended the Scholz coalition in November 2024), media, courts, EU law. |
| 6 | Money | Tax-sharing system (income, VAT, corporate taxes shared among federation, Länder, municipalities), fiscal equalisation between Länder, social insurance contributions; debt brake (Art. 109/115) with 2025 exemptions. |
| 7 | Enforcement | Länder administrations and police (federal police limited); independent judiciary; administrative courts. |
| 8 | Representation | Universal suffrage 18+ (16+ in some Länder and for European elections since 2024); strong parties (public funding); local self-government. |
| 9 | Rights | Basic rights (Arts. 1–19) directly enforceable; constitutional complaint available to anyone; European Convention on Human Rights and EU Charter. |
| 10 | Opponents | Legal opposition free; "militant democracy": party bans possible only by the Constitutional Court (NPD not banned in 2017 because too insignificant; later excluded from party financing, 2024); domestic intelligence (Verfassungsschutz) monitors extremist organisations, subject to court review (e.g., VG Köln 2026 on the AfD). |
| 11 | Connecting institutions | Parties, unions and employers (collective bargaining, co-determination), churches (church tax), associations, public broadcasters (ARD, ZDF), local government. |
| 12 | Ordinary person's view | Stable rights and courts; complex bureaucracy; strong welfare state; slow decision-making; declining trust in parties. |

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** The Basic Law's text and the practice of German government diverge in predictable ways:

| Constitution says | How it works in practice |
|---|---|
| Chancellor determines policy guidelines (*Richtlinienkompetenz*, Art. 65) | Coalition agreements and coalition committees constrain chancellors heavily |
| Bundesrat represents Länder | Also a partisan arena; opposition-led Länder can block "consent laws" (*Zustimmungsgesetze*) — the 2006 federalism reform reduced their share |
| Federal President is head of state | Moral authority, occasionally decisive (dissolution of Bundestag after lost confidence votes: 1983, 2005, December 2024) |
| Parties "participate in forming the political will" (Art. 21) | Parties dominate recruitment for public office and public broadcasting boards |
| Germany is a "social federal state" (Art. 20) | Extensive, insurance-based welfare state; long-term pressures from ageing |
| Constitutional Court interprets the Basic Law | It is a quasi-legislator on major issues (abortion, EU integration — "Lisbon" 2009, "PSPP" 2020 against the ECB bond programme; climate 2021; debt brake 2023) |
| Sovereign powers may be transferred to the EU (Art. 23) | Much law originates in Brussels; BVerfG reserves "identity control" |

## 3. Society — political dimension

- **Party system (2025 federal election):** CDU/CSU 28.5%, AfD 20.8%, SPD 16.4%, Greens 11.6%, Die Linke 8.8%; BSW 4.97% and FDP 4.3% failed the threshold; turnout ~83%, the highest since 1990 (deutschland.de). Friedrich Merz (CDU) elected Chancellor in May 2025 at the head of a CDU/CSU–SPD coalition.
- **State elections September 2026 (preliminary results reported):** AfD first in **Saxony-Anhalt** (43.8%; CDU 17.2%) — short of an absolute majority of seats (Heinrich Böll Foundation, 8 Sep 2026); AfD first in **Mecklenburg-Vorpommern** (38.2%, SPD 35.5%, CDU ~4.9%); **Die Linke** first in **Berlin** (25.7%) (Al Jazeera, 20 Sep 2026). The Chancellor called the results a "disaster." Government formation in Saxony-Anhalt raises the question of the "firewall" (*Brandmauer*) — the refusal of other parties to cooperate with the AfD.
- **Cleavages:** East–West; urban–rural; education; age; migration background; attitudes toward immigration and climate policy.

## 4. Everyday Life — citizen–state contact

Registration (*Meldepflicht*), tax offices, schools (Länder competence), social insurance, local administration, public broadcasting fees, courts; declining but still significant association membership; high trust in the Constitutional Court relative to parties and government.

## 5. Economy — governance

Social market economy; autonomous collective bargaining; co-determination; statutory minimum wage (since 2015); EU single market and euro; industrial policy debates (energy prices, China, US tariffs); investment backlog addressed by the 2025 special fund.

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

- **Constitutional patriotism** and memory culture as foundations of civic identity.
- **"Militant democracy"** (*wehrhafte Demokratie*) — debates about an AfD ban procedure.
- **Europeanism** — though contested by the AfD and BSW.
- **Security debate** after 2022: conscription had been suspended since 2011; the **Military Service Modernisation Act** (Bundestag, 5 Dec 2025, 323–272) keeps service voluntary but makes a questionnaire compulsory for 18-year-old men, reintroduces compulsory medical screening (*Musterung*) for men born from 2008, and allows the Bundestag to activate compulsory service by separate law if volunteer numbers fall short (Bundestag text archive).

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

Fragmentation making coalitions harder; AfD strength in the East; fiscal pressures (defence, pensions); energy transition; demographic change; populist distrust; external shocks (Russia, US policy, China).

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE (1949–1990):** The Bonn Republic: a Basic Law designed against Weimar's failures (see `twentieth_century/09_west_germany.md`).
↓
**PRESSURES:** Unification; European integration; party fragmentation; fiscal pressures (defence, pensions); populist distrust; external shocks (Russia, US policy, China).
↓
**TRANSITION (1990–2026):** Accession of the GDR under Art. 23 (1990); new Europe article (Art. 23, 1992); capital move to Berlin (1999); debt brake (2009); electoral reform capping the Bundestag at 630 seats (2023); debt-brake exemption for defence (2025); rise of the AfD to second-largest party (2025).
↓
**AFTER (2026, the present):** The system described in this file as of late September 2026 — its anti-Weimar safeguards tested by fragmentation and by AfD pluralities in eastern Länder.

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

Since 1949: size (16 Länder), capital, EU integration, party fragmentation, immigration, voting age, the military's role.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

The Basic Law's core design, federalism, the Constitutional Court's centrality, coalition government, social partnership.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| The Chancellor is like a president | Chancellors depend on coalitions and the Bundestag. |
| The Federal President is powerless | Mostly ceremonial, but has reserve powers (e.g., dissolution, signing laws after constitutional review). |
| Germany could "just ban" the AfD | Only the Constitutional Court can ban a party, with high thresholds (NPD 2017 precedent). |
| The Länder are administrative districts | They have constitutional statehood, own constitutions, and control education, police and culture. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **Is the Brandmauer sustainable?** Especially where the AfD wins pluralities in the East.
- **AfD ban procedure:** Legal (evidence thresholds, VG Köln 2026) and political (backlash) arguments.
- **Reform of federalism and bureaucracy** (speed of planning, digitalisation).
- **Electoral reform (2023)** — BVerfG upheld the size cap but restored the basic-mandate clause (July 2024).

### If you remember only 5 things

1. Power in Germany is deliberately spread out — federation vs Länder, chancellor vs coalition, parliament vs Constitutional Court, Berlin vs Brussels.
2. The Constitutional Court is one of the most powerful institutions in the country.
3. Coalition government and the Bundesrat make German politics consensual — and slow.
4. The party system has fragmented; the AfD is the second-largest national party and the largest in parts of the East.
5. The system's anti-Weimar safeguards are now being tested in practice.
