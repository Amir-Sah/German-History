## 1. Period in One Paragraph

The Congress of Vienna did not restore the Holy Roman Empire. Instead it created the **German Confederation** (*Deutscher Bund*): a league of initially 39 (later up to 41) sovereign states — from the Austrian Empire and Prussia (each only partly inside it) to tiny principalities and four free cities — plus foreign monarchs as members for some lands (the King of Britain for Hanover until 1837, the King of Denmark for Holstein, the King of the Netherlands for Luxembourg). Its only organ was a **Federal Assembly** (*Bundesversammlung*) of envoys in Frankfurt, chaired by Austria. It was designed for security and for keeping revolution down, not for building a nation. Jürgen Osterhammel describes it as "rather an institution for common consultation and coordination among independent states than a quasi-nation-state." Under Metternich's leadership it became an instrument of censorship and surveillance (Carlsbad Decrees, 1819). Meanwhile, economic integration proceeded outside it, through the Prussian-led **Customs Union** (*Zollverein*, 1834), which excluded Austria. The Confederation collapsed when Prussia went to war with Austria in 1866.

## 2. The Political Structure

### Regime Matrix — The German Confederation

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Formally ruled | Sovereign member princes and free cities, acting collectively through the Federal Assembly (Frankfurt), presided by Austria. |
| 2 | Actually held power | Austria and Prussia when they agreed (Metternich's system); otherwise, each state governed itself. |
| 3 | Power acquired | Dynastic legitimacy restored/confirmed at Vienna (1815). |
| 4 | Power maintained | Mutual guarantee of thrones; federal intervention clause; police cooperation; censorship. |
| 5 | Who could challenge | Liberal and national movements; revolutions (1830 locally, 1848 generally); Austro-Prussian rivalry. |
| 6 | Money | Member contributions (the Confederation itself had no tax); states' own revenues. |
| 7 | Enforcement | *Bundesexekution* (federal execution by members' troops); Central Investigation Commission in Mainz (1819) and the Mainz Information Bureau (1833) coordinating spies. |
| 8 | Representation | None at federal level. Article 13 of the Federal Act promised each state a "constitution of estates" (*landständische Verfassung*) — implemented in the south (Bavaria and Baden 1818, Württemberg 1819, Hesse-Darmstadt 1820), not in Prussia or Austria until 1848. |
| 9 | Rights | Varied by state; southern constitutions contained some basic rights; press freedom curtailed federally from 1819. |
| 10 | Opponents | Censorship, dismissal (e.g., the **Göttingen Seven** professors dismissed in 1837 for protesting Hanover's revocation of its constitution), imprisonment, exile (Heine, Büchner, Marx). |
| 11 | Connecting institutions | State bureaucracies, churches, schools, armies; associations (gymnastics, singing, shooting clubs) as unofficial national networks. |
| 12 | Ordinary person's view | Your state (Bavaria, Hanover…) was the state; "Germany" was a cultural idea or an economic zone. |

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** The Federal Act (1815) and Vienna Final Act (1820) framed a defensive league with a promise of representative estates. In practice, it functioned as a **conservative security alliance** against liberalism and nationalism, while the substantive politics of constitution-making, economic integration and rights happened at state level and through the Zollverein.

### The Carlsbad Decrees (1819)

After the student radical Karl Ludwig Sand murdered the conservative writer August von Kotzebue, Metternich pushed through: press censorship (for publications under 20 printed sheets = 320 pages), supervision of universities, dissolution of the student fraternities (*Burschenschaften*), and the Mainz investigative commission.

## 3. Society

- **Nobility** retained social dominance, especially in the east and in armies.
- **Bourgeoisie:** Educated (*Bildungsbürgertum*: officials, professors, pastors, lawyers) and propertied (*Wirtschaftsbürgertum*: merchants, early industrialists) — the social base of liberalism.
- **Artisans:** Squeezed by competition and freedom of trade; many became the "proletaroid" poor.
- **Peasants:** Legally freed step by step (Prussia's reforms; redemption laws in southern states, completed mostly after 1848) — at the price of redemption payments.
- **Rural underclass:** Rapidly growing; population rose from ~23 to ~35 million between 1800 and 1850 (Osterhammel).
- **Jews:** Emancipation incomplete and uneven; partial rollback after 1815 (e.g., Frankfurt, Hanseatic cities); "Hep-Hep" riots of 1819 against Jews in many towns.
- **Women:** Excluded from politics; the bourgeois ideal of separate spheres hardened; women active in charitable and patriotic associations.

## 4. Everyday Life

Mostly rural and local. Mass poverty (*Pauperismus*) grew: the 1846 *Brockhaus* encyclopedia defined it as the condition where "a numerous class of people can at most earn the barest livelihood through the most strenuous work." Harvest failures in 1846–47 caused famine and food riots. Emigration, especially to the United States, rose sharply — Osterhammel reports a peak of more than 80,000 departures in 1847. The **Biedermeier** style epitomised the middle-class retreat into domestic life.

## 5. Economy

- **Zollverein (1834):** Customs union under Prussian leadership, eventually including most non-Austrian states; created a common market and made Prussia the economic centre.
- **Railways:** Nuremberg–Fürth (1835), Leipzig–Dresden (1839); by 1850, ~6,000 km of track; the railway was the "growth locomotive" (Osterhammel) — pulling coal, iron and engineering.
- **Proto-industry and crisis:** Silesian linen weavers ruined by mechanised British competition — their 1844 revolt, crushed by the army, became a symbol (Heine's poem, Hauptmann's later play).
- Industrial "take-off" from the 1840s/1850s in the Rhineland, Saxony, Silesia, Berlin.

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

See `03_nationalism.md`. Main currents: **conservatism** (restoration of legitimate thrones, Christian state), **liberalism** (constitution, rule of law, press freedom, property-based representation), **nationalism** (German unity), **democratic radicalism** (popular sovereignty, republic — Büchner's *Hessian Courier*, 1834: "Peace to the huts! War on the palaces!"), early **socialism** and **communism** (Weitling; Marx & Engels's *Communist Manifesto*, 1848), **political Catholicism** (after the Cologne church conflict, 1837).

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

- Constitutional promises broken (Prussia).
- Economic integration without political integration.
- Pauperism and hunger (1846–47).
- Rising press, associations and public opinion.
- The Austro-Prussian rivalry.
- European revolutions (1830 in France/Belgium/Poland; February 1848 in Paris).

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE:** A federation of sovereign princes with no representation and a censorship regime.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Liberal-national movement; pauperism; economic integration via Zollverein; Europe-wide revolutionary wave.
↓
**TRANSITION (crisis):** March 1848 — revolutions in almost every German state; Federal Assembly bypassed by the Frankfurt National Assembly (see `04_1848_revolutions.md`).
↓
**AFTER (1850–66):** Confederation restored in 1850–51; then broken by Prussia's war against Austria in 1866 (see `05_unification.md`).

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

Peace (1815–1848) after decades of war; slow abolition of feudal dues; more schools; railways; but also hunger, pauperism, and state surveillance of political life.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

Monarchical sovereignty; nobility's privileged access to army and diplomacy; regional identities; confessional divides.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| The Confederation was a failed attempt at unity | It was designed *not* to be a nation-state. |
| The Zollverein inevitably produced political unification | It created interdependence, but many Zollverein states fought *with Austria* against Prussia in 1866. |
| The era was politically dead (Biedermeier) | Associations, press and constitutional politics in the south were very active. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **Reassessment of the Confederation:** Older nationalist historiography dismissed it; recent work (e.g., Jürgen Müller, *Der Deutsche Bund 1815–1866*) stresses its function as a peace order and its reform debates (e.g., federal reform plans of the 1860s) — similar to the Empire's reassessment.
- **Economic unity and national unity:** Debate over how far the Zollverein made Prussian leadership "natural" (a *kleindeutsch* retrospective narrative).

### If you remember only 5 things

1. After 1815, "Germany" was a league of ~39 sovereign states, not a country.
2. Its main job was keeping order and suppressing liberal and national movements.
3. Real political life happened in the states — some (in the south) got constitutions, Prussia and Austria did not.
4. Economic unity (Zollverein, railways) grew under Prussian leadership while political unity stalled.
5. Population growth and pauperism created explosive social pressure by the 1840s.
