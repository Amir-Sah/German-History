## 1. Period in One Paragraph

The German Empire was a federal monarchy of 25 states under Prussian leadership, with a Kaiser (the King of Prussia), a Chancellor appointed by and responsible only to the Kaiser, a Federal Council (*Bundesrat*) of state governments, and a **Reichstag** elected by universal, equal, secret suffrage for men over 25 — one of the broadest franchises in Europe. Yet the Reichstag could not appoint or dismiss the government, and the army was largely outside parliamentary control. Historians have called this constitution a "system of avoided decisions" (Wolfgang J. Mommsen): monarchical and democratic principles coexisted without either winning. Society industrialised at breakneck speed; cities, the working class and the Social Democratic Party grew enormously; Catholics and socialists were persecuted and then integrated; nationalism became more aggressive, antisemitism organised, and the Empire acquired colonies and built a battle fleet. Historians still debate whether this was an authoritarian state on a "special path" toward catastrophe, or a modern, dynamic society in gradual democratisation that was derailed by war.

## 2. The Political Structure

### Regime Matrix — The Kaiserreich

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Formally ruled | The **Bundesrat** (the allied princes and free cities) held sovereignty; the **Kaiser** (King of Prussia) presided, appointed the Chancellor, commanded the armed forces, declared war (with Bundesrat consent), summoned and closed the Reichstag. |
| 2 | Actually held power | Kaiser + Chancellor + Prussian state ministry + army leadership (military cabinet, General Staff); under Bismarck (to 1890) the Chancellor dominated; under Wilhelm II a mix of personal rule claims, court camarilla, bureaucracy, and increasingly the Reichstag's budget power; in WWI the Supreme Army Command. |
| 3 | Power acquired | Hereditary monarchy; Chancellor appointed by Kaiser. |
| 4 | Power maintained | Prussian hegemony (Kaiser = King of Prussia; Chancellor usually Prussian Minister-President); army loyalty; bureaucracy; alliances with agrarian and industrial elites; "negative integration" against "enemies of the Reich" (Catholics, socialists, Poles). |
| 5 | Who could challenge | Reichstag majorities (budget, laws); mass parties; federal states; public opinion; the army itself. |
| 6 | Money | Reich: customs and indirect taxes, "matricular contributions" from states; states: direct taxes. No Reich income tax until WWI. |
| 7 | Enforcement | State bureaucracies and police; courts (a genuine *Rechtsstaat* — rule of law — for most purposes); army could be deployed domestically (state of siege). |
| 8 | Representation | Reichstag (397 seats) by universal male suffrage (25+); constituency boundaries unchanged from 1871, over-representing rural areas as cities grew; state parliaments often with restricted suffrage (Prussia's three-class system to 1918; Saxony's plural vote). |
| 9 | Rights | No federal bill of rights; rights guaranteed in state constitutions and laws; legal equality; freedom of press (Press Law 1874) and association (Reich Association Law 1908) with exceptions. |
| 10 | Opponents | Legal persecution (Kulturkampf laws, Anti-Socialist Law), police surveillance, prosecutions for lèse-majesté; but not killing or camps. |
| 11 | Connecting institutions | Army (conscription), schools, churches, associations, parties, trade unions, social insurance, local government. |
| 12 | Ordinary person's view | Military service, school, social insurance card, voting in Reichstag elections (turnout rose to ~85% in 1912), police regulations, strong regional/confessional milieu. |

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:**
- *Said:* Sovereignty lay with the federal princes (Bundesrat); the Reichstag co-legislated and approved budgets.
- *Worked:* Prussia dominated the Bundesrat — it held 17 of 58 votes, but 14 votes sufficed to block constitutional change, and Prussia's size (about two-thirds of the population) gave it *de facto* hegemony. The Chancellor answered only to the Kaiser: "the Chancellor was not dependent on the confidence of parliament, as is usual in a parliamentary democracy" (Ziemann). The army budget was approved for seven years (*Septennat*, 1874; later five-year terms), removing about three-quarters of Reich spending from annual parliamentary control. The Kaiser's "power of command" (*Kommandogewalt*) over the army was outside ministerial responsibility — the **Zabern affair (1913)**, when officers illegally detained civilians in Alsace, ended with a Reichstag vote of no confidence against the Chancellor that had no constitutional consequence.
- *But also:* Because every law and budget needed the Reichstag, governments had to build majorities; from the 1890s, the Reichstag's weight grew with the expanding interventionist state (Ziemann: gradual "Aufwertung"). Governments were increasingly forced to negotiate with parties.

## 3. Society

**Social-moral milieus (M. Rainer Lepsius):** Four (or five) stable blocs, each with party, press, associations and worldview, persisting until 1933:
1. **Conservative-Protestant-agrarian** (East Elbia; German Conservative Party; Agrarian League from 1893)
2. **Liberal-Protestant bourgeois** (National Liberals; Left Liberals)
3. **Catholic** (Centre Party, *Zentrum*; ~20% of seats consistently; dense network of associations)
4. **Social Democratic working class** (SPD; Free Trade Unions)
(+ national minorities' parties: Poles, Danes, Alsatians, Guelphs)

**Classes:**
- **Nobility:** Dominated army officer corps, diplomacy, top Prussian administration, East Elbian land. Bourgeois officers grew in number but adopted aristocratic norms; the reserve-officer status was a coveted marker for middle-class men.
- **Bourgeoisie:** Industrialists (Krupp, Thyssen, Siemens), bankers, professionals, officials — economically dominant, politically divided.
- **Mittelstand:** Artisans, shopkeepers, small farmers — fearful of both big capital and socialism; susceptible to antisemitic and protectionist politics.
- **Industrial working class:** Grew to the largest social group; SPD became the largest party by votes from 1890 and by seats in 1912 (~35% of the vote, 110 seats).
- **Rural labourers**, many Polish seasonal workers.

**Religion:** Protestants ~62%, Catholics ~36%, Jews ~1%. The **Kulturkampf** (1871–1887): liberal and state campaign against the Catholic Church — pulpit paragraph (1871), Jesuit expulsion (1872), May Laws (1873), compulsory civil marriage (1875). At its peak (1875–76) a quarter of Catholic parishes in Prussia were vacant through imprisonment or flight of priests; bishops were imprisoned or exiled. It failed: Catholics rallied to the Centre; Bismarck ended most measures by 1887.

**Minorities:**
- **Poles** (~10% of Prussia's population): Germanisation policies — language laws (school instruction, 1870s; Associations Law 1908 restricted Polish in public meetings), Settlement Commission (1886) buying land for German settlers; expulsions of ~30,000 non-Prussian Poles and Jews (1885–86).
- **Danes** in North Schleswig; **Alsace-Lorraine** as a Reichsland without full state status until 1911.
- **Jews:** Legal emancipation completed (North German Confederation 1869, extended 1871); but organised **antisemitism** from the late 1870s (Wilhelm Marr coined "antisemitism" in 1879; Treitschke's "The Jews are our misfortune," 1879; antisemitic parties won up to 16 Reichstag seats in 1893); informal exclusion from officer corps and senior civil service.

**Women:** Excluded from the vote; admitted to universities only from 1900 (Baden) to 1908 (Prussia); legally subordinate in marriage under the Civil Code (BGB, 1900); a large women's movement (bourgeois *Bund Deutscher Frauenvereine*, 1894; proletarian women's movement led by Clara Zetkin). Women's industrial employment grew.

## 4. Everyday Life

- **Urbanisation:** Population grew from 41 million (1871) to 65 million (1910); cities over 100,000 went from 8 to 48; by 1910, 21.3% lived in big cities (LeMO). Internal migration east → west (Ruhr Poles). Berlin tenements (*Mietskasernen*) crowded; lodgers renting only a bed (*Schlafgänger*).
- **Work:** 10–12-hour days common around 1890; factory discipline; strong craft identities.
- **Social insurance:** Health (1883), accident (1884), old-age and invalidity (1889) — the world's first national social insurance system, designed partly to wean workers from socialism.
- **Schooling:** Near-universal elementary literacy; a strongly tiered system (Volksschule for the masses; Gymnasium and university for the few).
- **Military service:** 2–3 years of conscription; the army as "school of the nation" and of obedience; veterans' associations (*Kriegervereine*) with millions of members.
- **Associations:** Choirs, gymnastics, cycling, sports, church groups, SPD workers' culture clubs — Germany as a "society of associations."

## 5. Economy

Germany became Europe's leading industrial economy by 1914 in chemicals, electrical engineering and steel ("second industrial revolution"). Big banks financed industry; cartels were legal and common ("organised capitalism"). Agriculture was protected by tariffs from 1879 (the "marriage of iron and rye" — alliance of heavy industry and East Elbian grain producers). See `07_industrial_society.md`.

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

- **Nationalism** shifted from liberal-oppositional to conservative-state-centred and imperialist; radical-nationalist pressure groups (Pan-German League 1891, Navy League 1898 with ~1 million members incl. corporate members, Colonial Society) pushed the government to be more aggressive.
- **Liberalism** split and weakened after 1878–79 (Bismarck's turn to protectionism and anti-socialism).
- **Political Catholicism** (Centre), **Social Democracy** (Marxist theory, reformist practice — see `07_industrial_society.md`).
- **Antisemitism** as a political code (Shulamit Volkov's "cultural code") for anti-modern, anti-liberal resentment.
- **Social Darwinism, racial thought, *völkisch* ideas** among intellectuals and radical nationalists.
- **What ordinary people believed:** Reichstag voting shows deep and stable milieu loyalties; high turnout; strong regional and confessional identities; widespread monarchism alongside growing support for socialists.

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

- A growing SPD demanding democratisation; Prussian suffrage reform blocked.
- The "Reichstag's rise" with the expanding state.
- Fiscal crisis (who pays for the fleet and army?) — the 1913 defence levy was the first direct Reich tax on property.
- Foreign policy: Weltpolitik, naval race with Britain, encirclement anxieties, Balkan crises.
- War in 1914; and then defeat.

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE (1914):** Semi-constitutional monarchy with a vibrant mass-party democracy in the electorate but not in government.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Democratisation demands; nationalist radicalism; war (1914–18) and military dictatorship; hunger; defeat.
↓
**TRANSITION (crisis):** October 1918 constitutional reform (parliamentarisation) → November revolution (sailors' revolt at Kiel, councils) → Kaiser's abdication (9 Nov) → republic proclaimed.
↓
**AFTER:** Weimar Republic (see `twentieth_century/02_weimar_republic.md`).

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People? — 1871–1914

National citizenship; social insurance; mass literacy; urban life; voting as a routine act; rising real wages from the 1880s; conscription on the Prussian model everywhere.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

Federalism and state monarchies; noble dominance in army and diplomacy; confessional milieus; the gap between electoral democracy and governmental accountability.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| The Empire was a simple autocracy | It had universal male suffrage, a free press (largely), rule of law and powerful parties; it was a *semi*-authoritarian constitutional monarchy. |
| Wilhelm II ruled personally | His "personal rule" was erratic; bureaucracy, military and parliament constrained him. |
| Social insurance was pure benevolence | It was also a political strategy against the SPD. |
| The road to 1933 started in 1871 | A teleological view many historians now reject or heavily qualify. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **Sonderweg (Special Path):** Hans-Ulrich Wehler (*Das Deutsche Kaiserreich*, 1973) — pre-industrial elites (Junkers, military, bureaucracy) blocked democratisation, used "social imperialism" and "negative integration"; Germany diverged from the "Western" path of bourgeois democracy, creating preconditions for Nazism.

  *In plain words:* Did the German Empire take a 'special path' (Sonderweg) towards Nazism? Hans-Ulrich Wehler said yes: old pre-industrial elites — landowning nobles (Junkers), the military, the bureaucracy — blocked democracy, diverted discontent into colonial expansion ('social imperialism') and united people against supposed internal enemies ('negative integration'). Unlike Western countries, Germany thus never became a middle-class democracy.
- **Critique:** David Blackbourn & Geoff Eley (*The Peculiarities of German History*, 1984) — there was no "normal" Western path; the German bourgeoisie was successful economically and culturally ("silent bourgeois revolution"); popular mobilisation came from below, not only elite manipulation. Thomas Nipperdey stressed the Empire's ambivalence and openness ("grey tones").

  *In plain words:* Was there really a 'normal' Western path from which Germany strayed? David Blackbourn and Geoff Eley said no such norm existed: Germany's middle class succeeded in business and culture (a 'silent bourgeois revolution'), and mass politics grew from below rather than being steered by elites. Thomas Nipperdey stressed that the Empire was mixed and open-ended — 'grey tones', not black and white.
- **Kocka's moderated Sonderweg (1988):** meaningful comparative differences (bureaucratic tradition, weak parliamentarism) without determinism.

  *In plain words:* Can the special-path idea survive in a milder form? Jürgen Kocka (1988) argued that Germany did differ from Western countries in meaningful ways — a strong tradition of bureaucratic rule and a weak parliament — but that these differences did not make later catastrophe inevitable.
- **Democratisation view:** Margaret Lavinia Anderson (*Practicing Democracy*, 2000) — Germans learned democratic practices through fiercely contested elections. Benjamin Ziemann (bpb 2016) warns against reading the period "from its end."

  *In plain words:* Were Germans in the Empire learning democracy? Margaret Lavinia Anderson argues yes: fiercely contested elections taught voters democratic habits in practice. Benjamin Ziemann adds a warning against reading the period backwards from how it ended, as if its whole history pointed to that end.
- **The 2021 "Richter debate":** Hedwig Richter argued the Empire had considerable democratic vitality and Germany belonged to the West; Eckart Conze, Ulrich Herbert and others objected that she underplayed authoritarian structures, nationalism and antisemitism — Herbert: "The concept of democracy is devalued if one detects an element of 'democracy' in the rule of the Nazi regime." (Metzler, *Public History Weekly*, 2021). **CONTESTED.**

  *In plain words:* How democratic was the German Empire? In 2021 Hedwig Richter argued that it had real democratic energy and that Germany belonged to the West. Eckart Conze, Ulrich Herbert and others replied that she played down its authoritarian structures, nationalism and antisemitism; Herbert warned that 'democracy' loses meaning if one finds democratic elements even in Nazi rule. The dispute remains open.

### If you remember only 5 things

1. Men had the vote, but the government answered to the Kaiser, not to parliament — a "system of avoided decisions."
2. Prussia dominated through the Kaiser, the Chancellor and its blocking power in the Bundesrat.
3. Society was organised into stable milieus — Protestant-conservative, liberal, Catholic, socialist — each with its own party and culture.
4. The state persecuted Catholics and socialists, failed, and ended up having to negotiate with them.
5. Whether the Empire was an authoritarian "special path" or a democratising modern society is still debated.
