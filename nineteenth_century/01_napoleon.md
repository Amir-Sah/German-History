## 1. Period in One Paragraph

In twenty years, French revolutionary and Napoleonic power destroyed the old political map of Germany. France annexed the left bank of the Rhine (1797/1801). To compensate the princes who lost land there, the **Reichsdeputationshauptschluss (1803)** abolished almost all church states and most imperial cities, handing them to secular princes — some five million people received new rulers. In 1806 sixteen princes left the Empire to form Napoleon's **Confederation of the Rhine**, and Emperor Francis II laid down the imperial crown: the Holy Roman Empire ended after about 850 years. Bavaria, Württemberg, Baden and others became much bigger, more centralised "middle states," introducing French-style administration, legal equality before the law and religious equality. Prussia, crushed in 1806, responded with its own reforms from above (abolition of hereditary serfdom, municipal self-government, military reform). The "Wars of Liberation" (1813–15) ended French rule, and the Congress of Vienna created a **German Confederation** of about 39 sovereign states. The nationalist legend of a people rising against Napoleon was largely constructed later.

## 2. The Political Structure

### Three sequences of change

**(a) The territorial revolution, 1801–1806**
- **Lunéville (1801):** Left bank ceded to France; compensation promised.
- **Reichsdeputationshauptschluss (25 Feb 1803):** Secularisation of all ecclesiastical principalities (except the Mainz elector's, moved to Regensburg); **mediatisation** of ~45 imperial cities (Burgdorf). Winners: Baden, Württemberg, Bavaria, Prussia.
- **1804:** Francis proclaims himself Emperor of Austria.
- **Confederation of the Rhine (July 1806):** 16 princes declare separation from the Empire; later ~36 members. Bavaria and Württemberg become kingdoms, Baden a grand duchy.
- **6 August 1806:** Francis II declares the imperial office and the Empire dissolved (legally questionable, but final). Many small territories (imperial knights, counts) were mediatised.

**(b) Reform in the Rhine Confederation states (Bavaria under Montgelas, Baden, Württemberg, Westphalia)**
- Centralised ministries; uniform provinces; abolition of estates' privileges; religious equality (important for Catholic–Protestant mixed new territories); Bavarian constitution 1808; Code Napoléon in some states (Westphalia, Baden adopted a version: *Badisches Landrecht*, 1810); abolition of serfdom in law (Bavaria 1808). Purpose: integrating new subjects and extracting revenue and soldiers for Napoleon.

**(c) Prussian reforms (1807–1819)** after defeat at Jena–Auerstedt (1806) and the Peace of Tilsit (1807), which halved Prussia's territory:
- **October Edict (9 Oct 1807, Stein):** abolished hereditary subjection (*Erbuntertänigkeit*) from 1810; free choice of occupation; free sale of land (nobles could buy peasant land, burghers noble land).
- **Municipal Ordinance (1808):** elected town councils — self-government for property-owning citizens.
- **Regulation Edict (1811, Hardenberg):** peasants could acquire ownership of their holdings by ceding **one-third to one-half of their land** to the lord (or paying); an 1816 declaration excluded smaller holdings without draft animals.
- **Freedom of trade (1810–11)**, **Jewish edict (1812)** granting Prussian Jews citizenship with restrictions.
- **Military reform (Scharnhorst, Gneisenau, Boyen):** officer careers opened to talent (in principle), abolition of degrading punishments, and **universal conscription (1813/14)**.
- **Education (Humboldt):** University of Berlin 1810; humanist gymnasium.
- **Constitution promised (1815) — never granted** until 1848/50.

### Regime Matrix — a Rhine Confederation state (e.g., Bavaria under Montgelas, 1799–1817)

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Formally ruled | King (from 1806), sovereign after leaving the Empire. |
| 2 | Actually held power | Leading minister and bureaucracy; externally, Napoleon. |
| 3 | Acquired | Dynastic; enlarged by French grace. |
| 4 | Maintained | Centralised administration, police, army; French alliance. |
| 5 | Who could challenge | Napoleon; Austria; rebellious new subjects (Tyrol 1809, Andreas Hofer). |
| 6 | Money | Uniform taxes; secularised church property; mediatised lands. |
| 7 | Enforcement | Prefect-style officials, gendarmerie, conscription. |
| 8 | Representation | Minimal; 1808 constitution largely on paper; 1818 constitution created a bicameral diet. |
| 9 | Rights | Legal equality in principle; religious equality; end of serfdom in law. |
| 10 | Opponents | Repression (Tyrolean rebellion crushed 1809–10). |
| 11 | Connecting institutions | Bureaucracy, army, state-controlled churches and schools. |
| 12 | Ordinary person's view | New rulers, new flags, heavy conscription for Napoleon's wars (e.g., ~30,000 Bavarians marched to Russia in 1812; few returned), and new legal equality. |

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** Rhine Confederation constitutions promised equality and representation; in practice they served bureaucratic absolutism and French war needs. Prussian reforms promised a "nation of citizens"; the October Edict and Regulation Edict in practice strengthened large estates (the rbb Preußen-Chronik notes nobles expanded their holdings and kept police and judicial powers).

## 3. Society

- **Church:** Lost its principalities and much property; Catholic institutions (universities, monasteries) closed; long-term, a more Rome-oriented, less aristocratic Catholic Church (ultramontanism) developed.
- **Imperial knights and counts:** Lost sovereignty (mediatised), kept some privileges.
- **Peasants:** Legally freed from serfdom (east) — but in Prussia at a price: land cessions or debts; many smallholders became landless labourers.
- **Jews:** Partial emancipation (France's Rhineland; Westphalia 1808; Prussia 1812), partly rolled back after 1815.
- **Bureaucrats:** Became the dominant reforming force — "revolution from above."

## 4. Everyday Life

War: requisitions, quartering, conscription on an unprecedented scale. Ute Planert's study of southern Germany shows ordinary people experienced French wars primarily as **coercion, suffering and disruption**, with widespread resistance to conscription, and very limited "national" enthusiasm. In Prussia, 1813 saw more genuine mobilisation (volunteer units like the Lützow Free Corps, Landwehr militia), but volunteers were disproportionately students and educated youths.

## 5. Economy

The Continental System (1806–13) blocked British trade — hurting ports, protecting some continental manufactures. Secularisation transferred huge property. Freedom of trade in Prussia weakened guilds. Heavy war costs and debt.

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

- **Early German nationalism:** Fichte's *Addresses to the German Nation* (1807–08), Ernst Moritz Arndt, Friedrich Ludwig Jahn (gymnastics movement, 1811) — anti-French, cultural and sometimes ethnic nationalism, largely among educated young men.
- **Romanticism:** Idealised the Middle Ages and the Empire; Burgdorf notes that after 1806 there was a "collective retreat into medieval romanticism."
- **Liberal reform ideas** among officials: rule of law, property rights, self-government.
- **Reactions to 1806:** Mixed — some indifference, some dismay. Goethe's mother wrote that it felt like the death of an old friend long given up by the doctors; the removal of prayers for emperor and Empire from church services caused local unrest.

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

French military superiority; the princes' desire for territorial gains; the Empire's inability to defend itself; Prussia's collapse in 1806; fiscal crisis.

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE (1790):** ~300 territories in a legal Empire; church states; imperial cities; serfdom in the east.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Revolutionary war; French annexation; compensation politics; princely greed.
↓
**TRANSITION (crisis):** 1803 secularisation → 1806 end of Empire → Prussian collapse.
↓
**AFTER:** ~39 sovereign states; enlarged middle states with modern bureaucracies; reformed Prussia enlarged by Rhineland and Westphalia (1815); German Confederation (see `02_german_confederation.md`).

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

Millions changed rulers; many changed legal systems; serfdom ended in law; religious minorities gained equality in many states; men were conscripted in large numbers; church lands and monasteries vanished from the landscape.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

Monarchy; nobility's social dominance (especially in Prussia's east); the princes' sovereignty (now greater than ever); local lordship in practice.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| The Germans rose as one nation against Napoleon | A myth constructed later (Planert); many Germans fought *for* Napoleon; southern states switched sides only in late 1813. |
| Napoleon brought only oppression | He also brought legal equality, religious toleration and administrative modernisation — often welcomed by minorities and officials. |
| Prussian reforms freed the peasants | They ended personal subjection but the price benefited large landowners. |
| The Empire's end went unnoticed | Reactions ranged from indifference to profound dismay. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **"Defensive modernisation" (Hans-Ulrich Wehler) / "revolution from above":** Reforms as responses to French pressure. Consensus on the fact; debate on how "modern" the outcomes were.
- **Myth of the Wars of Liberation:** Planert (2007) and others vs older national narratives.
- **Rhine Confederation reforms:** Once dismissed as French imposition, now seen (e.g., by Elisabeth Fehrenbach) as a significant modernising phase for southern Germany.

### If you remember only 5 things

1. French power, not German nationalism, destroyed the Holy Roman Empire.
2. The winners were the middle-sized princes, who became sovereign kings and modernised by decree.
3. Prussia reformed from above after defeat — freeing peasants on terms that favoured landlords.
4. For ordinary people the era meant conscription, requisitions, new rulers and new legal equality.
5. The "people's uprising" of 1813 is largely a later myth.
