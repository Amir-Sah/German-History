## 1. Period in One Paragraph

Germany was unified not by a national parliament but by the Prussian state, through three wars in seven years. In 1862 Prussia's liberal-dominated parliament refused to fund King Wilhelm I's army reform; the king appointed Otto von Bismarck, who governed without a legal budget and declared that the great questions of the day would be decided "not by speeches and majority resolutions … but by iron and blood." Bismarck then used short, limited wars — against Denmark (1864), Austria and its German allies (1866) and France (1870–71) — to exclude Austria, annex or subordinate northern Germany, and bring the southern states into a Prussian-led federal empire proclaimed at Versailles on 18 January 1871. Many liberals, who had wanted unity *through* freedom, accepted unity *before* freedom. The result was a "revolution from above": a nation-state that met the national movement's central goal, but under monarchical and military control, with a parliament elected by universal male suffrage but without power over the government.

## 2. The Political Structure

### The steps

| Step | What happened | Structural significance |
|---|---|---|
| **1858–62 "New Era"** | Liberal revival in Prussia; National Association (*Nationalverein*, 1859) campaigns for kleindeutsch unity under Prussia | Liberal nationalism looks to Prussia |
| **1862–66 Constitutional conflict** | Prussian Landtag refuses army budget; Bismarck governs without it ("gap theory": where the constitution is silent, the king decides) | Executive power beats parliamentary budget right |
| **1864 Danish War** | Prussia and Austria take Schleswig and Holstein | Joint administration → friction |
| **1866 German War** | Prussia defeats Austria and most middle states (Bavaria, Saxony, Hanover, Württemberg…) at Königgrätz; Confederation dissolved | Austria excluded from Germany; Prussia annexes Hanover, Hesse-Kassel, Nassau, Frankfurt, Schleswig-Holstein |
| **1866 Indemnity Act** | Prussian Landtag retroactively legalises Bismarck's budgetless rule; liberals split — **National Liberals** accept Bismarck | Liberalism divides into pro-government and oppositional wings |
| **1867 North German Confederation** | Federal state north of the Main; Reichstag by universal male suffrage; Bundesrat | Blueprint of the 1871 constitution |
| **Southern states** | Secret military alliances with Prussia (1866); Customs Parliament (1868) — southern voters elect anti-Prussian majorities | Unity not popular in the south |
| **1870 Ems Dispatch; Franco-Prussian War** | Bismarck edits a telegram to provoke France; France declares war; southern states join; France defeated (Sedan, Sept 1870) | National war creates emotional unity |
| **Nov 1870 treaties** | Southern states join; Bavaria gets reserved rights (own army in peacetime, post, railways); Ludwig II paid secretly from the "Guelph fund" | Negotiated princely union |
| **18 Jan 1871** | Wilhelm I proclaimed German Emperor in the Hall of Mirrors, Versailles — before princes and officers, no parliamentarians invited | Unity as a princely-military act |
| **Frankfurt Peace (May 1871)** | Alsace-Lorraine annexed as "Reichsland"; 5 billion francs indemnity | Lasting Franco-German enmity |

### Regime Matrix — Prussia during the constitutional conflict (1862–66)

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Formally ruled | King under the 1850 constitution with a bicameral Landtag. |
| 2 | Actually held power | King + Bismarck + army leadership; parliament's budget right ignored. |
| 3 | Acquired | Royal appointment of the minister-president. |
| 4 | Maintained | Army, bureaucracy, press controls; later, military success. |
| 5 | Challengers | Liberal majority in the Landtag (Progress Party). |
| 6 | Money | Taxes kept being collected without an approved budget. |
| 7 | Enforcement | Bureaucracy obeyed the king; courts did not stop it. |
| 8 | Representation | Three-class suffrage Landtag. |
| 9 | Rights | Constitutional rights on paper; press decrees (1863) restricted them. |
| 10 | Opponents | Disciplinary action against liberal officials; press prosecutions. |
| 11 | Connecting institutions | Army, bureaucracy, Protestant church, conservative associations. |
| 12 | Ordinary person's view | Little change in daily life; enthusiasm after victories in 1866 and 1870. |

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** The 1850 Prussian constitution gave the Landtag budget rights; Bismarck showed that the crown could simply govern without them if the army and bureaucracy stayed loyal — and that victory could buy retroactive legitimacy (Indemnity Act 1866).

## 3. Society

- **Liberal bourgeoisie:** split between those who prioritised national unity and economic liberalisation (National Liberals) and those who prioritised parliamentary rights (Progress Party).
- **Catholics** (especially in the south and Rhineland): feared a Protestant Prussian empire; the exclusion of Catholic Austria left them a one-third minority.
- **Workers:** First workers' parties formed during unification — Lassalle's General German Workers' Association (ADAV, 1863), Bebel and Liebknecht's Social Democratic Workers' Party (SDAP, 1869). Bebel and Liebknecht opposed the annexation of Alsace-Lorraine and were tried for treason (1872).
- **Minorities newly included:** Poles (Prussian east), Danes (North Schleswig), French-speakers and Alsatians, Hanoverian loyalists ("Guelphs").

## 4. Everyday Life

Rapid industrial growth in the 1850s–60s; the wars were short but costly (e.g., ~45,000 German dead in 1870–71). National celebrations after Sedan; Sedan Day (2 September) became a national holiday. For many in annexed Hanover, Hesse or Frankfurt, 1866 meant resented Prussian rule.

## 5. Economy

The 1850s boom; railways, coal and iron; the Zollverein's liberal trade treaties (with France, 1862). After 1871 the French indemnity fuelled a speculative boom (*Gründerzeit*) and the crash of 1873. The North German Confederation passed liberal economic laws (freedom of trade 1869; uniform weights, measures and currency later — the Mark in 1873).

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

- **Realpolitik:** Ludwig von Rochau's term (1853) — politics based on power, not principle; adopted by many liberals.
- **Borussian (Prussian-school) history:** Droysen, Sybel, Treitschke argued Prussia had a historic "mission" to unify Germany — a retrospective legitimation.
- **Großdeutsch Catholic and southern particularist** resentment; **democratic** critics (e.g., in Württemberg's People's Party).

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

The unresolved national question after 1848; Austro-Prussian rivalry; economic integration; European shifts (Crimean War weakened Austria–Russia ties; Italian unification 1859–61 showed how war could unify); Prussia's army reform and constitutional conflict.

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE:** Confederation of sovereign states with Austria presiding; failed 1848 hopes.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Rivalry; national movement; industrial growth; Prussian military modernisation.
↓
**TRANSITION (crisis):** Three wars (1864, 1866, 1870–71).
↓
**AFTER:** A Prussian-dominated federal empire without Austria (see `06_german_empire.md`); Austria reorganised as Austria-Hungary (1867).

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

A new national state, uniform laws, currency and markets; conscription extended across Germany on the Prussian model; national symbols and holidays. For annexed populations and minorities, new pressures to conform.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

The monarchies (22 princely states + 3 free cities remained as federal members); aristocratic dominance of army and diplomacy; confessional divides; regional identities.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| Bismarck had a master plan from 1862 | He exploited opportunities; outcomes were contingent (Austrian or French victory was possible). |
| All Germans longed for Prussian-led unity | Many southerners, Catholics, Hanoverians and democrats opposed it. |
| Unification was a German "people's" achievement | It was a treaty among princes backed by Prussian arms, though popular national enthusiasm followed. |
| "Germany" in 1871 = all German speakers | It excluded Austria's ~10 million German speakers and included several million non-Germans. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **Bismarck as genius vs product of structures:** Older biographies (Gall, Pflanze) vs structural accounts (economic integration, Zollverein, liberal nationalism).
- **"Revolution from above" (Wehler) and "white revolutionary" (Gall):** Bismarck used revolutionary means (war, universal suffrage) for conservative ends.
- **Inevitability:** Recent historians (Clark, Breuilly) stress contingency.

## 13. Confidence Assessment

| Finding | Confidence |
|---|---|
| Unification achieved through Prussian wars and princely treaties | HIGH |

### If you remember only 5 things

1. Germany was unified by Prussian wars and princely treaties, not by a parliament.
2. Bismarck showed that a king with a loyal army could govern without parliament's money — and victory would excuse it.
3. Many liberals chose unity first and freedom later; that choice shaped the Empire.
4. Austria's exclusion (1866) decided what "Germany" would mean for the next 150 years.
5. Unity was not universally popular; Catholics, southerners and minorities were wary.
