## 1. Period in One Paragraph

Germany acquired a colonial empire late and quickly: in 1884–85 Bismarck placed merchant holdings under "imperial protection," creating colonies in **South West Africa** (Namibia), **Togo**, **Cameroon**, **German East Africa** (Tanzania, Rwanda, Burundi) and the Pacific (New Guinea, Samoa, and others), later adding Kiautschou (Qingdao) in China (1897). By area it was the third- or fourth-largest colonial empire, yet it was economically marginal and home to only a few tens of thousands of Germans. Colonial rule was violent: wars against the Herero and Nama in South West Africa (1904–08) — which Germany recognised as **genocide** in 2021 — and the Maji Maji war in East Africa (1905–07), where famine as a weapon killed very large numbers (estimates up to ~200,000–300,000). Germany lost its colonies in the First World War (Versailles 1919), but colonial ideas, racial science, and nostalgia survived into the Weimar and Nazi eras. Since the 2000s, colonial history has become central to German memory debates.

## 2. The Political Structure

### Regime Matrix — German colonial rule (e.g., South West Africa)

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Formally ruled | The Kaiser via the Reich Colonial Office (from 1907; earlier the Foreign Office's colonial department); a governor in each colony. |
| 2 | Actually held power | Governors, military commanders (*Schutztruppe*), settler interests, trading and land companies; local intermediaries (chiefs) where co-opted. |
| 3 | Acquired | "Protection treaties" with local leaders (often misunderstood or coerced), conquest, and European diplomacy (Berlin Conference 1884–85). |
| 4 | Maintained | Military force, collective punishment, land seizure, forced labour, taxation, divide-and-rule. |
| 5 | Who could challenge | African and Pacific societies through resistance and war; missionaries and SPD/Centre critics in the Reichstag (budget power over colonies). |
| 6 | Money | Colonies ran deficits covered by Reich subsidies; local head/hut taxes compelled wage labour. |
| 7 | Enforcement | Schutztruppe, police, flogging (legal for colonial subjects), courts with separate law for "natives." |
| 8 | Representation | None for colonised people; limited councils for settlers. |
| 9 | Rights | Colonised people had no citizenship rights; separate legal status; mixed marriages banned in SW Africa (1905), East Africa and Samoa. |
| 10 | Opponents | Killed in war, executed, imprisoned in concentration camps (*Konzentrationslager* used by German authorities in SW Africa), dispossessed. |
| 11 | Connecting institutions | Missions, schools, trading firms, military. |
| 12 | Colonised person's view | Land loss, forced labour, violence, taxation; in some regions, mission schooling. |

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** "Protection treaties" claimed consent and protection; in practice, colonies were ruled by force and legal discrimination, with the Reichstag only intermittently able to check abuses (it debated scandals, e.g., Carl Peters's atrocities, and in 1906 refused supplementary funds for the war in SW Africa — leading to the "Hottentot election" of 1907, won by pro-colonial parties).

## 3. Society — in Germany

Colonial enthusiasm was concentrated in pressure groups (German Colonial Society, founded 1887; ~40,000 members), commercial circles (Hamburg, Bremen), missions and the educated middle class. Colonialism penetrated popular culture: "colonial goods" shops, advertising, *Völkerschauen* (human zoos, e.g., Hagenbeck in Hamburg), school atlases, novels.

## 4. Everyday Life — in the colonies

For colonised societies: land expropriation (especially settler colonies in SW Africa), cattle seizures, forced labour, taxes payable only in cash, destruction of political structures, missions reshaping religion and education.

## 5. Economy

Colonies accounted for well under 1% of German foreign trade; Germany's key economic relationships were with Europe and the Americas. Colonies cost more than they returned to the Reich budget, though specific firms profited.

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

- **Imperialism as national prestige** ("a place in the sun," Bülow 1897); **Weltpolitik**.
- **Social imperialism (Wehler's thesis):** colonialism as diversion from domestic class conflict — **CONTESTED**.
- **Racial ideas** and **"living space" (*Lebensraum*)** — Friedrich Ratzel coined *Lebensraum* (1901); the term later became central to Nazi eastward expansion.
- **Critics:** SPD (Bebel attacked colonial atrocities), parts of the Centre, some liberals — though critics often shared assumptions of European superiority.

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

Resistance wars; scandals; the First World War — colonies occupied by Allied forces (except East Africa, where Lettow-Vorbeck's campaign, relying on African soldiers and porters, caused huge civilian suffering).

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE (pre-1884):** Merchants, missionaries and explorers; no formal empire.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Great-power rivalry; nationalist lobbies; commercial interests; domestic politics.
↓
**TRANSITION:** 1884–85 protectorates; 1904–08 genocidal war; 1907 colonial reforms (Dernburg).
↓
**AFTER:** Loss of colonies (1914–19); Versailles Article 119; "colonial revisionism" in Weimar; Nazi colonial plans shelved in favour of eastern conquest; post-1945 near-forgetting; since 2000s, restitution, apology and memory debates.

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

In Germany: exposure to colonial imagery and racial hierarchies in everyday culture. In the colonies: devastation for many societies, most extremely for the Herero and Nama.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

Germany's economy remained Europe-focused; after 1919 colonial ideas persisted without colonies.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| German colonialism was short and therefore unimportant | It was brief but included the first genocide of the 20th century and shaped racial thinking. |
| German colonialism was "milder" | The Herero/Nama and Maji Maji wars show otherwise. |
| There is a straight line from Namibia to Auschwitz | The "continuity thesis" (Jürgen Zimmerer) is debated; see §12. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **Herero and Nama genocide:** Scholarly consensus that the 1904–08 war became genocidal after General Lothar von Trotha's orders (October 1904 against the Herero, April 1905 against the Nama). The German Federal Government recognised it as genocide in 2021, asked for forgiveness, and initialled a Joint Declaration with Namibia; implementation and compensation (a €1.1 billion development-aid package was announced in 2021) remain disputed by Herero and Nama representatives, who were not direct parties. The Federal Government estimates approximately 100,000 deaths. (Bundesregierung, 28 May 2025)
- **Continuity from colonialism to the Holocaust:** Jürgen Zimmerer and Isabel Hull argue that colonial practices (annihilation warfare, camps, racial law) formed part of the genealogy of Nazi crimes; critics (e.g., Robert Gerwarth & Stephan Malinowski; Birthe Kundrus) caution against simple lines and stress differences.
- **"Historikerstreit 2.0" (2021–22):** A. Dirk Moses's essay "The German Catechism" challenged the centrality of Holocaust singularity in German memory culture; critics defended it. **CONTESTED.**

## 13. Confidence Assessment

| Finding | Confidence |
|---|---|

### If you remember only 5 things

1. Germany's colonial empire was late, short (1884–1919) and economically marginal — but violent.
2. The war against the Herero and Nama became a genocide; Germany formally recognised it in 2021.
3. Colonialism was driven by nationalist lobbies and prestige more than by economic need.
4. Colonial racial ideas and the term *Lebensraum* outlived the colonies.
5. How colonialism connects to Nazi crimes is a live scholarly debate, not a settled line.
