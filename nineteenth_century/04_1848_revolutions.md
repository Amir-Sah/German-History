## 1. Period in One Paragraph

In March 1848, news of the February revolution in Paris triggered uprisings, mass petitions and street fighting across the German states. Frightened princes appointed liberal "March ministries," lifted censorship and promised constitutions. A **National Assembly** elected by (mostly) broad male suffrage met in Frankfurt's St. Paul's Church (*Paulskirche*) to create a German nation-state with a constitution. It drafted a catalogue of **basic rights** and, in March 1849, a constitution for a federal hereditary empire with a parliament elected by universal male suffrage — choosing the "small German" solution without Austria. But the King of Prussia, Frederick William IV, refused the crown ("a dog-collar" from the gutter). Meanwhile Austria and Prussia had recovered militarily; radical uprisings in Saxony, the Palatinate and Baden (spring 1849) were crushed by Prussian troops. The revolution failed. But it was no mere failure: it defined the agenda of German politics — nation, constitution, rights, social question — and even the victors learned from it.

## 2. The Political Structure

### Actors and arenas

| Arena | What happened |
|---|---|
| **Streets and villages** | Urban barricade fights (Berlin, 18 March; Vienna); peasant revolts against remaining feudal dues (Baden, Württemberg, Silesia); attacks on Jews in some rural areas; workers' and artisans' protests. |
| **State governments** | Liberal "March ministries" (e.g., Camphausen–Hansemann in Prussia). |
| **Pre-Parliament & Frankfurt National Assembly** (18 May 1848 – 1849) | ~800 deputies over its life (~585 at a time); dominated by educated professionals (lawyers, officials, professors — the "professors' parliament") ; first proto-parties (clubs named after meeting places: Café Milani (right), Casino (right centre), Württemberger Hof, Westendhall, Deutscher Hof, Donnersberg (left)). |
| **Provisional Central Power** | Archduke Johann of Austria as "Imperial Regent" (*Reichsverweser*) — with no army or money of its own. |
| **State parliaments** | Prussian National Assembly (Berlin) — dissolved by the king in December 1848, who then imposed (*oktroyierte*) a constitution. |
| **Workers' organisations** | Stephan Born's *Allgemeine Deutsche Arbeiterverbrüderung* — first nationwide workers' association. |
| **Catholic associations** | *Piusvereine*; first German Catholic Congress (*Katholikentag*, Mainz 1848). |

### The Paulskirche Constitution (28 March 1849)

- **Basic Rights (proclaimed December 1848):** equality before the law, abolition of noble privileges as an estate, freedom of person, press, religion, assembly, association, science; abolition of the death penalty (except martial law); freedom of movement and occupation; and the end of feudal subordination: "Every relationship of subjection and bondage ceases forever" (*Jeder Unterthänigkeits- und Hörigkeitsverband hört für immer auf*).
- **Structure:** a hereditary emperor; a **bicameral Reichstag** — *Volkshaus* elected by universal, equal, secret male suffrage; *Staatenhaus* representing states; ministers responsible to parliament; an imperial court.
- **Territory:** *kleindeutsch* in effect (Austria refused to split its multinational empire).
- It was never implemented, but Osterhammel calls it "a foundational document of German democracy" (*Basisdokument der deutschen Demokratie*); it served as a model for the Weimar Constitution (1919) and the Basic Law (1949).

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** The Paulskirche constitution (and the Basic Rights law of December 1848) claimed authority for the whole nation, and 28 governments accepted it in April 1849 — but not Prussia, Austria, Bavaria, Saxony or Hanover. Real power never left the princes: they commanded the armies, collected the taxes and ran the bureaucracies, while the Central Power in Frankfurt had neither soldiers nor revenue. The constitution was therefore a programme, not a functioning order.

### Why it failed (standard factors, with debate)

1. **Military power remained with the princes:** armies stayed loyal; the Frankfurt Assembly had no force of its own.
2. **Divisions among revolutionaries:** liberals (constitutional monarchy, property-based order) vs democrats (republic, social reform); fear of "red" revolution pushed liberals toward compromise with the crowns.
3. **Peasants satisfied early:** once feudal dues were abolished or redemption agreed (spring 1848), much of the countryside withdrew.
4. **The national question:** großdeutsch vs kleindeutsch; conflicts with non-German nationalities (Poles in Posen, Czechs refusing Frankfurt, Danes in Schleswig) — the Assembly voted for "German" claims in Posen and Schleswig; the Malmö armistice (Sept 1848) exposed its impotence.
5. **Timing:** Austria and Prussia recovered in autumn 1848 (Vienna retaken October; Prussian assembly dissolved December).
6. **Frederick William IV's refusal** (April 1849): he rejected a crown offered by a parliament as illegitimate.

### Regime Matrix — the revolutionary order (March 1848 – July 1849)

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Who formally ruled? | The German Confederation still existed; in each state the monarch with a liberal "March ministry"; nationally the Frankfurt National Assembly and the Provisional Central Power under Archduke Johann (*Reichsverweser*). |
| 2 | Who actually held power? | The princes, who kept command of armies, taxes and officials; the streets and the assemblies held moral authority only while the crowns were weak (spring–autumn 1848). |
| 3 | How was power acquired? | Revolution — mass protest and barricade fighting forced concessions in March 1848; elections to the National Assembly. |
| 4 | How was power maintained? | Public opinion, a free press, associations, the threat of renewed unrest. |
| 5 | Who could challenge the government? | The crowns and their armies (counter-revolution in Vienna, October 1848, and Berlin, November–December 1848); radical democrats; non-German national movements. |
| 6 | How was money obtained? | The Central Power had no revenue of its own; the states kept their taxes. |
| 7 | How were decisions enforced? | Only through state armies, which obeyed their princes. |
| 8 | How were ordinary people represented? | A National Assembly elected by broad but male-only suffrage; the Paulskirche constitution provided universal, equal, secret male suffrage. |
| 9 | Rights? | The Basic Rights (December 1848) — equality before the law, freedoms of press, religion, assembly and association, the end of feudal subordination; valid only where states accepted them; annulled in 1851. |
| 10 | Opponents? | Open debate during the revolution; after the last uprisings were crushed (1849), courts-martial, executions, prison and emigration. |
| 11 | Institutions connecting state to society | Democratic and constitutional clubs, workers' and Catholic associations (*Piusvereine*), the press, parliaments. |
| 12 | Ordinary person's view | Hope and hunger; the end of remaining feudal dues in many regions — then repression and, for many, emigration. |

## 3. Society

Osterhammel stresses the revolution **mobilised far beyond the bourgeoisie**: peasants, urban workers and craftspeople. He adds that "terror was practised less by the revolutionaries than by their opponents" — e.g., executions after the fall of Vienna (Robert Blum, a Frankfurt deputy, shot November 1848) and after Rastatt (Baden, July 1849). **Women** participated in clubs, petitions, and journalism (Louise Otto-Peters's *Frauen-Zeitung*, 1849), but were excluded from voting. **Jews:** Basic Rights promised full civic equality; some Jewish deputies (Gabriel Riesser, vice-president of the Assembly); simultaneously, anti-Jewish riots in rural Baden and elsewhere.

## 4. Everyday Life

For many peasants the revolution's concrete gain was the end of remaining feudal burdens (with compensation). For urban populations: political clubs, newspapers (press freedom), civic guards, street politics — a burst of participation. After 1849: arrests, trials, and **mass emigration of "Forty-Eighters"** to the USA (e.g., Carl Schurz, later a US senator and cabinet member).

## 5. Economy

The revolution coincided with the tail end of the 1846–47 crisis; recovery from 1849–50 and the boom of the 1850s helped stabilise the restored order. Feudal redemption laws of 1848–50 finished the transition to private property in land.

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

Liberalism, democracy, nationalism, early socialism (*Communist Manifesto* published February 1848, little influence in 1848 itself), political Catholicism, conservatism reorganising (the *Kreuzzeitung*, founded 1848). Nationalism showed its double face: emancipatory for Germans, dismissive of Polish and Danish claims (Wilhelm Jordan's notorious 1848 speech in the Posen debate about "healthy national egoism").

See `02_german_confederation.md` §7: pauperism, hunger, constitutional frustration, economic crisis, European contagion.

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE:** Confederation of princes; censorship; no national parliament.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Hunger; liberal-national movement; Paris February revolution.
↓
**TRANSITION (crisis):** March 1848 revolutions → National Assembly → constitution (1849) → counter-revolution.
↓
**AFTER (1850s "Reaction"):** Confederation restored; Basic Rights annulled (1851); but **Prussia keeps a constitution** (imposed 1848, revised 1850) with a parliament elected under the **three-class suffrage** (voters grouped by taxes paid; each third of tax revenue elected a third of electors) — which lasted until 1918. Most feudal burdens remained abolished.

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

- End of remaining feudal dues and patrimonial rights in many states (though Prussian estate owners kept manorial police powers until the 1872 district reform, and "estate districts" (*Gutsbezirke*) administered by their owners survived until 1927).
- Constitutional states became the norm (Prussia included).
- Experience of mass politics; after defeat, repression and emigration.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

Monarchs controlled armies; aristocratic dominance in Prussia; no national state; no parliamentary government (ministers responsible to kings, not parliaments).

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| 1848 was a purely bourgeois, professors' revolution | Parliament was bourgeois, but the movement included peasants, workers and artisans. |
| 1848 was a total failure | It failed to create a nation-state, but ended feudalism in most places, spread constitutionalism, and produced the template for later German democracy. |
| The failure of 1848 put Germany on a "special path" to Nazism | A teleological argument (A.J.P. Taylor's "turning point where history failed to turn"); contested. |
| Liberals betrayed the revolution out of cowardice | They feared social revolution and civil war; their choices reflected real dilemmas. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **"Failure" vs "learning process":** Older view (Veit Valentin, Taylor) — failed revolution; newer view (Dieter Langewiesche, Osterhammel, Christopher Clark's *Revolutionary Spring*, 2023) — Europe-wide event with lasting institutional effects.
- **Social base:** Marxist/GDR historiography — "bourgeois revolution" betrayed by the bourgeoisie; Western social history — multiple parallel revolutions (national, liberal, democratic, social, peasant) with different aims.
- **1848 and the Sonderweg:** Part of the broader Sonderweg debate (see `06_german_empire.md`).

### If you remember only 5 things

1. In 1848 Germans elected a national parliament and wrote a constitution with sweeping basic rights.
2. It failed because the princes kept the armies — and because revolutionaries were divided over society and borders.
3. Peasants got what they most wanted (end of feudal dues) early and withdrew.
4. Prussia emerged with a constitution but a heavily unequal three-class vote that lasted until 1918.
5. The Paulskirche constitution became the template for later German democracy.
