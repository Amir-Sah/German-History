## 1. Period in One Paragraph

Within two generations, the German lands turned from a mainly agrarian society with widespread rural poverty into Europe's leading industrial economy. Railways (from 1835) pulled coal, iron and engineering; the Ruhr, Saxony, Silesia and Berlin became industrial centres; after 1870 chemical and electrical industries made Germany a world leader in science-based production. Millions moved from the countryside to cities, or emigrated to America. Industrialisation created a new **working class** whose poverty and insecurity was the "social question" of the age. Workers organised — in unions, co-operatives, clubs and above all the **Social Democratic Party (SPD)**, which grew despite a twelve-year ban (1878–90) into the largest party in Germany and the model for socialist parties worldwide. The state responded with both repression and the world's first national social insurance. Social Democracy's combination of revolutionary rhetoric and reformist practice shaped German politics into the 20th century.

## 2. The Political Structure — as it touched labour

- **Before 1860s:** Workers' associations restricted; combination (unions/strikes) illegal in most states until the 1860s (Prussia's Industrial Code of 1869 for the North German Confederation allowed coalitions).
- **Anti-Socialist Law (1878–1890):** After two assassination attempts on Wilhelm I (neither by socialists), Bismarck banned social democratic associations, meetings and publications — but *not* the party's candidates standing for the Reichstag. Some 1,500 people were imprisoned, ~900 expelled from their home towns (standard estimates, **MEDIUM**). The SPD's vote grew regardless; the law lapsed in 1890.
- **Social insurance (1883–1889):** Health insurance (1883, contributions 2/3 worker, 1/3 employer), accident insurance (1884, employers), old-age and invalidity pensions (1889; payable at 70). Self-administered funds gave Social Democrats and unionists administrative experience.
- **Industrial courts (1890), labour protection laws** (Sunday rest, child labour limits) in the "New Course."
- **Police and courts** often sided with employers in strikes; troops were used against miners (e.g., 1889 Ruhr miners' strike).

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** Formally, Reichstag elections were universal, equal and secret for men, and from 1869 workers had the legal right to combine. Yet the Anti-Socialist Law (1878–90) banned social democratic associations, meetings and publications while still letting SPD candidates stand. In practice workers could vote but were kept out of government; police, courts and troops often sided with employers in strikes — while the self-administered social insurance funds quietly gave Social Democrats and unionists administrative experience.

## 3. Society

**The working class** was never homogeneous: skilled craftsmen vs unskilled labourers; miners vs metalworkers vs textile workers (many women); Protestant vs Catholic (Catholic workers often joined Christian unions and voted Centre); Polish miners in the Ruhr; rural labourers (hard to organise).

**Social Democratic milieu:** From cradle to grave — SPD party, Free Trade Unions (~2.5 million members by 1913), consumer co-ops, workers' gymnastics, cycling, choir and education clubs, libraries, newspapers (dozens of dailies; *Vorwärts*). The SPD had ~1 million members by 1914 — the largest party organisation in the world. Historians describe this as a "state within the state" or a "negative integration": workers were excluded from state power but integrated into their own counter-society (Guenther Roth).

**Employers:** Paternalist "masters in their own house" (*Herr im Hause*) — e.g., Krupp's company housing, pensions and hostility to unions.

**Women:** Textile and domestic service employment; home work (*Heimarbeit*); the proletarian women's movement (Clara Zetkin); the SPD was the first major party to demand women's suffrage (Erfurt Programme, 1891).

## 4. Everyday Life

- **Work:** Long hours (12+ hours mid-century, ~9–10 by 1914), piece-rates, factory rules, accidents.
- **Housing:** Overcrowded tenements, lodgers, poor sanitation; cholera in Hamburg (1892) exposed urban inequality.
- **Diet and wages:** Real wages rose substantially from the 1880s to 1914; diet improved (more meat, sugar); but insecurity (illness, accident, old age, unemployment — no unemployment insurance until 1927) remained.
- **Migration:** ~5 million Germans emigrated to the Americas in the 19th century (peaks 1850s and 1880s); internal migration from east to west; by 1914 Germany had become a country of *immigration* for Polish and Italian labourers.

## 5. Economy

| Phase | Features |
|---|---|
| c. 1835–1873 "take-off" | Railways, coal, iron; Zollverein; joint-stock banks (Darmstädter Bank 1853); boom and 1873 crash |
| 1873–c.1895 "Great Depression" (a slowdown, not collapse) | Falling prices; protectionism (1879); cartels; state intervention |
| c.1895–1914 high industrialisation | Chemicals (BASF, Bayer, Hoechst), electrical engineering (Siemens, AEG), steel; export boom; big banks; technical universities and research labs |

**Organised capitalism:** cartels (~300 by 1900 in some counts; coal syndicate 1893), big banks on company boards, employers' associations, and interest groups (Central Association of German Industrialists 1876; Agrarian League 1893).

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

- **Lassalle vs Marx:** Ferdinand Lassalle (ADAV 1863) — state-assisted producer co-ops, universal suffrage, national orientation; Bebel/Liebknecht (SDAP 1869) — Marxist, internationalist. Merged at **Gotha (1875)**.
- **Erfurt Programme (1891):** Marxist theory (Kautsky: capitalism will collapse) + practical demands (universal suffrage including women, 8-hour day, progressive taxation, secular schools). The SPD was, as LeMO quotes, "a revolutionary, but not a revolution-making party."
- **Revisionism debate (1898–1903):** Eduard Bernstein argued capitalism was not collapsing; socialism should be achieved by gradual reform. Formally rejected by the party congress, but in practice unions and many leaders were reformist.
- **Left radicals:** Rosa Luxemburg (mass strike), Karl Liebknecht.
- **Catholic social teaching:** Bishop Ketteler; *Rerum Novarum* (1891); Christian unions; the Centre's social policy.
- **Bourgeois social reform:** *Verein für Socialpolitik* (1872, "armchair socialists" — Schmoller).
- **What workers believed:** Research on workers' libraries and autobiographies suggests many read little Marx; their socialism was about dignity, justice, suffrage and a better life.

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

Growing SPD and unions; mass strikes (1905 Ruhr); suffrage struggles (Prussian suffrage demonstrations 1910); the gap between working-class numbers and political power.

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE (1840s):** Agrarian society with pauperism; artisans; no unions.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Railways, factories, urbanisation; social question; repression.
↓
**TRANSITION:** Organised labour becomes a mass movement; social insurance state emerges.
↓
**AFTER (1914):** SPD votes for war credits (4 Aug 1914) — integration into the nation at war; split in 1917 (USPD); in 1918–19 the SPD becomes the governing party of the republic.

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

From village to city; from seasonal farm work to factory time; from charity to insurance; from subjects to organised voters and union members; higher living standards by 1914 — but with long hours, crowded housing and insecurity.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

Class hierarchies; limited social mobility (few workers' children reached Gymnasium); gender roles; confessional divides even within the working class.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| Industrialisation immediately impoverished workers | Early phase (1840s) saw pauperism mostly from population growth; real wages rose strongly later. |
| Bismarck's social insurance ended the social question | It covered sickness, accident and old age, not unemployment; pensions were small and started at 70. |
| The SPD was a revolutionary party in practice | Its rhetoric was revolutionary; its practice was parliamentary and reformist. |
| All workers were socialists | Catholic, rural and many unskilled workers were not. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **Industrialisation and democracy:** Did rapid industrialisation under an authoritarian state (Gerschenkron's "late development") create social tensions that politics could not absorb (Wehler), or did it create the social basis for democracy (Anderson, Nipperdey)?

  *In plain words:* Did fast industrial growth help or hurt democracy in Germany? Wehler, using Gerschenkron's idea of 'late development' (a country industrialising quickly to catch up with earlier ones), argues that rapid industrialisation under an authoritarian state created social tensions that politics could not absorb. Anderson and Nipperdey argue that it instead built the social foundations for democracy.
- **Negative integration (Roth, 1963) vs participatory integration:** Were workers excluded or gradually included (e.g., through unions, insurance, municipal politics)?

  *In plain words:* Were workers shut out of the Empire or gradually drawn in? Guenther Roth (1963) spoke of 'negative integration': workers were excluded from state power and built their own separate world around the Social Democrats. The opposing view, 'participatory integration', holds that trade unions, social insurance and local politics slowly brought workers into the system.
- **Social insurance:** Social-conservative pacification vs genuine welfare innovation (both).

  *In plain words:* Was Bismarck's social insurance (state schemes covering sickness, accidents and old age) a conservative tool to calm workers and weaken socialism, or a genuine welfare innovation? The chapter's answer is that it was both at once.

### If you remember only 5 things

1. Railways, coal and steel, then chemicals and electricity, made Germany an industrial leader within two generations.
2. The "social question" — urban poverty and insecurity — became the central domestic issue.
3. Workers built a whole counter-society around the SPD and unions; repression made it stronger.
4. The state answered with both persecution and the world's first social insurance.
5. Social Democracy spoke revolution but practised reform — the key to its role after 1918.
