## 1. Period in One Paragraph

German nationalism was born as an idea of educated minorities before it became a mass movement. Around 1800, with no German state, intellectuals imagined a **cultural nation** (*Kulturnation*) defined by language, literature and history. The Napoleonic wars added an anti-French, sometimes ethnic edge (Fichte, Arndt, Jahn). After 1815, nationalism fused with **liberalism**: the demand for a unified Germany went together with demands for constitutions, press freedom and rule of law — against the princes who ran the Confederation. Its carriers were students (*Burschenschaften*), gymnasts, singers, academics, lawyers, journalists and liberal businessmen, organised in associations and festivals (Wartburg 1817, Hambach 1832). The period before the March Revolution of 1848 is called the **Vormärz**. Nationalism was not yet the conservative, state-centred ideology it became after 1871; it was mainly an oppositional, reformist movement — but it already contained exclusionary strands (anti-French, anti-Jewish, anti-Polish) that would grow later.

## 2. The Political Structure

No new structure; nationalism operated as a **movement** against the Confederation's order (see `02_german_confederation.md`). Key arenas:

- **Associations (*Vereine*):** Gymnastics (*Turner*, Jahn, from 1811), singing clubs, student fraternities (Jena 1815, colours black-red-gold from the Lützow Free Corps uniforms), shooting clubs. Since political parties were illegal, clubs were proto-parties.
- **Festivals:** Wartburg festival (1817, students burning "un-German" books); **Hambach festival (1832)**, 20,000–30,000 people calling for unity, freedom and popular sovereignty — followed by federal repression.
- **Southern German parliaments** (Baden, Württemberg, Bavaria) as training grounds of liberal politicians.
- **Press and publishing**, operating around censorship.

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** The Federal Act of 1815 (Art. 13) promised "estates-based constitutions" in every member state, and the Confederation presented itself as the guardian of order in Germany. In practice, the Carlsbad Decrees (1819) imposed censorship and surveillance of universities, political parties were illegal, and national and liberal politics had to survive as gymnastics, singing and student clubs, festivals and a censored press — a movement without legal political institutions.

## 3. Society

**Who were the nationalists?** Overwhelmingly educated, urban, Protestant-majority, male — professors, students, officials, lawyers, some merchants and artisans. Dieter Langewiesche and others show that nationalism long remained compatible with **federal and regional loyalties**: the gymnastics and singing movements celebrated "federative national consciousness" (bpb, Ziemann) and did not demand abolishing the states.

**Who was not?** The rural majority, most Catholics (wary of Prussian-Protestant leadership), most of the nobility, and most workers — though artisans' associations and early socialist groups (e.g., "League of the Just" among German craftsmen in Paris/London) had their own national and international views.

## 4. Everyday Life

For most people, loyalty was to village, region, dynasty and church. National symbols (black-red-gold, songs like *Des Deutschen Vaterland* — "Where is the German's fatherland?") spread through clubs and festivals, reaching tens of thousands but not millions before 1848.

## 5. Economy

The Zollverein and railways created a national *economic* space; economist Friedrich List argued for national economic development with protective tariffs (*National System of Political Economy*, 1841). Liberal businessmen (e.g., in the Rhineland: Hansemann, Camphausen, Mevissen) wanted unity for market and legal reasons.

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

| Idea | Content | Carriers |
|---|---|---|
| **Cultural nation** | Nation = shared language and culture | Herder, Romantics, Grimm brothers (German Dictionary begun 1838) |
| **Liberal nationalism** | Unity + constitution + rights; nation as community of citizens | Southern liberals (Rotteck, Welcker's *Staatslexikon*), Hambach speakers |
| **Democratic nationalism** | Popular sovereignty; republic | Büchner, Hecker, Struve (Baden radicals) |
| **Ethnic/anti-French nationalism** | Nation as community of descent; France as hereditary enemy | Arndt, Jahn (who also had antisemitic views) |
| **Conservative "Christian-German" state** | Monarchy, estates, Christianity; nation as organic whole | Friedrich Wilhelm IV's circle |
| **Großdeutsch vs kleindeutsch** | Should Germany include Austria (großdeutsch) or exclude it under Prussia (kleindeutsch)? | Debate crystallised in 1848 |

**"Where is the German's fatherland?"** — The question itself (Arndt, 1813) shows that the nation's borders were undefined: German speakers lived in Alsace, Bohemia, Switzerland, the Baltic, Hungary; many non-Germans (Poles, Czechs, Danes) lived inside the Confederation. The **Rhine crisis (1840)** — French claims to the Rhine — produced a wave of patriotic songs ("Die Wacht am Rhein"; Hoffmann von Fallersleben's "Lied der Deutschen," 1841, whose third stanza is today's national anthem).

**Distinguishing levels:**
1. *Elites (princes, ministers):* mostly hostile — nationalism threatened sovereignty.
2. *Intellectuals:* the creators.
3. *Movements:* students, gymnasts, singers, liberals.
4. *Ordinary people:* mostly regional/dynastic/religious identities; national feeling episodic (1840 Rhine crisis).

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

The contradiction between economic integration and political fragmentation; broken constitutional promises; repression (which radicalised some); the 1840 Rhine crisis; pauperism; European revolutions.

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE:** Nationalism as an idea of intellectuals.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Associations, festivals, press; economic integration; foreign crises.
↓
**TRANSITION (crisis):** 1848 — the national question on the table in Frankfurt.
↓
**AFTER:** Nationalism survives 1848's defeat and is later captured by Prussian state power (1860s–1871).

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

Before 1848, relatively little — except those in clubs and festivals, and those persecuted for political activity.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

Dynastic, regional and confessional loyalties dominated.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| Germans always wanted a nation-state | Before 1848, national unity was the programme of an educated minority. |
| German nationalism was from the start authoritarian and ethnic | Vormärz nationalism was mainly liberal-oppositional, though exclusionary strands existed. |
| Black-red-gold is an old imperial flag | It comes from 1815 student and volunteer-corps symbolism, invented as tradition. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **Civic vs ethnic nationalism (Hans Kohn's dichotomy):** Kohn classed German nationalism as "eastern/ethnic" vs "western/civic"; current historians find this too schematic — German nationalism had both strands.
- **Modernist theories** (Gellner, Anderson, Hobsbawm) — nations as products of modernity, print and state; applied to Germany by Otto Dann, Dieter Langewiesche. **Critics** (e.g., Caspar Hirschi) argue for older, pre-modern roots in humanism.
- **Federal nationalism (Langewiesche):** German national identity long combined with loyalty to states.

### If you remember only 5 things

1. German nationalism started as a *cultural* idea of educated people in a land without a state.
2. Before 1848 it was usually liberal: unity *and* freedom, against the princes.
3. Associations and festivals were its organising tools, because parties were banned.
4. No one agreed where "Germany" ended — Austria? Bohemia? Schleswig? Alsace?
5. Exclusionary strands (anti-French, anti-Jewish) were present early, but did not yet dominate.
