## 1. Period in One Paragraph

For about five centuries, the Rhine and Danube — and between them a fortified line called the **Limes** — divided what is now Germany into a Roman part (the south and west) and a non-Roman part (the north and east). The Roman part had cities (Cologne, Trier, Mainz, Augsburg, Regensburg), roads, taxes, law, stone buildings, wine and, from the 4th century, Christianity. The frontier was not a wall that separated two worlds: it was a controlled border across which people, goods, soldiers and ideas moved constantly. Rome tried and failed to conquer up to the Elbe (the Varus disaster of 9 CE was the turning point). When Roman power in the West dissolved in the 5th century, Frankish and Alamannic rulers took over, but Roman towns, bishops and Latin literacy survived and became the backbone of the medieval church. This Roman/non-Roman line still shapes Germany's map of old cities, wine regions and — later — Catholic and Protestant areas.

## 2. The Political Structure

### Regime Matrix (Roman provinces of Germania Inferior, Germania Superior, Raetia)

| # | Question | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Who formally ruled? | The Roman emperor, through provincial governors. |
| 2 | Who actually held power? | The army. The Rhine legions were among Rome's largest concentrations of soldiers; their commanders made and unmade emperors (e.g., Vitellius 69 CE; the "Gallic Empire" 260–274 based partly in Cologne and Trier). |
| 3 | How was power acquired? | Imperial appointment; in crises, usurpation by army commanders. |
| 4 | How was power maintained? | Garrisons, forts, roads; alliance and subsidy treaties with peoples beyond the frontier; urban elites co-opted through citizenship and office. |
| 5 | Who could challenge the government? | Mutinous legions; frontier peoples; usurpers. |
| 6 | How was money obtained? | Land and poll taxes, customs at the frontier, requisitions for the army. |
| 7 | How were decisions enforced? | Military and provincial administration; Roman law courts. |
| 8 | How were ordinary people represented? | Not at all at imperial level; local town councils (*curiae*) of property-owners. |
| 9 | Rights? | Roman citizens had legal protections; after 212 CE (Constitutio Antoniniana) nearly all free inhabitants were citizens. Slaves had none. |
| 10 | Opponents? | Executed, enslaved, or deported; frontier peoples subject to punitive expeditions. |
| 11 | Institutions connecting state to society | Army (as employer and customer), towns, markets, roads, later the Church. |
| 12 | Ordinary person's view | Taxes and soldiers; new goods and jobs; law courts for the propertied. |

**What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** In theory the emperor ruled through a civil and military hierarchy; in practice, the frontier army was the political centre of gravity, and on the outer side of the Limes Rome ruled indirectly through client leaders it paid and armed.

## 3. Society

- **Inside the provinces:** A mixed population — Celtic-speaking locals, Germanic-speaking settlers (e.g., the Ubii resettled at Cologne), soldiers from all over the empire, merchants. Social layers: Roman officials and officers; town councillors; artisans and traders; rural tenants; slaves.
- **Religion:** Roman gods, local Celtic and Germanic deities often merged with them (e.g., "Mercury" worship), eastern cults among soldiers (Mithras), and after the 4th century Christianity, with bishops attested at Cologne and Trier. Trier was an imperial residence in the 4th century.
- **Jews:** An imperial decree of 321 CE addressed to Cologne's council is the earliest written evidence of Jewish residents north of the Alps.

## 4. Everyday Life

Provincial town dwellers lived with baths, stone houses, markets and wine production (Moselle, Rhine). Rural life in villas and farms supplied the army. Across the frontier, people traded cattle, hides, amber and slaves for Roman metalwork, pottery and coins; young men served as auxiliaries.

## 5. Economy

The army was the engine: it needed grain, meat, leather, weapons and labour, stimulating agriculture and crafts in the frontier zone. The Limes, per the German Limes Commission, "was not so much a military bulwark but rather a monitored border where entry and exit were controlled and goods were traded or cleared through customs."

## 6. Ideas and Ideologies

Roman imperial ideology (peace through conquest, civilisation vs barbarism); later Christian universalism. Among non-Romans, we have no written self-description.

**Later myth-making:** The victory of **Arminius** (Hermann) over Varus became, from the 16th century (humanists) and especially the 19th century, a national founding myth — the Hermannsdenkmal near Detmold (completed 1875) is its monument. Arminius was a Roman-trained officer and citizen of equestrian rank fighting for leadership among the Cherusci, not for "Germany"; he was killed by his own kinsmen. Heidrun Derks (then head of the Kalkriese museum) argues that the event fits many criteria of a *lieu de mémoire* shaped by "historical re-interpretations since the 16th century," but that the concept has limits for it — the task is to escape this "memory trap." She describes 9 CE as the culmination of "almost 30 years of struggle in Germania" in which Legions XVII, XVIII and XIX were lost.

## 7. What Created Pressure for Change?

- Roman civil wars and the 3rd-century crisis (invasions, usurpations, inflation) → the upper Limes abandoned c. 260; the Alamanni settle in the southwest.
- Frontier peoples increasingly organised into larger confederations.
- Growing reliance of the Roman army on "barbarian" recruits and allied (*foederati*) groups.
- 5th-century collapse of central Western Roman authority.

## 8. Transition

**BEFORE:** A Roman-ruled south and west with cities, taxes and bishops; a non-Roman north and east linked by trade and war.
↓
**PRESSURES:** Imperial crisis; confederation-building beyond the frontier; dependence on allied warriors.
↓
**TRANSITION:** 5th century — Franks take over the Rhineland (Cologne c. 450s), Alamanni the southwest; Roman administration fades, but bishops remain.
↓
**AFTER:** The Frankish kingdom under the Merovingians absorbs the former provinces and then, gradually, the lands beyond the Rhine.

## 9. What Changed for Ordinary People?

In the former provinces: fewer long-distance goods, decline of towns and coin use, rural life more local — but continued Christian worship around surviving bishops. Some towns shrank dramatically; many were never fully abandoned.

## 10. What Stayed the Same?

Roman urban sites, road lines, viticulture, Latin as the language of church and writing, and the bishoprics in old Roman cities (Cologne, Trier, Mainz, Worms, Speyer, Augsburg, Regensburg) that would be central to medieval Germany.

## 11. Important Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| The Limes was a wall to keep barbarians out | A monitored, porous border for control and trade. |
| The Varus battle "founded Germany" | A 19th-century national myth; Arminius was a Roman-trained leader in a local power struggle. |
| Rome "fell" in a sudden collapse | Gradual transformation; Frankish kings often ruled as heirs of Roman power. |
| The Romans never influenced "free Germania" | Archaeology shows deep economic and cultural influence beyond the frontier. |

## 12. Historiographical Debate

- **Where exactly was the Varus battle?** Kalkriese (near Osnabrück) is — in Steinacher's careful phrasing (2022/23) — "identified by many scholars as the place of the Varus defeat"; it is widely accepted as at least one major battle site — thousands of Roman military finds — but some scholars argue it could be a later engagement (e.g., Germanicus's campaigns of 15–16 CE). **MEDIUM CONFIDENCE** for identification.
- **"Transformation" vs "decline and fall":** Did the end of Roman rule mean catastrophe (Bryan Ward-Perkins) or continuity and transformation (the "Transformation of the Roman World" programme)? For the Rhineland, archaeology supports both — continuity of church and some towns, sharp decline of material complexity.

## 13. Confidence Assessment

| Finding | Confidence | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Limes functioned as controlled trading border | HIGH | UNESCO/Limes Commission and archaeological consensus |
| Roman defeat in 9 CE ended plans to conquer to the Elbe | HIGH | Roman sources + archaeology (though Roman campaigns continued to 16 CE, and a Roman army still fought at the Harzhorn near Braunschweig in the 230s CE) |
| Kalkriese = site of the Varus battle | MEDIUM | Strong but not undisputed |
| Continuity of bishoprics across 5th c. | MEDIUM–HIGH | Varies by city; strongest for Cologne, Trier, Mainz |

### If you remember only 5 things

1. For 500 years, half of today's Germany was Roman and half was not — and that line still shows in the map of old cities and bishoprics.
2. The frontier was a contact zone of trade and recruitment, not an iron curtain.
3. The Roman army was the real political and economic power in the region.
4. Roman rule faded rather than collapsed; the Church kept Roman towns and Latin alive.
5. "Hermann the German" is a 19th-century myth built on a Roman-trained officer's local power struggle.
