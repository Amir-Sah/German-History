## 1. Germany has always been a land of migration

Movements **into**, **out of** and **within** German lands have been constant: Roman soldiers and traders; Frankish settlers; eastward settlement (12th–14th c.); Huguenots (1685); emigrants to Hungary, Russia and America (18th–19th c.; ~5 million to the Americas in the 19th c.); internal east→west migration (Ruhr Poles); Polish and Italian seasonal workers (pre-1914); forced labourers (1939–45, ~13 million); expellees (~12 million, 1945–50); GDR refugees (~3 million to 1961); guest workers (1955–73); late resettlers (~2.5 million after 1990 — estimates); EU migrants; asylum seekers (1990s Yugoslav wars; 2015–16); Ukrainian refugees (2022–). In 2025, 26.3% of the population had an immigration history (Destatis).

## 2. Minorities — status over time

| Group | Key phases |
|---|---|
| **Jews** | Medieval protected-but-vulnerable status; pogroms 1096, 1348–50; expulsions; early modern "protected Jews"; Enlightenment emancipation debate; legal equality 1869/71; antisemitism from 1879; Nazi persecution and genocide; post-war tiny communities; post-Soviet growth after 1990; rising antisemitic incidents today |
| **Sorbs** (Slavic, Lusatia) | Present since early Middle Ages; Germanisation pressure; Nazi repression; GDR official protection; constitutional protection in Saxony and Brandenburg |
| **Danes** (Schleswig) | Annexed 1864; Germanisation; 1920 plebiscite; Bonn–Copenhagen Declarations (1955); Danish minority party SSW exempt from 5% threshold |
| **Poles** | Prussian partitions (1772–95); ~10% of Prussia's population by 1900; Kulturkampf and Germanisation; Ruhr Poles; after 1918 most regions to Poland; Nazi mass murder of Polish civilians; post-1990 large Polish immigration (EU) |
| **Sinti and Roma** | Present since 15th c.; persecuted as "vagrants"; Nazi genocide (Porajmos); post-war discrimination and delayed recognition (1982 federal recognition of genocide); national minority status |
| **Frisians** | Recognised minority |
| **Turkish-Germans** | From 1961; ~3 million people of Turkish origin; citizenship barriers until 2000/2024 reforms; racist attacks (Mölln, Solingen, NSU) |
| **Muslims generally** | ~5–6 million; debates over Islam's place (e.g., "Islam belongs to Germany" — President Wulff, 2010) |

## 3. Citizenship regimes

| Period | Principle |
|---|---|
| Before 1871 | State subject status in each German state |
| 1913 Reich and State Citizenship Law | *Ius sanguinis* (descent) — allowed descendants abroad to remain German; hindered naturalisation of immigrants |
| 1935 Nuremberg Laws | Racial citizenship ("Reich citizens" vs "subjects") |
| FRG 1949 | Art. 116: "Germans" include ethnic German refugees/expellees → right of return for ethnic Germans from Eastern Europe |
| 2000 | Partial *ius soli* for children of long-term residents; "option model" |
| 2005 | Immigration Act — first acknowledgment of immigration as policy field |
| 2024 | Naturalisation after 5 years (3 with special integration), dual citizenship generally allowed |
| 2025 | 3-year fast track abolished; 5 years standard (Bundestag, Oct 2025) |

## 4. Debates

- "Germany is not a country of immigration" (official stance to ~1998) vs reality.
- Integration vs assimilation; multiculturalism; *Leitkultur*.
- Right to asylum: 1949 unconditional (Art. 16) → 1993 compromise (Art. 16a) → EU asylum reform (GEAS 2024–26).
- Memory in a migration society: how people without German ancestors relate to Holocaust memory.

### If you remember only 5 things

1. Migration — in, out and within — is a constant of German history, not a recent anomaly.
2. Citizenship was defined by descent from 1913 until 2000 — a key obstacle to integrating immigrants.
3. Minorities' fates tracked the regime: toleration, emancipation, persecution, genocide, recognition.
4. After 1945, Germany absorbed 12 million expellees and later millions of guest workers and refugees.
5. Today over a quarter of residents have an immigration history; how to define "German" is again a political question.
