## 1. The claim and the question

"Prussian-German militarism" is one of the most common explanations for German history's catastrophes. The claim has three parts: (1) the military had outsized political power; (2) military values (obedience, hierarchy, honour, glorification of war) pervaded society; (3) this led to aggressive wars. Historians accept parts of this — especially (1) for 1860s–1933 — but debate its origins, uniqueness and causal weight.

## 2. Evidence across periods

| Period | Military's political position | Military values in society | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prussia 1713–1806 | Army central to state finances and administration; Junker officer corps; canton system | Büsch: "social militarisation" of rural society | CONTESTED (Hagen: negotiated, not total) |
| Prussian reforms 1807–1819 | Reformers sought "citizens in arms" | Universal conscription (1813/14) | Liberal and conservative strands |
| 1860s | Army reform provokes constitutional conflict; king/Bismarck prevail | Victories 1864–71 glorify the army | Decisive: army outside parliamentary control |
| Kaiserreich | Kaiser's command power; Septennat; military cabinet; Zabern affair (1913) | Reserve officer prestige; veterans' associations; "Captain of Köpenick" satire (1906) | Strong evidence of political autonomy of the military |
| WWI | OHL dominates politics (1916–18) | War mobilisation; "Ideas of 1914" | Military near-dictatorship |
| Weimar | Reichswehr as "state within the state"; secret rearmament; role in 1932–33 intrigue | Paramilitary leagues; cult of the front soldier | Army's disloyalty to republic crucial |
| Nazi | Army subordinated to Hitler (1934 oath; 1938 purge); complicit in war of annihilation | Total militarisation of society | Army as instrument, not master |
| FRG | Bundeswehr (1955) under parliamentary control; "citizen in uniform"; right to conscientious objection | Strong pacifist streak; "culture of restraint" | Deliberate demilitarisation of political culture |
| GDR | NVA under SED; militarised education | Paramilitary youth training | Party-militarism |
| Since 2022 | *Zeitenwende*; rearmament; new service law (2025) | Debate over "war-readiness" (*Kriegstüchtigkeit*) | New phase |

## 3. Interpretations

- **Strong thesis:** Prussian militarism as the root (A.J.P. Taylor; Allied Control Council Law No. 46 dissolving Prussia in 1947 as "a bearer of militarism and reaction").
- **Structural-institutional thesis:** The problem was the military's *constitutional autonomy* 1860s–1933, not a national character (Wehler; Gordon Craig).
- **Revisionist/comparative:** Other European societies were also militarised (France's conscription, Britain's imperial wars); Prussia's image was partly constructed after 1871 and by enemies (Clark, *Iron Kingdom*).
- **Military culture thesis:** Isabel Hull (*Absolute Destruction*, 2005) — institutional military culture in Imperial Germany produced a tendency to extreme solutions (e.g., Herero genocide, WWI occupation practices).

## 4. Assessment

- **HIGH confidence:** The military had unusual political autonomy from the 1860s to 1933, and this mattered in 1914, 1918 (stab-in-the-back myth) and 1932–33.
- **CONTESTED:** That Germans were culturally "more militaristic" than other Europeans before 1914.
- **Rejected:** A straight line from Frederick the Great to Hitler.

### If you remember only 5 things

1. The key problem was the army's independence from parliament (1860s–1933), not a "German character."
2. That independence mattered in 1914, in 1918's stab-in-the-back myth, and in 1933.
3. Under Hitler the army became an instrument — and a willing partner in crimes.
4. Post-1945 democracy put the army firmly under parliament and produced a strong culture of restraint.
5. Since 2022, Germany is renegotiating that restraint.
