## 1. From estates to classes to "levelled" and new divides

| Era | Organising principle | Top | Middle | Bottom |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Medieval | Estates by birth (pray/fight/work) + lordship | Princes, high clergy, nobility | Townspeople (patricians, guild masters) | Peasants (free to serf), labourers, "dishonourable" |
| Early modern | Estates + territorial service | Princes, nobility (esp. Junkers in the east) | Officials, pastors, professors (*Bildungsbürgertum*), merchants | Peasants under Grund-/Gutsherrschaft; rural underclass |
| 1800–1870 | Estates dissolving into classes | Nobility + new industrial/bank bourgeoisie | Educated and propertied bourgeoisie; artisans | Pauperised rural and urban poor; emerging factory workers |
| 1871–1918 | Class society + confessional milieus | Nobility (army, diplomacy, land) + big business | Bildungs- and Wirtschaftsbürgertum; "old" Mittelstand (artisans, shopkeepers) and "new" (white-collar) | Industrial working class (largest group), rural labourers |
| 1918–1933 | Class society under strain | Old elites + industry | Middle classes ruined by inflation | Workers divided (SPD/KPD); mass unemployed |
| 1933–1945 | Racial hierarchy overlaid on class | Party/SS elite + traditional elites | "Aryan" middle classes (gains from Aryanisation) | Workers (jobs, no rights); below them: excluded "races," forced labourers |
| FRG 1949–1990 | Meritocratic-market society with welfare state | Business, professional and political elites (with Nazi-era continuity early) | Broad "middle class" (Schelsky's "levelled middle-class society" — contested) | Guest workers, low-skilled |
| GDR 1949–1990 | Party-state hierarchy | Nomenklatura | "Intelligentsia" (new, from workers' families in early decades) | Workers (official "ruling class") — hidden hierarchies of access (West money, party membership) |
| Since 1990 | Education, region, migration background | Mostly West German-origin elites | Service-sector middle classes | Low-wage service workers; parts of the East; some migrant groups |

## 2. The big transformations

1. **End of personal bondage (c. 1780–1850):** Serfdom abolished in stages (Austria 1781; Bavaria 1808; Prussia 1807–1850); in Prussia the price benefited landlords (land cessions of 1/3 to 1/2), producing a rural proletariat.
2. **Industrial class formation (c. 1850–1914):** Workers moved from craft and land to factory; built their own milieu.
3. **Destruction of old elites:**
   - *Middle classes* by inflation (1923).
   - *Jewish bourgeoisie* by Nazi persecution (1933–45).
   - *Junkers* by war, flight and the Soviet zone land reform (1945).
   - *East German bourgeoisie* by expropriation and flight (1945–61).
4. **Mass prosperity and mobility (1950s–70s):** Rising wages, education expansion, homeownership (lower than in many countries), consumer society.
5. **New inequalities (since 1990s):** East–West, wealth concentration, low-wage sector, migration background.

## 3. Mobility

- Pre-1800: limited — church, service, towns.
- 19th century: education (Gymnasium/university) as the bourgeois route; very limited for workers' children.
- GDR 1950s–60s: very high upward mobility for workers' and farmers' children (policy), then closing.
- FRG 1960s–70s: education expansion raised mobility; since PISA (2001) Germany noted for strong dependence of school success on parental background.

## 4. Interpretations

- **"Feudalisation of the bourgeoisie"** (Sonderweg thesis): bourgeois adopted aristocratic values (reserve officers, duels). **Counter:** Blackbourn/Eley — the bourgeoisie shaped modern Germany culturally and economically ("silent bourgeois revolution").
- **Milieus (Lepsius):** Class was politically mediated through confessional and regional milieus until the 1960s.
- **"Levelled middle-class society" (Schelsky 1953):** Criticised as ideology masking persistent inequality.

### If you remember only 5 things

1. Birth-based estates dissolved into industrial classes only in the 19th century.
2. Peasant emancipation in Prussia strengthened the landlords who would dominate the army and politics until 1918.
3. Twentieth-century catastrophes destroyed whole elites — Jewish, Junker, East German bourgeois.
4. The post-war West created a broad middle class; the GDR created a new party elite.
5. Today's divides are less about class labels than region, education, wealth and migration background.
