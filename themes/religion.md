## 1. The long arc

**Christianisation (4th–10th c.)** → **Imperial Church** (10th–11th c.) → **Investiture Controversy** (11th–12th c.) → **late-medieval lay piety and anticlericalism** → **Reformation and confessional division** (16th c.) → **confessionalised territorial states** (1555–1806) → **secularisation of church states** (1803) → **Kulturkampf and Catholic milieu** (1870s–1960s) → **Protestant nationalism and the churches under Nazism** → **churches as post-1945 moral authorities (West) / marginalised (East)** → **rapid secularisation and religious pluralism (since 1990s)**.

## 2. Religion and power — comparative table

| Era | Relationship of religion to state | Key effect |
|---|---|---|
| Medieval | Church as government (prince-bishops), landowner, legitimiser of kings | Germany's unusual density of ecclesiastical principalities |
| Reformation | Protestant princes become heads of churches; Catholic princes partner with Rome | Religion territorialised; pastor as state official |
| 1648–1806 | Confessional parity in imperial law; territorial churches | Stable coexistence, but confessional identities hardened |
| 1803 | Secularisation: church states abolished | Catholic Church loses political power, gains independence (ultramontanism) |
| Kaiserreich | Protestant-national establishment; Kulturkampf against Catholics | Catholic milieu and Centre Party (~20% of seats) |
| Weimar | Separation (no state church) but cooperation (church tax, religious education) — settlement still valid (Art. 140 GG) | Protestant churches largely anti-republican; Catholic Centre in government |
| Nazi | Concordat (1933); German Christians vs Confessing Church; persecution of dissenting clergy; churches mostly silent on Jews | Institutional survival; moral failure with individual exceptions (Galen, Bonhoeffer, Lichtenberg) |
| FRG | "Cooperative separation"; CDU as Christian party; churches influential until 1960s | Church tax, church welfare; secularisation from 1960s |
| GDR | Atheist state; Jugendweihe; persecution then 1978 accommodation | Membership collapsed (~90%→~25–30%); churches sheltered 1980s opposition |
| Since 1990 | Pluralism: Islam (~5–6 million Muslims), revived Jewish communities, majority now without church membership | Debates over religious symbols, Islam education, church privileges |

## 3. Religion and voting / politics

- **Confession was the strongest predictor of Nazi voting** (Protestant rural areas high; practising Catholics low — Falter).
- **Catholic milieu** gave political Catholicism stable representation from 1870 to the 1960s.
- **Protestant pastors' households** produced a large share of German intellectuals and officials — and, in the Kaiserreich and Weimar, strong national-conservative sentiment.

## 4. Jews in German history (religion + minority)

Present since Roman times (321 CE, Cologne); medieval ShUM communities; recurring massacres and expulsions (1096, 1348–50, 15th c.); early modern "protected Jews" and court Jews; Enlightenment emancipation debates (Mendelssohn, Dohm); legal emancipation 1869/71; flourishing and assimilation alongside rising antisemitism (1879–); ~500,000 in 1933; Holocaust; tiny post-war communities (~30,000 in 1989 in the West); growth to ~100,000 community members through post-Soviet immigration after 1990.

## 5. Debates

Confessionalisation thesis (Schilling/Reinhard vs critics); the churches' role in Nazism (complicity vs resistance); secularisation theory and its German specifics (East German "forced secularisation").

### If you remember only 5 things

1. The Church was a political power in medieval Germany — prince-bishops ruled territories.
2. The Reformation made religion a matter of territory and princes.
3. Confession shaped politics for 400 years — including who voted Nazi.
4. Both churches survived Nazism institutionally but mostly failed morally.
5. Germany is now majority non-church-affiliated and religiously plural.
