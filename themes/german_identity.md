## 1. Key point

"German identity" did not exist in its modern form for most of German history. It was built in layers — language, empire, confession, territory, culture, nation-state, race (catastrophically), civic constitution — and each layer was contested. Many people's primary loyalties were local, regional, dynastic or religious until the late 19th century and beyond.

## 2. Timeline of layers

| Period | Dominant identity layers | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Antiquity | Local kin groups; Roman label *Germani* | No self-designation as "Germanic" |
| 8th–9th c. | Frankish realm; regional peoples (Saxons, Bavarians…); *theodiscus* = vernacular language | "German" first a language word |
| 10th–13th c. | Imperial (Roman) identity of the elite; *Teutonici* as external/papal label; regional duchies | Scales: a "German people" becomes visible via the imperial turn |
| 14th–16th c. | "Holy Roman Empire of the German Nation"; humanist rediscovery of Tacitus; *Gravamina* against Rome | National sentiment among the educated; printing |
| 16th–18th c. | Confession (Lutheran/Catholic/Reformed); territory (Saxon, Bavarian…); imperial patriotism; "German liberty" | Two cultures within one Empire |
| Late 18th c. | *Kulturnation* — language, literature, Bildung (Herder, Goethe, Schiller) | Nation without state |
| 1806–1848 | Anti-French nationalism; liberal-national movement; black-red-gold | Minority movement; federal nationalism |
| 1848 | "Where is the German's fatherland?" — großdeutsch vs kleindeutsch | Borders undefined |
| 1871–1918 | Nation-state identity (kleindeutsch, Prussian-led, Protestant-tinged); Kaiser, army, Sedan Day | Catholics, socialists, Poles marginalised as "enemies of the Reich" |
| 1913 | Citizenship law based on descent (*ius sanguinis*) | Germanness defined by blood lineage in law |
| 1918–1933 | Humiliated nation; "Germans abroad" (*Auslandsdeutsche*) after border changes; republican vs völkisch identities | Black-red-gold vs black-white-red flag conflict |
| 1933–1945 | Racial *Volksgemeinschaft*; Jews expelled from the nation | Identity as race — genocidal |
| 1945–1990 | Two states; West: post-national, Western, "constitutional patriotism," D-Mark pride; East: socialist nation, antifascist | Both defined against the Nazi past |
| 1990– | United Germany; civic citizenship (2000, 2024 reforms); memory culture; European identity; East German identity; plural/immigrant identities | Contested by radical right's ethnic nationalism |

## 3. Five tensions that recur

1. **Cultural vs political nation:** Language/culture vs state/citizenship.
2. **Kleindeutsch vs großdeutsch:** With or without Austria — settled 1866, reopened 1938, settled again 1945.
3. **Ethnic vs civic:** Descent-based citizenship (1913–1999) vs residence/constitution-based (2000–).
4. **Region vs nation:** Bavarians, Saxons, Rhinelanders — federal identities remain strong.
5. **Pride vs guilt:** After 1945, the Nazi past as a negative founding reference.

## 4. What ordinary people identified with (best evidence)

- Pre-1800: locality, lord, church, dynasty.
- 19th c.: increasingly nation, but mediated through region and confession (Catholic Bavarians distrusted Protestant Prussia).
- Kaiserreich: nation reached the masses via school, army, press, festivals — Sedan Day was contested (Catholics and socialists often abstained).
- 20th c.: surveys (post-1945) show West German identification with the Federal Republic growing via prosperity and later democracy; East German identities strengthened *after* 1990 as a response to transformation.

## 5. Interpretations

- **Modernist view** (Gellner, Anderson, Hobsbawm, Langewiesche): nationhood a 19th-century product.
- **Pre-modern roots** (Scales, Hirschi): medieval and humanist German identity real, though different from modern nationalism.
- **Federal nationalism** (Langewiesche): German nation long compatible with multiple states.
- **Post-national identity** (Habermas): West German constitutional patriotism.

### If you remember only 5 things

1. "German" began as a word for a language, not a people or state.
2. For centuries, identity was layered — imperial, regional, confessional — not national.
3. The nation became a mass identity only in the 19th century, and in a Prussian, kleindeutsch form.
4. Nazism turned national identity into racial identity — with genocidal results.
5. Since 1945/1990, German identity has been rebuilt around the constitution, Europe and memory of the Nazi past — and is again contested.
