*Cross-period analysis: Holy Roman Empire → territorial states/Prussia → Confederation → German Empire → Weimar → Nazi dictatorship → FRG / GDR → united Germany.*

## 1. The core pattern: dispersed power, with two catastrophic exceptions

For most of German history, power was **plural and layered** — split between emperor and princes, princes and estates, federation and states, chancellor and coalition, parliament and court. The two exceptions — the **Nazi dictatorship** and (in a different way) the **SED dictatorship** — were attempts to abolish that plurality. Much of post-1945 West German constitutional design deliberately *restored* dispersal as a safeguard.

## 2. Comparative table

| System | Formal ruler | Real power | Checks that worked | Checks that failed | Constitution vs reality |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Medieval Empire** | Elected king/emperor | King + princes + bishops; territorial lords locally | Princely consent; elections; church | — (no central enforcement) | No written constitution; rituals and privileges *were* the constitution |
| **Early modern Empire (1495–1806)** | Emperor with Diet | Territorial princes; emperor via Habsburg lands | Imperial courts (subjects could sue rulers); Diet; circles; confessional parity | Against great powers (Prussia, Austria) | Formally a hierarchy; in practice a "mixed constitution" of loose coupling (Gotthard) |
| **Prussia (1700s)** | Absolute king | King + bureaucracy + army; Junkers locally | Junker privileges (a check on the crown *from below*, not for peasants) | Estates marginalised | "Absolute" at the top, noble self-rule at the bottom |
| **Confederation (1815–66)** | Federal Assembly of sovereign princes | Austria + Prussia; each state for itself | Southern constitutions | Federal repression of liberals | League for order, not nation |
| **Kaiserreich (1871–1918)** | Bundesrat/Kaiser | Kaiser, Chancellor, Prussian ministry, army; growing Reichstag leverage | Reichstag budget & legislation; courts; press | Army outside parliamentary control; no ministerial responsibility | "System of avoided decisions" |
| **Weimar (1918/19–33)** | The people; Reichstag + President | Coalitions (to 1930); President and camarilla (1930–33) | Elections; courts (partly); Prussian government until 1932 | Art. 48; elite disloyalty; anti-system majorities | Parliamentary on paper; presidential in practice after 1930 |
| **Nazi Germany (1933–45)** | Führer | Hitler + competing agencies (SS, party, ministries, army, Four-Year Plan) | None (army conspiracy failed) | All | Weimar constitution never abolished — simply overridden |
| **FRG (1949–)** | The people; Bundestag, Chancellor | Chancellor + coalition; Länder; Constitutional Court; EU; social partners | Courts, federalism, free press, elections | Minor (e.g., limited early accountability for Nazi-era officials) | Close alignment; "provisional" constitution became permanent |
| **GDR (1949–90)** | Volkskammer/State Council | SED Politburo; Soviet Union | None internal; Soviet veto | All | Democratic forms, party dictatorship reality |

## 3. Five recurring mechanisms

1. **Election of rulers by elites (not the people):** Medieval electors chose kings; in 1933 a small circle around Hindenburg "chose" Hitler — a structural echo, not a causal line.
2. **Law and courts as checks:** Imperial courts (to 1806), the Rechtsstaat (19th c.), the Constitutional Court (since 1951). The Nazi "Dual State" shows how law can coexist with lawlessness.
3. **Federalism:** From territorial princes to Länder — the most durable feature of German politics. Only the Nazi regime (1934) and the GDR (1952, Länder abolished for districts) destroyed it.
4. **The army's autonomy:** A decisive variable. The Prussian/Imperial army's independence from parliament (1860s–1918) and the Reichswehr's "state within the state" (1919–33) contributed to disasters; post-1955 the Bundeswehr as a "parliamentary army" of "citizens in uniform" (*Innere Führung*) reversed this.
5. **Emergency powers:** Prussian state of siege (1851), Art. 48 (1919), Reichstag Fire Decree (1933). The Basic Law's 1968 emergency laws were designed with parliamentary controls precisely because of this history.

## 4. Who was excluded, over time

| Era | Excluded from political power |
|---|---|
| Medieval–1806 | Peasants, most townspeople, women, Jews |
| 1815–1848 | All but propertied (and in many states all but princes' appointees) |
| 1849–1918 | Women; in Prussia, workers' votes devalued by three-class suffrage |
| 1918/19–1933 | None formally (all adults 20+); in practice anti-republican elites had outsized power |
| 1933–1945 | Everyone except the Nazi leadership; Jews and others excluded from citizenship itself |
| FRG | Non-citizen residents (millions of guest workers and their children until citizenship reforms) |
| GDR | Everyone outside the SED leadership |

## 5. Interpretations

- **Sonderweg:** Germany's delayed parliamentarisation and powerful pre-industrial elites explain 1933 (Wehler, Bracher). **Counter:** Other European states had similar features without fascism (Blackbourn/Eley); the decisive failures were contingent (1930–33).
- **Federal tradition as a resource:** Historians of the Empire (Whaley, Schmidt) see a long German tradition of negotiated, law-bound, federal power that post-1949 democracy reactivated.

### If you remember only 5 things

1. German power was usually dispersed — among princes, states, courts and parties.
2. The worst disasters came when that dispersal was abolished (1933, and differently 1949 in the East).
3. Written constitutions often diverged from reality: check who controlled the army, the emergency powers and the courts.
4. Federalism is the most durable thread from the Empire to today.
5. The Basic Law is best read as a catalogue of lessons from 1918–1945.
