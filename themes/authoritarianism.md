## 1. Types of authoritarian rule Germany experienced

| Type | Examples | Key traits |
|---|---|---|
| **Princely/"absolutist" rule** | 17th–18th-c. territories; Prussia | Claimed unlimited authority; in practice negotiated with estates and constrained by imperial law |
| **Bureaucratic reform absolutism** | Prussia 1807–1848; Rhine Confederation states | "Revolution from above" by officials; no participation |
| **Restoration police state** | Confederation 1819–1848 | Censorship, surveillance, repression of associations |
| **Constitutional monarchy with military autonomy** | Prussia 1850–1918; Kaiserreich | Parliament co-legislates but doesn't control government or army |
| **Military quasi-dictatorship** | OHL 1916–18 | Wartime dominance of generals |
| **Presidential emergency regime** | Weimar 1930–33 | Decree government; parliament sidelined |
| **Totalitarian/fascist dictatorship** | Nazi Germany 1933–45 | Charismatic leader, mass party, terror, racial ideology, genocidal war |
| **Communist party dictatorship** | GDR 1949–89 | Politburo rule, secret police, planned economy, closed border, social provision |

## 2. Nazi vs SED dictatorship — careful comparison

| Dimension | Nazi Germany | GDR |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | Domestic mass movement + elite deal | Imposed by occupying power; built by German communists |
| Ideology | Racial nationalism; expansion; antisemitism | Marxism-Leninism; internationalism (formally); antifascism |
| Leader | Charismatic Führer | Collective party leadership (with dominant General Secretary) |
| Terror | Camps; mass murder; genocide; war of annihilation | Surveillance; political imprisonment; border killings (~140+ at Berlin Wall); no genocide or war of conquest |
| Economy | Private capitalism under state direction | State ownership and planning |
| Social policy | Racial welfare (*Volksgemeinschaft*) | Universal social security (for citizens), women's employment |
| Popular support | Considerable (plebiscitary) until late war | Mixed; declining in 1980s; mass exit and protest in 1989 |
| End | Military defeat from outside | Peaceful collapse from inside + Soviet withdrawal |

**Comparisons are legitimate, equations are not:** Most historians compare structures (party, police, propaganda) while insisting on the fundamental difference in crimes (genocide, war of annihilation).

## 3. Why authoritarian rule was accepted (recurring mechanisms)

1. **Order after crisis:** 1819 (after revolutionary upheaval), 1933 (after depression and violence), 1949 East (after war and hunger).
2. **Material provision:** Social insurance (1880s), full employment (1930s), job security and subsidies (GDR).
3. **Nationalism and enemies:** "Enemies of the Reich," "Judeo-Bolshevism," "imperialist class enemy."
4. **Legal form:** Authoritarian regimes kept legal façades (Enabling Act; GDR constitution) that made compliance feel lawful.
5. **Fear and surveillance:** Gestapo (small; drew on denunciations, party organisations and the courts); Stasi (large, informant-based).

## 4. Debates

- **The "authoritarian personality"** (Adorno et al., 1950) and "Untertan" mentality (Heinrich Mann's novel, 1918) — cultural explanations, now treated cautiously.
- **Totalitarianism theory** (Arendt; Friedrich/Brzezinski) — revived after 1990 for GDR research; criticised for static models.
- **Consent vs coercion** (Gellately vs Evans for Nazism; Fulbrook's participatory dictatorship for the GDR).

### If you remember only 5 things

1. Germany experienced many kinds of authoritarianism — princely, bureaucratic, military, presidential, fascist, communist.
2. Authoritarian regimes typically followed crises and promised order and material security.
3. Legal façades made dictatorship look lawful.
4. Nazi and SED dictatorships can be compared structurally, but not equated in their crimes.
5. Surveillance worked through citizens: denunciations under the Nazis, informants under the SED.
