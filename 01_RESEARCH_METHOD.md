# Research Method

## 1. The question this project asks

Not *"what happened when?"* but *"how did German society work, and why did it change?"* For each system the research asked the same questions (see the [Regime Matrix](05_REGIME_MATRIX.md)):

- **Power** — who ruled formally, who ruled in practice, how power was acquired, kept, financed, enforced and challenged.
- **Society** — classes, estates, religion, gender, cities vs countryside, minorities, mobility.
- **Everyday life** — work, family, schooling, faith, housing, military service, contact with the state.
- **Economy** — who owned land and capital, how labour was organised, who paid taxes, how crises hit.
- **Ideas** — kept separate at four levels: elites, intellectuals, movements, ordinary people.
- **Change** — BEFORE → PRESSURES → TRANSITION → AFTER, and what changed (or didn't) for ordinary people.

## 2. Workflow actually followed

| Phase | What was done |
|---|---|
| 1. Preliminary chronology | Built from the German Historical Institute's GHDI volume structure, DHM/LeMO epoch structure and bpb *Informationen zur politischen Bildung* issues. |
| 2. Systems | Identified 13 distinct political systems (see Regime Matrix), plus sub-systems analysed in individual chapters (e.g., the revolutionary order of 1848–49, the presidential cabinets of 1930–33, the transitional GDR of 1989–90). |
| 3. Period research | Institutional overviews (bpb, LeMO, Historisches Lexikon Bayerns, GHDI introductions, Bundestag, Bundesarchiv) + primary documents (GHDI, Bundestag archive). |
| 4. Cross-checking | Key claims checked against at least one source of a different *type* (e.g., German civic-education text vs. peer-reviewed article vs. primary document). |
| 5. Disconfirmation | For each big narrative, searched specifically for the revisionist or critical position (e.g., "Westphalian myth", "spirit of 1914 myth", "myth of the Wars of Liberation", Hagen vs. Junker-tyranny narrative, Tooze vs. Aly, Evans vs. Gellately, Richter vs. Conze). |
| 6. Historiography | Mapped the major debates (list in `04_QUESTIONS_WORTH_EXPLORING.md` and each period file §12). |
| 7. Themes | Cross-period files in `themes/`. |
| 8. Audit | `sources/SOURCE_AUDIT.md` — claim-by-claim table. |
| 9. Synthesis | `00_FINAL_EXPLANATION.md`. |
| 10. Consistency check | Cross-file check of figures, dates and terminology (log at the end of the audit). |

## 3. Source hierarchy used

| Level | Type | Examples in this project | Used for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Primary sources; authoritative archival/institutional records | Paulskirche constitution, Twelve Articles, Kant 1784, October Edict (via GHDI); Bundestag records on the Enabling Act; Bundesarchiv MfS-Lexikon; USHMM victim documentation; Destatis; court rulings (VG Köln 2026) | Facts, texts, official numbers |
| **B** | Peer-reviewed research, university publications, academic monographs | Osiander (*International Organization* 2001); Kershaw (*Contemporary European History* 1993); Mombauer (*CEH* 2015); Röhl (*International Affairs* 2015); Kocka (*JCH* 1988); Ritschl on Borchardt; NBER WP 24106; Hagen, Verhey (CUP excerpts); Whaley & Scales; Gierok (EHS WP) | Interpretation, debates, quantitative estimates |
| **C** | Established museums, civic-education bodies, public broadcasters, professional historians writing for the public | bpb (Gotthard, Osterhammel, Ziemann, Piper, Hoffmann, Böick, Rother), DHM/LeMO, Historisches Lexikon Bayerns (Burgdorf), Stiftung Berliner Mauer, Deutsche Limeskommission, Bundesregierung | Well-established narrative; German institutional perspective |
| **D** | High-quality educational websites, documentaries | Terra X, Open Yale Courses, Deutschlandmuseum | Orientation, leads |
| **E** | General websites, blogs, popular videos | Various | Leads only |
| **F** | Forums, social media, unsourced content | — | Not used |

**Wikipedia** was used only to locate primary and secondary sources (Level E as a finding aid), never as the basis for a key claim.

**German civic-education sources (bpb, Landeszentralen) have a known orientation**: they are written to support democratic education in the Federal Republic. They are generally scholarly and signed by named historians, but they tend to frame history as a story of democratic learning. This was balanced with international academic work where possible.

## 4. Confidence scale

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **HIGH** | Multiple independent source types agree; no serious scholarly dispute about the fact. |
| **MEDIUM** | Well supported, but depends on limited data, or sources partly depend on each other, or scope is debated. |
| **UNCERTAIN** | Evidence insufficient (common for pre-modern numbers and for "what people believed"). This is the lowest level; the project does not use a separate LOW label. |
| **CONTESTED** | Reputable historians disagree about interpretation, causation or magnitude. |
| **REJECTED** | A claim or older interpretation that current scholarship has largely refuted (kept in the tables because readers often meet it). |

**How labels are written (closed vocabulary):** every Confidence cell starts with one label from the table, or with a range between two neighbouring levels of the ordinal scale HIGH → MEDIUM → UNCERTAIN (e.g., `MEDIUM–HIGH`, `MEDIUM–UNCERTAIN`). Anything after the label in parentheses is a qualifier — a source, a range, or a note on what exactly is contested (e.g., `HIGH (Destatis)`, `CONTESTED (largely rejected)`).

## 5. Terminology rules (to avoid anachronism)

| Term | Use it for | Don't use it for |
|---|---|---|
| **Germani / "Germanic peoples"** | Roman label for peoples east of the Rhine and north of the Danube; a *language family* (linguistics) | A self-conscious ethnic group or "the ancestors of the Germans" |
| **East Francia / Regnum Teutonicorum** | The eastern Frankish kingdom after 843; later the German kingdom inside the Empire | "Germany" as a nation-state |
| **Holy Roman Empire (the "Reich", *Altes Reich*)** | The multi-layered polity 962–1806 (imperial title from Otto I's coronation) incl. Bohemia, Austria, Low Countries, N. Italy at times; the chapter `medieval/02` starts in 919 because the German kingdom came first | A German nation-state |
| **German-speaking lands** | Areas where German dialects dominated | A political unit |
| **German states** | The dozens of territories (Prussia, Bavaria, Saxony…) | Provinces of a single state |
| **German nation** | An idea/identity; elite from 16th c. humanists, mass political from 19th c. | Something that always existed |
| **German Empire (*Kaiserreich*)** | The federal monarchy 1871–1918 | The Holy Roman Empire |
| **Germany / Federal Republic** | The post-1949 West German state and post-1990 united state | Earlier polities |
| **GDR / DDR** | German Democratic Republic 1949–1990 | "East Germany" before 1949 (use Soviet Zone, SBZ) |

## 6. Common errors actively watched for

Presentism · hindsight/teleology ("road to Hitler") · nationalist master narratives (Arminius, Barbarossa, Wars of Liberation) · Nazi, Soviet/SED and Cold-War propaganda taken at face value · romanticised Middle Ages · confusing legal form with actual power · confusing propaganda with opinion · single-person explanations · static borders · treating Germans as homogeneous.

## 7. Known limitations of this research

- Several important monographs (Wehler, Nipperdey, Wilson, Whaley, Evans trilogy, Fulbrook, Wolfrum) were used via their published summaries, excerpts and reviews rather than read cover to cover.
- Some institutional pages refused automated access (1914-1918-online, Heidelberg journal *Germania*, JSTOR/MUSE); where this happened the claim was sourced elsewhere and the gap is noted.
- Quantitative estimates for pre-1800 periods (population, war deaths) are inherently uncertain and are presented as ranges.
- "What ordinary people believed" is the hardest thing to know for any period before opinion research; for the Nazi period it relies on regime surveillance reports (SD *Meldungen aus dem Reich*) and exile SPD reports (*Sopade*), both of which have known biases.

## 8. Template and labelling rules (all 35 period files)

- **Sections:** every period file has the 14 numbered sections with these exact names — 1 Period in One Paragraph · 2 The Political Structure · 3 Society · 4 Everyday Life · 5 Economy · 6 Ideas and Ideologies · 7 What Created Pressure for Change? · 8 Transition · 9 What Changed for Ordinary People? · 10 What Stayed the Same? · 11 Important Misconceptions · 12 Historiographical Debate · 13 Confidence Assessment · 14 Sources. A heading may add a qualifier after an em dash (e.g., `## 2. The Political Structure — local level`). The file ends with "If you remember only 5 things".
- **Regime matrix:** section 2 of every file contains exactly one `### Regime Matrix — …` heading. Either it is followed by the full 12-question table, or — when the chapter describes a theme inside a system analysed elsewhere — by a line `**Matrix:** see <file>` (a cross-reference, heading `### Regime Matrix — cross-reference`).
- **Constitution vs reality:** section 2 of every file contains exactly one paragraph starting **What the constitution said vs how power actually worked:** (for periods without a written constitution, "constitution" means the formal rules and claims of the time).
- **Transition chains (section 8):** exactly four steps, in this order, separated by ↓: **BEFORE**, **PRESSURES**, **TRANSITION**, **AFTER**. A step may carry a qualifier in parentheses, e.g., **TRANSITION (crisis, 1930–33):**, and may contain a bullet list of phases.
- **Confidence:** see §4 for the closed vocabulary.

## 9. Date conventions (why some dates look different across files)

Some dates differ between files because they mark *different things*. The conventions are:

| Topic | Convention used | Why other dates appear |
|---|---|---|
| Frankish world | **c. 500–919** for the chapter `medieval/01` (it ends with the Ottonian succession) | **843** = division of the Frankish Empire at Verdun (the mental map shows the united empire to 843); **911** = end of the Carolingian line in East Francia (Louis the Child), used in the timeline and for "Carolingian kingship c. 751–911" |
| German kingdom / Holy Roman Empire | Kingdom from **919** (Henry I); empire from **962** (Otto I's imperial coronation) to 1806 | The name "Holy Roman Empire" itself appears only in the 13th century |
| Weimar Republic | **1918/19–1933** | Republic proclaimed 9 Nov 1918; National Assembly Feb 1919; constitution 11 Aug 1919 |
| Expellees | **about 12 million** Germans fled or were expelled (1945–50) | Some works give 12–14 million; totals vary with definitions (e.g., whether people who died during flight, or later resettlers, are counted) |
| Treuhand | **about 8,500** enterprises at the start | The number of firms grew as combines were split up |
| Chapter overlaps | Chapters overlap at their edges on purpose (e.g., `nineteenth_century/01` 1792–1815 and Part 2 ending 1806) | Each chapter includes the transition into the next |

## 10. Dictionary and plain-language lines

The knowledge base includes a reference dictionary (`dictionary/`) of 2,300 entries (437 people, 280 places, 1,583 terms) extracted from the chapter text and verified against well-established reference sources. Entries marked "Basis: KB+K" combine knowledge base content with reliable general knowledge, such as standard biographical dates. All entries were fact-checked but are not audited claim by claim like the source audit; some recent items (e.g., September 2026 election figures) come from KB text and are not independently verified.

Dense historiographical debates (section 12 of each period file) were made more accessible by adding a short "In plain words" explanation paragraph after each debate point. These 122 lines paraphrase the KB text and are meant for readers unfamiliar with the debates. They do not add new sources or interpretations.
